Private Markets Secondaries GP Economics Carry

Super carry is spiking in continuation funds. GPs are taxing their own LPs twice.

  • 35% of continuation funds closing in H1 2026 carried premium economics — 'super carry' above the standard 20% profit share — up from 15% in full-year 2025, per Evercore data.
  • The highest carry structure observed in a 2025 Morgan Lewis study hit 30%, with three-quarters of continuation funds using tiered structures that escalate the manager's cut as returns climb.
  • Bain Capital, the Boston-based PE firm, already runs a dual share-class model across all its funds where investors can opt into a 30% carry in exchange for a lower management fee — continuation funds are extending this logic industry-wide.

The take

A record share of continuation funds that closed in the first half of 2026 came with "super carry" baked in — terms that let the manager keep more than 20% of the profit, versus the standard 20%. Evercore, the biggest broker in this market, says 35% of deals by dollar volume carried premium economics, up from 15% for full-year 2025. Some structures now tier up to 30% carry at a 3x return. A continuation fund, for the uninitiated, is the structure a GP uses to move a portfolio company out of an aging fund and into a new vehicle it also manages — selling the asset to itself, with fresh capital, and resetting the clock.

The industry is selling this as an alignment story. That's the tell, and it's backwards. When a manager takes a bigger cut, the polite explanation is always that it "focuses the sponsor's mind." But you don't need to be paid 30% to focus on your single best asset — the one you deliberately chose not to sell in an open auction because you think it's a compounder. The super-carry surge isn't alignment. It's GPs discovering that in a market where LPs are desperate for DPI — actual cash distributions, not paper marks — they hold pricing power over their own liquidity, and they're using it to re-rate their own fees on the assets they know best.

Here's the second-order effect nobody is pricing. The continuation-fund boom exists because the exit market is broken: no IPOs, jammed sponsor-to-sponsor deals, 2021 vintages that can't be sold at their marks. So GPs invented a way to manufacture liquidity for old LPs while keeping the crown jewel. Fine. But the same broken exit market is what lets them jack carry to 30% — the new LP buying into the CV has nowhere better to put money either. Super carry is a symptom of illiquidity being monetized twice: once on the way out, once on the way back in, both times by the manager.

Watch the adverse selection. Evercore itself notes super carry is used to "enhance the attractiveness of a bid" in a competitive auction. Read that again — the buyer sweetens its offer to the selling LPs by promising the manager a bigger cut. The people paying for that generosity are the incoming CV investors, not the ones being courted. Churchill's Nick Lawler is right that a fair price and a real GP commitment matter more than the carry headline. But most incoming CV LPs can't independently price a single hand-picked asset, and they're now paying premium fees for the privilege of not knowing.

If you're allocating here, invert it. A GP taking 30% on its best asset is telling you exactly how good it thinks that asset is. Underwrite the asset, not the alignment slogan — and demand the marks that justify the carry.

The other side

The honest case for super carry is that it genuinely shifts fee weight from guaranteed management fees — which the manager collects regardless of outcome — toward performance-contingent carry that only pays if investors win first. If a GP takes 30% at a 3x return, they need to actually deliver that 3x, and on a single concentrated asset with no portfolio diversification to hide behind, that's a real bar. For incoming continuation fund investors, a manager with meaningful personal capital co-invested alongside a high-watermark carry structure may actually be safer than a standard-fee GP phoning it in on an asset they'd quietly love to exit.

To understand why this is accelerating now, you need to understand the structural position GPs are in. The private equity exit market has been functionally closed for most of the last three years. IPO windows have opened and shut without meaningful sponsor exits, and large leveraged buyouts selling to other sponsors — the so-called sponsor-to-sponsor deals that historically cleared a lot of inventory — are grinding because financing costs and valuation gaps haven't fully reconciled. Funds raised at peak 2021 valuations are sitting on assets they cannot sell at their marks without taking a visible loss. Continuation funds — vehicles where the GP moves an asset from an old fund into a new one it also manages, bringing in outside capital to cash out the existing limited partners — became the escape valve.

The mechanic is worth being precise about. When a GP runs a continuation fund process, it is both the seller and the buyer. It selects which asset to transfer, it sets the price in negotiation with an advisory board of existing LPs, and it then manages the new vehicle. Evercore and a handful of other investment banks run competitive processes to bring in secondary buyers — specialized funds that buy LP stakes and continuation fund interests — but the GP has enormous informational advantage over every bidder. It knows this asset better than anyone in the room.

That information asymmetry is exactly why super carry has found a home here. The GP knows whether the asset is a genuine compounder or a flattering mark on a mediocre business. The incoming CV investor, by contrast, is underwriting a single company with limited diligence time and no operating history inside the new structure. Super carry, framed as alignment, is really the GP charging a premium for information it already owns. The incoming investor is paying for conviction signals they cannot independently verify.

The dynamic compounds when you trace who is absorbing the cost. When a secondary buyer sweetens its bid by offering the selling GP better carry terms, the economics of that generosity do not come from the secondary buyer's own pocket — they come from the returns that would otherwise flow to the continuation fund's LPs. The LPs being courted — the ones getting liquidity — are made whole. The LPs funding the new vehicle absorb the dilution. It is a clean transfer, done politely, dressed up in the language of partnership.

Historically, the private equity industry has moved carry economics in one direction during stress periods: toward managers. After the financial crisis, as fundraising dried up and GPs needed to retain talent and signal durability, carry terms quietly softened in favor of sponsors. The post-2021 liquidity crunch is producing the same dynamic through a different mechanism — not weaker fund terms, but manufactured liquidity events that let GPs renegotiate economics on their single best assets at the moment those assets are most valuable and least fungible.

The number to watch going into the second half of 2026 is whether the 35% figure holds or climbs further. If exit markets remain closed, every GP holding a quality asset has the same incentive calculation — and the secondary market, which is itself flush with capital chasing limited deal flow, has limited leverage to push back.

Tech & AI Chips AI Infrastructure Private Credit

Google didn't win the chip war. It financed its way around it.

  • Google and partners created an SPV called 'Compute' that bought 1 million TPUs for $35 billion, funded by Apollo and Blackstone private credit — not by open-market chip buyers
  • Broadcom, which co-designed the TPU, wrote a residual value guarantee on the hardware, meaning it covers losses if Anthropic defaults and the chips can't be sold to recover the debt
  • Bitcoin miners TeraWulf, Cipher Digital, and Hut 8 — cash-poor but power-rich — are building Anthropic's data centers under a Google payment backstop, with Google receiving equity options in the miners as compensation

The take

Google has quietly assembled the largest infrastructure financing structure ever built — roughly $200 billion — to jam its own TPU chips into the market by wrapping them around a single customer, Anthropic. The mechanism is worth reading twice. Google, Broadcom, Apollo, and Blackstone set up an SPV called "Compute" that bought 1 million TPUs for $35 billion, funded by Apollo and Blackstone private credit, with Broadcom writing a residual value guarantee to cover losses if Anthropic can't pay its lease. Google builds the data centers and leases them back to a startup with no credit rating. That is not a chip sale. That is vendor financing dressed up as an ecosystem.

The consensus reads this as Google finally landing a punch on Nvidia. Wrong frame. The real story is that Google has decided the only way to move TPUs at scale is to manufacture the demand itself, because the open market wouldn't clear at these volumes without a guarantee wrapped around it. When 80% of a $200 billion contract is tied to your own chips going to your own funded customer, you haven't found product-market fit — you've built a captive buyer and financed both sides of the trade. Broadcom's residual value guarantee is the tell. Nobody underwrites a residual guarantee on a depreciating asset unless the residual is the whole point, and unless someone is nervous the primary obligor won't be there to pay.

Here's what nobody is pricing. The counterparty concentration is not a bug they'll diversify away — it is structural, because Anthropic is one of maybe three entities on earth that can absorb a million TPUs. So this "network" is really a bet that Anthropic's revenue ramp outruns its lease obligations, backstopped by Broadcom's balance sheet and Google's liquidity. The FT gets close but soft-pedals it: if Anthropic stumbles, the entire financing lattice — private credit, chip vendor, cloud provider, and a cohort of bitcoin miners — takes the loss together. That's not diversification. That's every participant long the same single name.

The miner angle is where it turns genuinely circular. TeraWulf, Cipher, Hut 8 — power-rich, cash-poor Bitcoin outfits — are building Anthropic's data centers in exchange for a Google payment backstop, and Google takes equity options in them as the sweetener. So Google guarantees the miners, the miners build for Anthropic, Anthropic pays Google, and Google finances Anthropic. Money and risk travel in a loop, and each hop books revenue for someone. This is the same daisy-chain that made Nvidia-OpenAI-Oracle look like escape velocity until you asked where the actual outside cash enters.

For allocators the read is simple: when a hyperscaler with a pristine balance sheet needs Apollo and Blackstone to warehouse its own inventory, the marginal TPU is not being pulled by demand — it's being pushed by financing. Watch the residual guarantees, not the headline chip counts. That's where the loss sits.

The other side

The bear case on the bear case is that vendor financing is a perfectly legitimate tool for scaling a new technology platform, and Google is playing from a position of genuine strength. Google Cloud revenue is growing fast, TPUs have real performance advantages on transformer workloads, and Anthropic — backed by Google and Amazon with billions in committed capital — is not a fly-by-night credit risk. If Anthropic's revenue ramp materializes as projected, every participant in this structure books returns, Google embeds its chips as the default AI compute substrate before Nvidia can lock up the same customers, and the 'circular' critique looks like the same skepticism that got aimed at AWS infrastructure buildout in 2012. Concentration risk is real, but so is first-mover lock-in.

To understand why this structure is unusual, it helps to know what normal chip sales look like. In a standard transaction, a semiconductor company sells chips to a hyperscaler or enterprise buyer who absorbs the credit risk, operates the hardware, and pays upfront or on commercial terms. The chip vendor gets paid and moves on. What Google has built is the opposite: it is simultaneously the chip supplier, the data center landlord, the customer's primary investor, and the guarantor of last resort for a ring of smaller contractors. That is not a supply chain. That is a balance sheet wrapped around a single revenue bet.

The historical parallel that comes to mind is not Nvidia and OpenAI, which the press keeps reaching for. It is the telecoms buildout of the late 1990s, when equipment vendors like Lucent and Nortel started extending vendor financing to their own customers to move hardware at volumes the open market wouldn't absorb. Lucent at its peak had extended roughly $7 billion in loans and guarantees to buyers of its own switches and routers. When the carriers couldn't service those obligations, Lucent took the write-downs directly — not just on bad loans, but on the inflated revenue it had booked when the financed sales were recorded. The chips shipped, the revenue was recognized, and then the whole thing unwound. Google is not Lucent — its liquidity position is incomparably stronger — but the mechanism rhymes.

The second-order implication that deserves more attention is what this structure does to TPU pricing signals. When the primary buyer of your chip is a company you fund, and the terms are set inside a private SPV rather than through arm's-length negotiation, the price of the chip stops being a market price. It becomes a transfer price inside a financing structure. That matters for anyone trying to benchmark GPU versus TPU economics, because the data point they are comparing against is not a real market clearing price — it is a number that was set to make the financing math work.

The miner participation adds a layer of complexity that is underappreciated. TeraWulf, to use the most specific example in the reporting, is a small publicly traded Bitcoin miner with stranded power assets in New York state. It has essentially been recruited to act as a construction and power-procurement intermediary for Google's AI infrastructure ambitions, taking on project execution risk in exchange for a payment guarantee from a company with a AAA-equivalent credit profile. For TeraWulf, that guarantee is transformative — it is the difference between a viable business model and a distressed balance sheet. For Google, it is a cheap way to access permitted power capacity without owning the regulatory and construction headache. The equity option Google receives in return means that if this works, Google also participates in the upside of the miner's re-rating as an AI infrastructure company. That is three layers of optionality stacked on one relationship.

The question allocators should be asking is not whether Anthropic is a good company. It probably is. The question is whether this financing structure creates the illusion of TPU demand that then gets cited as evidence of TPU product-market fit, which then justifies further capital allocation into the same loop. When the FT calls this the largest infrastructure financing ever assembled, that headline will be used in every Google Cloud sales deck for the next two years. The chip counts and contract values will be treated as organic market validation. They are not. They are the output of a financing decision, not a demand signal.

Tech & AI Open Source China Enterprise AI Frontier Models

China just open-sourced the frontier. America's best response is a safety memo.

  • Alibaba's Qwen 3.8-Max, a 2.4-trillion-parameter model matching Anthropic's Claude Sonnet 5 on independent benchmarks, is now available for free download — the first time Alibaba has released its top-tier weights publicly
  • DeepSeek V4-Flash, at 284 billion parameters, performs within one benchmark point of OpenAI's budget model GPT-5.6 Luna while costing 40 percent less per task on Artificial Analysis's cost-per-task index
  • Anthropic is raising Claude Sonnet 5 output pricing 50 percent on September 1, even as DeepSeek charges $0.28 per million output tokens — a gap that is now structural, not cyclical

The take

On Monday Alibaba's Qwen team pushed its top-tier 2.4-trillion-parameter Max model out from behind the API and onto Hugging Face, days after DeepSeek shipped a 284-billion-parameter V4-Flash that lands within a point of OpenAI's budget model at 40 percent lower cost per task. The specific thing worth noticing isn't another benchmark leapfrog — it's that a Chinese lab just open-weighted a genuine frontier-class model, and the best America can field in open weights, a model called Inkling, is under a billion parameters and can't keep up with DeepSeek's cheap tier. That's not a gap. That's a different sport.

The consensus read is that US labs still own the frontier and China owns the commodity floor. That framing is a comfort blanket. Look at what Dario Amodei actually said: he's fine with open models, just not Chinese ones, not distilled ones, and not ones that fail "rigorous safety metrics" — which is every open model that competes with Anthropic. When the strongest technical argument a $180B lab can make against a rival is "trust us, ours is safer," you are watching a company that has lost the price argument and the capability argument and is retreating to the regulatory one. Safety is the last refuge of an incumbent that can't cut its own price without vaporizing its margin. Sonnet 5's output pricing is going up 50 percent on September 1. DeepSeek's is $0.28 per million.

Here's the second-order effect nobody is pricing. The relevant number isn't the token price, it's the memory footprint. V4-Flash fits in 142GB at FP4 — one server. Qwen's 27-billion-parameter variant runs on a workstation. Once a frontier-adjacent model runs inside your own firewall on hardware you already depreciate, the entire enterprise-SaaS logic of the American labs collapses. You are no longer renting intelligence by the token from a US counterparty with an opaque data policy; you own the weights outright. For any regulated bank, sovereign fund, or defense-adjacent buyer — precisely the accounts the labs need to justify their valuations — sovereignty is the one axis OpenAI and Anthropic structurally cannot match. And China is handing it out for free.

The capital allocation read: the American frontier premium is being repriced from a durable moat to an 18-month lead that customers can rent around. Nvidia is fine either way — Qwen Max still needs 48-64 B200s to serve. The exposed party is anyone underwriting OpenAI or Anthropic on the assumption that inference revenue compounds at SaaS-like retention. It won't, because the switching cost is collapsing toward zero and the substitute is open-weight. Clément Delangue of Hugging Face, who sees every download, says China dominates open models and may take the frontier by next year. He has no reason to lie and every dataset to know. Believe the man with the logs, not the CEO with the safety memo.

The other side

The security objection is not pure theater. Regulated buyers — banks, insurers, defense contractors — face real procurement constraints around Chinese-origin software, and those constraints are encoded in law, not just preference. An open-weight model you run on your own server still carries supply-chain risk if the training pipeline and the team behind it are subject to Chinese government requests, and no enterprise legal team is going to sign off on that caveat lightly. Beyond procurement risk, frontier capability gaps still matter for long-horizon agentic work where error propagation is expensive — and independent benchmarks like Artificial Analysis put Qwen 3.8-Max at Claude Sonnet 5 levels, not Claude Opus 5 levels. The top of the capability stack may remain American longer than the open-weights story implies.

Say more →

The open-weights move by Alibaba deserves more attention than it is getting, because it changes the competitive logic at the structural level, not just the pricing one. Until now, the Chinese labs had released capable but mid-tier open models while keeping their best work behind APIs, roughly mirroring what OpenAI and Anthropic do. Qwen 3.8-Max breaks that pattern. Alibaba is now giving away, for free download, a model that sits on the same competitive tier as the product Anthropic is about to charge more for. That is not a pricing skirmish. That is a go-to-market strategy designed to hollow out the subscription base.

The mechanism matters here. A mixture-of-experts architecture means that despite Qwen 3.8-Max's 2.4 trillion total parameters, only 95 billion are active on any given request. That is how you get frontier-adjacent output at manageable compute cost. DeepSeek's V4-Flash uses a similar efficiency philosophy from the opposite direction — start small, optimize hard, end up with a 284-billion-parameter model that outperforms DeepSeek's own previous 1.6-trillion-parameter Pro version by 14 percent on Artificial Analysis's intelligence leaderboard. Both labs are converging on the same insight: raw parameter count is a marketing number, and efficiency per token is the real competition.

The historical parallel worth reaching for is what happened to enterprise database software in the 2000s. Oracle spent a decade insisting that open-source databases like MySQL and PostgreSQL were toys for hobbyists, not enterprise-grade, not safe for regulated data. That argument worked until it didn't. The moment the open alternative crossed a capability threshold that covered 80 percent of real workloads, procurement teams had all the justification they needed to stop writing Oracle checks. The remaining 20 percent of workloads that needed Oracle's top-tier features kept Oracle alive, but the growth story was over. The American frontier labs are approaching that inflection point, and unlike Oracle, they do not have decades of installed base and switching costs protecting them. The switching cost for a language model API is a changed endpoint URL.

The companies most exposed to this dynamic are the ones whose valuations are premised on inference revenue growing at SaaS-like retention rates. SaaS retention works when switching is painful and the product is embedded in workflows. Language model APIs are not embedded in the same way — the abstraction layer is thin, and increasingly, open-source orchestration tools like LangChain or LlamaIndex are designed explicitly to make swapping models trivial. When the substitute product is free, runs on hardware you already own, and produces output within a benchmark point of the paid alternative, the retention assumption breaks.

Nvidia remains insulated from this because both sides of the race need its chips. Qwen 3.8-Max requires 48 to 64 B200-class GPUs for customer-facing deployment — Nvidia's latest high-end accelerator — and DeepSeek's efficiency gains at the model level do not reduce the aggregate chip demand when usage scales. If anything, cheaper inference expands the total addressable workload, which expands chip demand. The pain is not at the hardware layer. The pain is at the application layer, specifically at any company that raised capital on the premise that American lab inference pricing would hold.

ICONIQ Portfolio Companies Anthropic AI Infrastructure Venture Debt

Anthropic isn't buying compute from Volta. It's printing a supplier from scratch.

  • Anthropic signed a $10bn, six-year compute deal with Volta, a cloud startup founded this year with a $2.4bn valuation and no operating history.
  • Volta will run Anthropic's capacity partly through Bitdeer, a bitcoin mining company, at a site in Norway — the deal is Volta's only known contract.
  • Anthropic's total compute commitments now span Amazon ($25bn), Google and Broadcom, AMD ($5bn), CoreWeave, Akamai, SpaceX, and a rumored $10bn Meta deal, putting its aggregate exposure well into the tens of billions.

The take

Anthropic just committed $10bn over six years to Volta, a cloud startup that launched this year, is valued at $2.4bn, and has no operating history. The capacity will run partly through Bitdeer, a bitcoin miner, out of a site in Norway. On its face this is one more line on Anthropic's supplier roll-call — Amazon at $25bn, Google and Broadcom, AMD at $5bn, CoreWeave, Akamai, even SpaceX, plus a rumored $10bn Meta deal. But the counterparty is the tell, and it says something the compute-diversification story is designed to obscure.

Here's what's actually going on. When a frontier lab hands a days-old startup a $10bn anchor contract, the lab is not buying compute — it is manufacturing a supplier. Volta cannot build against this deal without raising billions, and it can only raise because Anthropic's signature exists. Andreessen Horowitz, Altimeter, Nvidia, and Michael Dell are backing it precisely because the anchor de-risks the equity. So the same capital ecosystem that funds Anthropic is now funding Anthropic's landlord, who will buy Nvidia chips with money raised on the strength of Anthropic's promise to pay. That is not diversification. That is the circular financing that has quietly become the load-bearing structure of the entire AI buildout.

The consensus reads this as Anthropic gaining leverage — shift workloads between Amazon, Google, AMD, and now Volta, stay beholden to no one on price. That framing is backwards. A first-time operator with a single anchor customer has no leverage over that customer; Anthropic owns Volta's cash flows and therefore Volta. What Anthropic actually gains is off-balance-sheet capacity it doesn't have to build, financed by third parties who are underwriting Anthropic's demand curve as if it were a bond. The risk didn't disappear. It got distributed to a.16z's LPs and Bitdeer's shareholders.

The number that should focus the mind is Anthropic's $30bn run rate against compute commitments now running into the high tens of billions. That gap only closes if Claude's enterprise revenue keeps compounding at its current pace for years. If it doesn't — if the GLM-and-escalate pattern hollows out frontier pricing, if enterprise seat growth flattens — the losses don't stay inside Anthropic. They cascade through Volta, Bitdeer, the debt raised against the Norway site, and every fund that marked those positions to the anchor contract. Nvidia financing the customers who buy its chips is the 1999 vendor-financing playbook wearing a GPU.

For an allocator, the read-across is simple: the interesting exposure in this cycle is not the labs, it's the leverage stacked behind them. Anyone holding Volta-type paper — new-entrant compute financed on a single AI anchor — is short frontier-lab revenue durability without being paid for it. Price that risk before the marks do it for you.

The other side

Compute supply really is the binding constraint right now, and a lab that relies on two or three hyperscalers — especially ones who are also direct competitors — faces genuine prioritization and pricing risk. Locking in six years of capacity from a purpose-built, single-anchor provider gives Anthropic contractual predictability that Amazon or Google can quietly erode. If Volta executes and the Norway site delivers, Anthropic gets dedicated infrastructure at cost structure it helped set, which is a better outcome than being perpetually at the back of a hyperscaler's queue.

Say more →

The Volta deal is worth examining not as an isolated procurement decision but as a window into how the entire AI infrastructure cycle is being financed — and who is actually holding the risk at the end of the chain.

Start with the mechanics. Volta launched this year, has no meaningful operating history, and is valued at $2.4bn. It is backed by Andreessen Horowitz, Altimeter, Nvidia, and Michael Dell. None of those investors wrote checks because they believe in Volta's independent competitive position in cloud infrastructure. They wrote them because a $10bn anchor contract from Anthropic makes the equity underwritable. The anchor is the asset. Everything else — the Norway site, the Bitdeer partnership, the management team — is execution detail layered on top of a promise to pay.

This is a version of vendor financing, a structure that became notorious during the telecom buildout of the late 1990s. Then, equipment makers like Lucent and Nortel lent money to carriers who used it to buy equipment from those same manufacturers. Revenue booked, loans extended, balance sheets inflated — until demand disappointed and the whole thing unwound simultaneously. The AI iteration is more diffuse but structurally similar. Nvidia's chips get bought with capital raised against Anthropic's contract. Nvidia itself is an investor in Volta. The financial loop is not hidden; it is just spread across enough entities that no single participant has to look at the full circle.

The diversification framing Anthropic uses publicly — many suppliers, no single point of failure — is accurate as far as it goes. But it describes Anthropic's operational resilience, not the financial resilience of the ecosystem around it. A first-time operator with one customer does not have diversified revenue. It has a single revenue stream dressed up as an infrastructure business. If Anthropic's enterprise growth stalls, Volta has no fallback. The debt raised against the Norway site has no fallback. Bitdeer's shareholders, who are now indirectly underwriting frontier AI demand, have no fallback.

The number that makes this concrete is Anthropic's reported $30bn annualized revenue run rate set against compute commitments now spanning multiple multi-billion-dollar, multi-year contracts across a half-dozen counterparties. That run rate has to keep compounding — not just hold flat, but grow — to cover commitments structured on the assumption of continued scaling. Frontier pricing is not moving in a direction that makes this easier. Open-weight and Chinese models are compressing the cost floor on commodity inference tasks faster than anyone predicted eighteen months ago. If enterprise buyers start routing a meaningful share of workloads to cheaper alternatives, the revenue durability that makes all of this paper look solid begins to erode.

For investors trying to map exposure, the Volta deal clarifies where the interesting — and underpriced — risk actually sits. The frontier labs themselves have large, visible equity cushions and well-known backers who can bridge gaps. The new-entrant compute layer, financed on single-anchor contracts and marked to those contracts by the funds that hold it, has neither. Anyone holding that paper is effectively long Anthropic's revenue trajectory without the upside and without being compensated for the correlation. That is a bad trade at any valuation, and the Volta deal just made it a bigger one.

Family Office & Wealth Planning Robotics Venture Capital Family Office

Family offices aren't hedging the AI bubble. They're the biggest believers left.

  • Bernard Arnault's family office venture arm joined a Series A for Humanoid, a London-based industrial robot maker, within the past few weeks
  • Bezos Expeditions increased its position in Generalist AI, a US robotics software startup, while Azim Premji's family office — Premji Invest, the vehicle for the Indian IT billionaire's wealth — is in talks to lead a separate robotics funding round
  • The public AI trade is priced at $4 trillion market caps with no illiquidity premium remaining, making Series A robotics the last place the same bet is still available at venture-stage entry prices

The take

Over the past few weeks, the venture arm of Bernard Arnault's family office joined a Series A for Humanoid, a London industrial-robot maker; Bezos Expeditions topped up its stake in Generalist AI; and Azim Premji's family office is in talks to lead a round for another robotics startup. The framing in the Bloomberg piece is that family offices are "sidestepping AI fears" by rotating into robotics. That framing has it exactly backwards. This isn't a hedge against the AI bubble. It's a doubling-down on it, wearing a different jacket.

Here's what's actually going on. The public AI trade — Nvidia, the hyperscalers, the frontier labs — is now too crowded and too correlated for a family office to add anything. Everyone owns it, the multiples are priced for perfection, and there's no illiquidity premium left to harvest. Robotics is where the same bet is still available at Series A marks instead of $4 trillion market caps. Arnault and Premji aren't diversifying away from machine learning risk; they're buying the most capital-intensive, longest-duration, hardest-to-verify corner of it, and calling it prudence because it isn't a language model.

The tell is who's leading these rounds. When the operating businesses of Arnault (luxury), Bezos (logistics and cloud), and Premji (IT services) all move on humanoid robotics at once, that's not three independent investment theses. That's three families using permanent capital to buy call options on their own supply chains. LVMH wants robots in ateliers, Amazon wants them in warehouses, Wipro wants to sell them as a service. The family office is the vehicle precisely because the payback horizon — realistically 7 to 12 years to a working unit economic — is longer than any fund LP will tolerate. This is a structural advantage nobody else has, and it's the actual story.

The risk allocators should price is that robotics inherits the worst of both worlds: venture-stage burn rates with hardware-stage capital intensity and defense-stage regulatory drag. A humanoid Series A today is funding a company that has to be right on models, actuators, unit cost, and a decade of manufacturing scale-up before a single dollar of durable margin appears. The families can wait that out. A fund-of-funds allocator writing checks alongside them cannot, and will get marked at the family's entry price without the family's holding power.

So the read-across is simple. If you're co-investing next to these family offices, understand you're the tourist in a trade built for permanent capital. The right exposure here isn't the startup equity — it's the components, the actuators, the specialized chips, and the contract manufacturers that get paid whether or not any given humanoid company survives. Sell the picks and shovels. Let the billionaires fund the mine.

The other side

Robotics genuinely is a different risk profile than frontier AI — the moats come from hardware, proprietary training data on physical environments, and manufacturing relationships, none of which can be replicated by a better prompt or a new model release. The families backing these rounds have operating businesses that would become direct customers, which means they're underwriting demand, not just hoping for it. If that vertical integration thesis holds, the 'doubling down on AI' framing understates a real structural advantage that makes robotics a more defensible bet than another language model investment.

The mechanism worth understanding is why the family office structure specifically — rather than a traditional venture fund or a corporate venture arm — is the right vehicle for this trade. Venture funds run on a 10-year clock, sometimes extendable to 12, but limited partners are expecting distributions and markups on a schedule that has nothing to do with the actual development timeline for humanoid robotics. A convincing humanoid unit that can work reliably in an unstructured environment, at a cost that pencils out against human labor, is still realistically 7 to 12 years away from generating durable margins. No institutional LP will tolerate that duration without pressure to sell secondaries or write down the position. The family office has no such constraint. Permanent capital, in this context, is not just a structural nicety — it is the entire thesis.

The convergence of Arnault, Bezos, and Premji on the same sector within weeks of each other is worth interrogating rather than treating as coincidence. Bernard Arnault controls LVMH, the French luxury conglomerate, whose ateliers employ highly skilled craftspeople doing repetitive precision work — exactly the profile of tasks early industrial robots can displace or augment. Jeff Bezos built the most robotics-dense fulfillment network on earth at Amazon, and Bezos Expeditions is effectively a personal extension of his long-duration bets. Azim Premji founded Wipro, one of India's largest IT services firms, which has a direct commercial interest in selling robotics integration as a managed service to enterprise clients. These are not three generalist investors who independently decided robotics looked interesting. These are three operating empires quietly hedging their own labor costs and building proprietary exposure to the infrastructure their businesses will eventually need to buy.

The second-order risk that doesn't get enough attention is the co-investment dynamic this creates. When a family office with 50-year holding power takes a Series A position, it sets the mark. Other investors — fund-of-funds allocators, smaller single-family offices without the same permanence, growth equity funds looking for differentiated deal flow — often come in at or near that same entry price. But they don't inherit the same holding power. When a Series B is delayed, when a manufacturing partnership falls through, when the unit economics don't materialize on the expected timeline, those co-investors face pressure to exit or mark down in ways the anchor family office simply does not. They are tourists pricing themselves as locals.

The historical parallel that fits is early industrial automation in the 1980s. The families and conglomerates that owned both the factories and the early robot suppliers captured the full value chain as automation scaled. Everyone else who funded pure-play robotics startups without the downstream demand guarantee mostly got washed out before the technology was mature enough to generate returns. The picks-and-shovels layer — actuator manufacturers, specialized semiconductor designers building chips for edge inference in physical environments, and contract manufacturers with the precision tolerances humanoids require — got paid regardless of which humanoid company ultimately won. That dynamic is repeating, and the lesson is the same: the startup equity is the most narratively compelling and the most structurally exposed position in the stack.
