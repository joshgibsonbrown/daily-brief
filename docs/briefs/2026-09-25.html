Private Markets Blackstone Structured Credit Secondaries

Blackstone couldn't sell its own secondaries product to the market it invented.

privateequitywire.co.uk
  • Project Eclipse — a $3bn collateralised fund obligation (CFO) built on ~700 PE fund stakes — has been formally shelved after months of failed marketing, with junior debt yielding up to 12% still unable to attract buyers.
  • Roughly 23% of the underlying collateral is 15 years old or older, with 8% dating back at least two decades — the long-dated nature of the assets was the primary investor objection.
  • Blackstone could have retained the equity and some junior debt to address concerns, but sources say that structure broke the economics of the deal for Blackstone itself.

The take

This is the fifth or sixth time this month the same theme has landed here — everyone from GIC to Partners Group to Morgan Stanley building an off-ramp for aging private assets — but this instance breaks the pattern in a specific way. Blackstone, the firm that literally owns the largest secondaries business in the world through Strategic Partners, could not clear a $3bn deal designed to solve exactly the problem it sells other people solutions for. Project Eclipse has now been "weighing abandonment" since mid-August and is now formally shelved. Six weeks, and the smartest liquidity engineers in the business couldn't find a buyer.

Look at what the market actually rejected. The junior debt was being marketed at up to 12% yield, the senior at 7.5%. That is not tight pricing. That is a deal screaming for demand, and buyers still walked. The reason is in the collateral: 700 fund interests, roughly a quarter of them fifteen years old or older, 8% dating back at least two decades. A twenty-year-old fund stake is not an illiquid asset waiting for a market — it is an asset the entire secondaries food chain has already passed on, repeatedly, priced, and declined. Wrapping 700 of them in a bond structure does not change what they are. It just adds leverage on top of assets nobody wanted unlevered.

This matters because CFO issuance was supposed to be the next relief valve, with Evercore projecting volume roughly doubling to about $30bn this year. AlpInvest, Dawson, Ardian — everyone is building these. The pitch is that you can manufacture a liquid, rated instrument out of a pool of stale LP interests. Eclipse is the test case for the tail of that trade, and the tail failed. There is a real distinction the CFO boom has been blurring: recent, marked-up secondary positions can be securitized because a genuine bid exists underneath them. Genuinely aged, orphaned fund stakes cannot, because there is no clearing price — only a mark that hasn't been tested against cash.

The knock-on that lands on whoever is holding these structures: the CFO market is going to bifurcate hard between young collateral and old collateral, and the old collateral is where the sponsors most need liquidity and least deserve it. If Blackstone modifies Eclipse and returns — the report leaves that door open — it does so by keeping the equity and some junior debt itself, which the sources say breaks the economics. Translation: the only way the deal works is if Blackstone eats the risk it was trying to sell. That is not a financing. That is a mark-to-market event Blackstone chose not to trigger.

The broader signal for anyone still treating structured secondaries as a guaranteed release valve: the valve works until the assets get old enough that the discount required to move them exceeds what the sponsor will admit to on paper. Eclipse just found that wall. Everyone building CFOs on 2005-vintage paper should assume they'll find it too.

The other side

One failed deal doesn't indict the CFO market — it indicts one deal's collateral. The broader CFO pipeline is dominated by recent, higher-quality secondary positions where a genuine bid exists underneath the structure, and Evercore's projection of roughly $30bn in CFO issuance this year reflects real demand for that cleaner paper. Eclipse may simply be an outlier: a portfolio assembled from the deepest tail of an aging secondaries book, priced aggressively, and correctly rejected on its own merits. Blackstone retains the option to restructure and return, and a modified deal with better collateral selection or a different tranche structure could clear without any broader implications for the asset class.

The CFO — collateralised fund obligation — is structurally similar to a CLO, except instead of corporate loans, the collateral is a pool of LP stakes in private equity funds. The pitch is that diversification across hundreds of fund interests smooths idiosyncratic risk enough to support a rated bond on top. That logic holds when the underlying stakes have a discoverable market price. It breaks when the stakes are old enough that the secondaries market has already priced them, declined them, and moved on.

Eclipse's collateral is a case study in that distinction. About 700 fund interests, with roughly a quarter of them between 15 and 20 years old and another 8% dating back at least two decades. A fund stake from 2006 has been through the 2008 crisis, a decade of low-rate-driven multiple expansion, and a post-2022 distribution drought. If it hasn't been sold by now, there is a reason — and that reason is almost certainly that every sophisticated buyer who looked at it decided the discount required to compensate for uncertainty, illiquidity, and remaining life was too steep for the seller to accept. Packaging 700 of those decisions into a bond and adding leverage does not resolve the underlying disagreement about price. It just concentrates the risk in whoever buys the junior tranche.

The pricing tells you how hard Blackstone was trying. A 12% yield on junior debt is not a routine structured credit coupon — that is distressed-adjacent territory for a product being marketed as a diversified, engineered liquidity solution. The senior tranche at 7.5% is more conventional, but the gap between senior and junior implies the market was being asked to absorb meaningful first-loss risk on assets with no clear exit path. Buyers read that correctly and walked.

The supplementary reporting from mid-August shows this wasn't a sudden decision — Blackstone was already weighing abandonment six weeks before the formal shelving. That timeline matters because it means the firm spent months trying to solve the equity tranche problem and couldn't. The sources indicate that the only structural fix — Blackstone retaining the equity and some junior debt — would have destroyed the economics for Blackstone. Which means the deal was only viable if someone else agreed to hold the riskiest slice of a portfolio of assets that the entire secondaries industry had previously passed on. That buyer did not materialise at any yield on offer.

The CFO market more broadly is now going to face a sorting problem. AlpInvest (part of Carlyle), Dawson Partners, and Ardian are all building CFO vehicles, and the asset class was being positioned as a scalable release valve for the distribution drought hitting LP portfolios. Eclipse demonstrates that the release valve has a pressure limit. Vehicles built on recent, marked-up secondary positions — where a cash bid genuinely exists underneath the structure — will likely clear fine. Vehicles built on the oldest, most orphaned tail of aging PE books will not, regardless of how the coupon is set, because the discount required to move the collateral exceeds what the sponsor will recognise on its own books. Triggering that discount is a mark-to-market event, and Blackstone chose to shelve the deal rather than take it.

That choice is itself informative. The firm that runs the largest secondaries business in the world, with more information about clearing prices for aged fund stakes than any other market participant, looked at what Eclipse would require and decided the accounting consequence was worse than the liquidity benefit. Anyone else building CFOs on comparable vintage paper should treat that as a data point about where the floor actually is.

Family Office & Wealth Planning Succession Governance Citi Direct Investing

A third of family offices expect a leadership change in five years and most have no real plan for it.

altassets.net, investmentnews.com, briefs.co, ijr.com
  • One-third of the 351 family offices surveyed by Citi expect a leadership transition within five years, with unclear succession plans, unready heirs, and lack of shared vision named as the top three barriers
  • Forty percent plan to increase direct investing — the most illiquid, relationship-dependent assets on the balance sheet — even as governance infrastructure lags behind investment sophistication
  • Inflation concern jumped from 37% to 63% of respondents year-over-year, while trade and tariff anxiety collapsed from 60% to 18%, reshaping how these offices are building defensive positioning

The take

Third pass on the Citi report in a week. The public-equities rotation and inflation numbers we've covered; the new data point worth mining is succession, and it's the one line item that doesn't fit the rest of the survey's story.

Here's the tension nobody in the wealth-management press is sitting with. Roughly a third of these offices expect a leadership change inside five years, and the top three barriers they name are unclear plans, unready heirs, and no shared vision. That is not a scheduling problem. It's an admission that the average single-family office has spent a decade professionalizing its investment engine — direct deals, hedging, AI in the due-diligence stack — while leaving the governance layer as informal as it was when the founder wrote checks off a legal pad. You cannot run a $500m direct-investment book on a handshake decision process once the person whose handshake it was is gone.

Now layer that against what the same families are actually buying. Monnier's own framing is that the next gen wants direct deals because you can "touch it" — a stake in a business across the street beats a line item in a fund report. Forty percent plan to step up direct investing. Read that honestly: you are handing the least tested, least governed generation the most illiquid, most concentrated, hardest-to-exit assets on the balance sheet, and calling it engagement. Direct positions are exactly what a shaky succession destroys value in — no manager to manage the position through the transition, no secondary bid on a Tuesday, valuation entirely dependent on relationships the departing principal owned personally.

The public-equities pivot is the quiet acknowledgment of this. Half these offices raised listed exposure and 42% didn't touch their equity mix at all — that's not a market call, that's pre-positioning for a handover. Liquid books transfer cleanly. You can split a portfolio of developed-market equities across three siblings and a trust in an afternoon. You cannot split a 90% stake in a European security business three ways without someone getting bought out at a number that starts a lawsuit. The rotation to quality and liquidity and the coming succession wave are the same trade, and the survey treats them as unrelated findings in different chapters.

The opportunity sits in the gap between the two. Every adviser is pitching the investment side — access, sourcing, co-invest. The scarce, defensible, sticky mandate is governance architecture: decision frameworks, family constitutions, the boring machinery that determines whether the direct-investment book survives contact with the second generation. Cross-border complexity compounds it — 38% expect their geographic footprint to grow, meaning the succession happens across multiple tax regimes at once, right as Beijing and others are tightening the offshore structures these families used to hold it all.

The firms that win the next decade of this money won't be the ones with the best deal flow. They'll be the ones who were in the room when the founder finally admitted the plan on the legal pad wasn't a plan.

The other side

The succession alarm may be overstated. Family offices have always operated on long, informal timelines, and the fact that a third are now naming the problem explicitly — and engaging tools like advisory boards, structured investment exposure, and educational programs — suggests the governance gap is closing, not widening. The same professionalisation that built out the direct-investment stack over the past decade is now turning toward the human layer, and the five-year window is long enough to build real frameworks if the intent is there.

Say more →

The Citi survey covered 351 single-family offices across 41 countries, polled in June and July 2026 — a sample large enough to treat the succession data as structurally meaningful rather than anecdotal. What makes the finding striking is the mismatch in maturity it reveals. These offices have spent years building institutional-grade investment operations: 40% plan to increase direct investing, APAC offices report 79% participation in direct deals, and AI has moved from aspiration to active deployment across due diligence, reporting, and workflow. The investment engine is sophisticated. The ownership transfer mechanism is not.

The three barriers respondents name — unclear plans, insufficient readiness among future leaders, and lack of shared vision — are not resource problems. A family office managing a nine-figure direct-investment book has the capital to hire advisers, draft documents, and run governance workshops. What those three barriers describe is a founder who has not yet made the psychological transition from operator to steward, and an institution that has built around that founder's judgment rather than around repeatable process. That works until it doesn't.

The direct-investment concentration makes the timing particularly sharp. Alexandre Monnier, head of family office advisory at Citi Wealth, frames the next generation's preference for direct deals as a feature — tangible assets, real ownership, something you can understand beyond a line in a report. That framing is accurate as far as it goes. But direct positions carry a specific vulnerability at transition: their value is often inseparable from the relationships, reputation, and deal access of the person who sourced them. A co-investment in a private company secured because the founder sat on a board or knew the CEO personally does not automatically transfer to a 34-year-old heir, however capable. The position may be on the balance sheet, but the edge that made it worth owning may not survive the handover.

The public-equity rotation sits in a different light once you hold that context. A net 34% of respondents increased listed equity exposure over the past year, and nearly a third plan further additions to global developed-market stocks over the next 12 months. Liquid, transparent, divisible assets are exactly what a multi-beneficiary estate needs when the founder's personal relationships are no longer the glue holding the portfolio together. The rotation toward quality and liquidity is prudent portfolio construction on its own terms, but it also functions as quiet succession preparation — whether or not the offices framing it as a macro call would describe it that way.

Cross-border complexity sharpens the urgency further. Some 38% of respondents expect their family's geographic footprint to expand over the next five years. A succession that was already complicated by unclear governance now has to execute across multiple tax regimes simultaneously, at a moment when jurisdictions from Beijing to Singapore are actively tightening the offshore structures these families have historically used to hold assets. The governance gap and the regulatory tightening are converging on the same five-year window the survey identifies as the transition horizon.

The advisory opportunity in that gap is real and underserved. Most wealth managers are competing on investment access — deal flow, co-invest rights, sourcing relationships. Those are commoditised faster than the firms offering them tend to admit. The mandate that is genuinely scarce is the one that sits upstream of the investment decision: family constitutions, decision-rights frameworks, conflict-resolution mechanisms, the architecture that determines whether a direct-investment book survives contact with a second generation that has three different views on what the family office is actually for. That work is less glamorous than a co-invest in a growth-stage company. It is also the work that determines whether the co-invest is still intact in a decade.

ICONIQ Portfolio Companies Anthropic Biotech IPO

Anthropic's biology lab is an IPO story dressed as a science story.

techcrunch.com, the-decoder.com, anthropic.com
  • Claude ran roughly 950 agents for 21 hours, burning 210 million tokens to flag a novel enzyme system in bacteriophage DNA — function still unknown, pre-print not peer-reviewed
  • Lucas Harrington, who co-founded Mammoth Biosciences with CRISPR Nobel laureate Jennifer Doudna, called the method routine genome mining with similar systems known since 2008
  • The Verge noted the announcement lands as Anthropic prepares to go public — the same week Amodei's slowdown plea collided with Reuters reporting on an accelerated model release timeline

The take

Anthropic revealed a Bay Area wet lab this month, and days later claimed its first big result: Claude, running roughly 950 agents over 21 hours and 210 million tokens, flagged a novel enzyme system in phage DNA it's calling ART, with "properties reminiscent of CRISPR." Feng Zhang, one of CRISPR's inventors, called the finding "genuinely intriguing." Lucas Harrington — Doudna's former PhD student and a Mammoth Biosciences co-founder — called it routine genome mining that's been possible for a decade, with the hard part (what the enzyme actually does) still unsolved. Both can be right. That's exactly why the timing matters.

The Verge buried the actual story in one clause: this drops "as the company prepares to go public." Line that up against what we covered on September 21 — Amodei's 3,800-word slowdown plea landing eight days before Reuters reported the company was weighing an accelerated model release ahead of an IPO. This is the same playbook. Astra is eating Claude's enterprise share (13% versus 8% on Ramp's tracking), open-weight Chinese models are crushing per-token pricing, and Anthropic needs a growth story that isn't a race to the bottom on inference margins. "Claude cures disease" is a far better IPO narrative than "Claude writes cheaper code than GLM."

So watch what got optimized. The discovery is unvalidated — function unknown, pre-print not peer-reviewed, the underlying enzyme already known from prior work. The precise, quantified part is the compute: 950 agents, 210 million tokens, 21 hours. Anthropic is not really selling a biology result here. It's selling a demonstration that you can point a swarm of Claude agents at a scientific database and burn 210 million tokens to find something a lab full of PhDs would take months to surface. The enzyme is the demo. Token consumption at scale is the product, and the buyer is a drug-discovery or biotech customer who currently pays for humans.

That reframes the skeptic fight. Whether ART turns out to be the next CRISPR barely matters to the thing being underwritten. What matters is whether pharma R&D budgets — call it a rough $250-300B a year globally as outside context — start routing a slice through agentic search instead of headcount. If even a few percent moves, that's a bigger and stickier revenue line than enterprise coding, and it's one OpenAI's consumer-facing Astra brand is worse positioned to sell into. Anthropic is trying to escape the commodity-token knife fight by climbing into the highest-margin knowledge work on earth.

The risk is that this is Amodei's second contradiction in a month. He warns about AI-enabled bioterror as his signature fear, then publicly celebrates his AI mining phage genomes for programmable DNA-cutting enzymes. The safety-narrative and the growth-narrative are now openly at war inside the same company, weeks before it asks public markets to price both. Buy the compute-consumption thesis if you like it. Just don't confuse it with a validated scientific breakthrough — Anthropic is counting on you to.

The other side

Feng Zhang, one of the scientists who pioneered CRISPR genome editing and a professor at MIT and the Broad Institute, reviewed the pre-print and called the RNA-repeat array finding 'genuinely intriguing' and worthy of further investigation — that is not a throwaway endorsement. More importantly, the critics are grading on a science rubric when the actual claim is narrower: that agentic AI can compress months of computational genomics work into 21 hours. If that compression is real and reproducible across protein families, the skeptics are right about this specific enzyme and wrong about the underlying capability being demonstrated.

Say more →

The structure of this announcement is worth pulling apart carefully, because the quantified and the unquantified are doing very different jobs.

What is precisely measured: 950 agents, 21 hours, 210 million tokens, a database of over 200,000 reverse transcriptases narrowed to 3,500 candidate systems, then to 20 human-readable reports, then to one flagged result. That chain of numbers is not accidental. It is a procurement argument aimed at a specific buyer — a pharma or biotech R&D team that currently pays a team of computational biologists to do the same narrowing over months.

What is not measured: whether ART does anything useful. Anthropic's own announcement states the function is unknown. The pre-print is not peer-reviewed. Amodei acknowledged on X that a Stanford team previously found a system 'in some ways similar.' Harrington, whose Mammoth Biosciences uses genome mining professionally to hunt for CRISPR systems, was direct: the method has existed for decades, similar systems have been known since 2008, and the hard part — characterizing what a system actually does — remains untouched.

Both things are simultaneously true, and that is the point. Anthropic is not claiming a drug. It is claiming a workflow. The enzyme is the demo reel; the product is agentic search applied to biological databases at a scale and speed that human teams cannot match on cost. Whether ART eventually joins CRISPR and restriction enzymes in the toolkit of useful molecular machines is a question for peer review over the next few years. Whether a pharma company will pay for Claude to run this kind of sweep across their proprietary sequence data is a question that can be answered in a sales cycle.

The lab itself is deliberately constrained in ways that matter for the safety narrative. It operates only at biosafety levels BSL-1 and BSL-2 — the lowest tiers, covering work with organisms not known to cause disease in healthy adults. No human pathogens. All physical experiments performed by human scientists. Claude has not been given control of lab equipment. Amodei left the door open to a fully automated lab eventually, but the current setup is specifically designed to be defensible against the bioterrorism concern he has publicly named as one of his primary fears.

That defensibility is doing real work here, because the contradiction is visible. Anthropic's safety positioning — the reason it has historically commanded a premium over less safety-focused competitors — depends on Amodei being credible when he raises biosecurity concerns. Celebrating Claude mining phage genomes for programmable DNA-cutting enzymes in the same month he published a 3,800-word case for slowing down AI development creates a tension that public market investors will eventually have to price. The safety brand and the growth narrative are pulling in opposite directions, and the IPO will force a resolution.

The competitive logic is cleaner. OpenAI's Astra is a consumer-adjacent product; its brand is built around general capability and coding productivity. Drug discovery and fundamental biology research are relationship-intensive, regulated, and require deep domain credibility — exactly the kind of market where Anthropic's more academic, safety-forward positioning is an asset rather than a liability. If Anthropic can establish Claude as the default agentic layer for computational biology before Astra's distribution advantages fully compound, it carves out a defensible vertical with margins that inference commodity pricing cannot easily erode. That is the escape route from the per-token knife fight, and this announcement is the opening move.

Tech & AI China Semiconductors Compute

Alibaba's V900 still has no FLOPS figure. That omission is the spec sheet.

tomshardware.com
  • The V900's spec sheet lists 216GB of memory and 1,200GB/s inter-chip bandwidth but omits FLOPS, process node, foundry, and power draw — Huawei's competing Ascend 960PR publishes all four.
  • Alibaba pulled the V900's commercial release forward to Q1 2027 from Q3 2027, while CEO Eddie Wu simultaneously acknowledged that global supply-chain shortages are limiting Alibaba's ability to scale compute.
  • Qwen 4 is currently in training; the 5-to-10-trillion-parameter model on the roadmap has no hardware to run on until mass production begins next year.

The take

Second time on the V900 in two days. The chip announcement itself hasn't changed — same 216GB, same 500,000-card supercluster, same Q1 2027 production date pulled forward from Q3. What's worth revisiting is the one number Alibaba still refuses to publish: FLOPS.

Eddie Wu called this "the most powerful AI chip in China" and Alibaba described it as three times the M890, but there is no absolute compute figure, no process node, no foundry, no power draw. Compare that to Huawei's Ascend 960PR, which at least ships a spec sheet — 192GB, 2.4TB/s memory bandwidth, 2.2TB/s scale-up interconnect. Alibaba gives you memory capacity and inter-chip bandwidth and asks you to infer the rest. When a vendor leads with cluster size (500,000 cards) instead of per-chip throughput, it's telling you the per-chip number isn't the flex. The play is aggregation: enough silicon, wired densely enough, that you brute-force your way to a 10-trillion-parameter Qwen even if each accelerator is a fraction of a Blackwell.

That's a rational answer to a hardware embargo, and it's the same logic behind the 20-gigawatt-by-2032 target I flagged Tuesday. But it has a cost nobody in the Apsara keynote wanted to state: Wu himself said global supply-chain shortages are "currently limiting the speed at which we can scale." Translate that. Alibaba can design a chip and pull the timeline forward six months, but it can't manufacture at the volume its own roadmap demands, because the leading-edge foundry capacity for a 500,000-card cluster isn't domestically available at scale. Huawei already shelved its global Ascend rollout to feed domestic demand. So the real constraint on Chinese frontier compute isn't design talent — it's wafers, and that hasn't moved.

The tell worth watching — the delivered training run, not the press release. Alibaba's own evidence is thin: Qwen3.8-Max ran 33 automated iteration cycles over a month to move its Artificial Analysis score from 40 to 45. That's a five-point gain from a month of compute you can't independently verify was done on V900 silicon, because the V900 doesn't exist in mass production yet. Qwen 4 is "in training." The 5-to-10-trillion-parameter model is a roadmap slide. Every capability claim here is forward-dated to hardware that ships in 2027.

For a Singapore book, the actionable piece isn't whether China closes the chip gap — it's the timing arbitrage between announcement and delivery. Alibaba is compressing its stated roadmap by two quarters while admitting it can't source enough silicon to hit it. Those two facts are in the same keynote. When a company advances a date and pleads supply constraint in the same breath, the date is marketing and the constraint is the balance sheet. Price the constraint. The chip that matters is the one that trains a verifiable model at cost, and that chip is still a year of unverified manufacturing away.

The other side

Cluster scale is a legitimate engineering strategy, not a dodge. If Alibaba can wire 500,000 V900 cards into a single coherent supernode — a non-trivial networking achievement — the aggregate throughput can exceed what any single chip's FLOPS figure would suggest, and the 42% chip-area reduction Alibaba demonstrated in its EDA experiment points to real design competence, not just marketing. The six-month pull-forward on the production timeline, from a May roadmap that placed V900 in Q3 2027 to a Q1 2027 commitment announced this week, is also a concrete operational signal, not a vague aspiration.

Say more →

The pattern here is worth naming precisely because it keeps recurring across Chinese AI hardware announcements: capability is asserted by comparison rather than by absolute measure. The V900 is three times the M890. The M890 could cluster 128 chips; the V900 can cluster more than 1,000 as a single system. These are real improvements, but they are improvements over a baseline Alibaba also controls, and neither figure anchors to an external reference point a buyer can independently verify.

Huawei's Ascend 960PR, due in Q3 2027, at least gives procurement teams something to work with: 192GB of memory, 2.4TB/s of memory bandwidth, 2.2TB/s scale-up interconnect. Those numbers can be stress-tested against workloads. Alibaba's V900 cannot be stress-tested yet because it doesn't exist in commercial volume, and the benchmark Alibaba did publish — Qwen3.8-Max moving from 40 to 45 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index over 33 automated cycles across a month — was almost certainly not run on V900 silicon, since the chip isn't in mass production. The result is a capability claim that is simultaneously forward-dated to 2027 hardware and backward-attributed to a current model.

The customer traction numbers are real and worth separating from the hardware claims. As of May, T-Head — Alibaba's chip division — had shipped more than 560,000 Zhenwu chips to over 400 external customers. Alibaba now puts that customer count above 650. That's a functioning external market, not a captive internal deployment, and it means the V900 has a commercial base to land into when production begins. The question is whether production can actually meet the demand that the roadmap implies.

Wu's supply-chain admission is the most important sentence in the Apsara keynote and received the least attention. Huawei shelved its global Ascend rollout to prioritize domestic Chinese demand — a fact the source material notes directly — which tells you something about how tight leading-edge wafer capacity is even for the most strategically prioritized Chinese hardware programs. Alibaba is designing chips that require foundry capacity it does not control domestically at the volumes a 500,000-card supercluster demands. Pulling the production date forward by two quarters while acknowledging that constraint doesn't resolve the constraint; it tightens the margin for error.

The 20-gigawatt data center target by 2032 sits six years out and is subject to the same foundry bottleneck. For context outside this story, SpaceX is targeting more than 10 gigawatts of AI compute by 2027 and currently operates around 1.4 gigawatts — a comparison Alibaba itself invited in the keynote. The gap between Alibaba's 2032 ambition and where the leading US hyperscalers will likely be by then is not obviously closing on the current trajectory, supply constraints being what they are.

What the V900 announcement does accomplish is competitive signaling at a specific moment: Huawei launched a new chip last week, and Alibaba responded within days with a higher-profile announcement and a more aggressive timeline. That sequencing is deliberate. Whether the timeline holds is a manufacturing question, and the answer to that question won't be visible until Q1 2027 shipment volumes are reported.

Geopolitics Iran Energy Gulf

A phased Hormuz swap is on the table. The clocks on each side run in opposite directions.

aljazeera.com, theprint.in, al-monitor.com
  • Iran signaled it will move its transit-fee demand off the main agreement into a side attachment — the first structural concession on the issue in eight months of talks
  • A senior Iranian official told Reuters the strait could reopen within seven days if Washington lifts its naval blockade and eases military pressure
  • Dennis Ross, a former US negotiator on Iran, puts the odds of a deal before the November 3 midterms at 30%

The take

Ninth time this section has tracked the Hormuz standoff since July, and the New York shuttle talks are the first direct-ish contact since the July collapse. What's actually new: both sides now openly frame the endgame as a phased swap — Iran reopens the strait, Washington lifts the blockade and unfreezes assets — and, crucially, Iran has signaled it will drop the transit-fee demand into a side attachment rather than the main agreement. That's the concession the last eight months of coverage said wouldn't come.

Don't confuse it with softening. Iran moved the fee off the main table precisely because it clarified what it actually cares about: administrative control of the waterway, not tolls. The senior Iranian official's line — "our fingers are on the trigger" — sits in the same breath as the offer. Tehran is trading the least valuable piece of its position to protect the most valuable one. Fees are recoverable later; control, once ceded to a Gulf or multinational arrangement, is gone. Read the paperwork play we've flagged since September: Iran wins by keeping its name on the lane at the IMO, not by collecting a quarter-point on cargo.

The pricing signal worth watching is Brent at roughly $100 with the market treating these talks as constructive. That's the market front-running a deal that Dennis Ross puts at 30% before the November 3 midterms. The mispricing is the timeline, not the direction. Trump has told everyone the deal comes "after the midterms," and the mechanics explain why: before the vote he's the constrained party — high gasoline prices, low munitions stocks, Republicans bleeding on diesel-driven inflation — so he needs the deal more than Tehran does. After the vote, as Ross and the Israeli intelligence read both note, the pressure asymmetry flips and Trump regains the option to escalate. Iran knows this. So Tehran's genuine incentive is to close before November while Washington is soft, and Washington's incentive is to stall past it. Those two clocks run in opposite directions, which is why the talks can look productive and still produce nothing.

The party that keeps getting squeezed in this structure is the Gulf. Saudi Arabia is already selling 60 million barrels a month delivered off Oman, outside the strait entirely; the UAE is back to pre-war export volumes by physically routing around the chokepoint. They've engineered their way past Hormuz operationally, but they've rejected both Iranian control and transit fees, and they're the ones pressing Trump for an endgame he won't define. A phased deal that leaves Iran with "administrative control" is precisely the outcome they told him they won't accept. Whoever the $4 trillion of Gulf sovereign capital is underwriting a reopening, the version most likely to actually clear is the one those funds have publicly refused. The other side

The case for genuine near-term progress is real: Iran's economy is being strangled by a US blockade that has choked off oil exports, targeted its remaining commercial airlines, and is driving diesel-inflation that hurts ordinary Iranians. The blockade, as Ross put it, is 'strangling them.' An Iranian delegation arrived in New York with full authority to pursue diplomacy and described the UNGA gathering as a 'golden opportunity' — language that suggests Tehran's pragmatists have at least temporarily outrun the hardliners who called the New York trip an insult to the slain supreme leader. If economic pain is acute enough, the 30% probability Ross assigns may be too conservative.

The structural problem with the New York talks is not that they failed — they didn't, exactly — but that both sides left with their core leverage intact and their core demands unmet. Washington controls the blockade that is strangling Tehran's budget. Tehran controls access to a waterway that carries roughly a fifth of global oil and a third of seaborne LNG. Neither side moved off that position in New York; what moved was a fee demand that Iran had already decided was expendable.

The transit-fee concession deserves to be read carefully. Iran dropping the toll into a side attachment is not a retreat from its position on Hormuz — it is a clarification of what that position actually is. The senior Iranian official quoted by Reuters was explicit: Iran is not primarily focused on collecting fees; it is focused on preserving administrative control of the waterway. That distinction matters enormously for the Gulf states, who have publicly rejected both Iranian control and transit fees. A phased deal structured around blockade relief in exchange for reopening the strait — without resolving who administers it — is precisely the outcome Gulf leaders told Trump they would not accept when they met him in New York on the same day the shuttle talks were running.

The Gulf's operational workaround is real but incomplete. Saudi Arabia has sold 60 million barrels for both September and October delivery off the coast of Oman, outside the strait entirely. UAE exports have recovered to pre-war levels by physically routing around the chokepoint. But routing around Hormuz is expensive — shipping companies are charging fees of as much as a quarter of a cargo's value to sail through the strait with transponders off — and it does nothing to resolve the underlying question of who controls the lane. The Gulf states want that question answered before any deal closes. The phased structure being discussed in New York explicitly defers it.

The midterm clock compounds this. Trump has said publicly he expects a deal around the November 3 elections. The mechanics of that timeline favor Tehran in the near term: high US gasoline prices, Republican exposure to diesel-driven inflation, and military commanders who warned munitions stocks were running low after the July resumption of fighting all constrain how hard Washington can push before the vote. After the midterms, as both Ross and regional analysts note, those constraints ease and Trump regains the option to escalate or intensify pressure. Iran's rational move is to close a deal while Washington is soft; Washington's rational move is to extract more concessions by waiting. The talks can look productive — and Tuesday's shuttle contacts genuinely were the first substantive engagement since July — while the underlying incentive structure pushes both sides toward delay.

Brent crude hovering near $100 on the back of these talks reflects the market pricing in directional progress without pricing in the timeline risk. A third of Gulf oil is still estimated to be missing from global supplies, refined-product shortages are driving inflation across multiple economies, and the gap between the deal that ends the war and the deal the Gulf will actually tolerate has not closed. The $100 print treats the phased swap as more durable than the mechanics suggest it is.
