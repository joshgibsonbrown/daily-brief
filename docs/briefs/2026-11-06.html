Perkins is refounding Canva, not just cutting it

techcrunch.com, fortune.com, afr.com
  • Canva's revenue growth forecast has been cut from 30% to 20%, and T. Rowe Price's latest mark puts the implied valuation at $30.4 billion — down from $42 billion at the August 2025 employee stock sale peak
  • Perkins is offering laid-off workers $10,000 founder grants and six months of severance — unusually generous terms that will seed a new wave of Sydney-based startups
  • The restructure merges product, growth, and platform into a single org and requires remote staff to relocate, a structural overhaul Perkins describes as the company's first in thirteen years

The take

Second time on Canva in three days. Monday's piece covered the T. Rowe markdown to $30.4 billion and the 1,271 cuts as a valuation story. What's worth adding is Perkins' memo itself, because it reframes what this layoff actually is.

Most tech layoffs are margin management dressed up as strategy — trim 10%, tell the board you're "focused," protect the forward number. Perkins is doing something structurally different, and she said so in plain language: she's "refounding" the company, merging product, growth and platform into one org, killing sub-specialization, and dragging remote staff back to Sydney. That is not an efficiency cut. That's an admission that the operating model that got Canva to $3.5 billion in revenue is the wrong model for what's coming.

Here's the mechanism she's responding to. Canva's business was selling design tools to hundreds of millions of mostly-free users and converting a slice to paid. AI broke both ends of that funnel at once. On the cost side, serving generative features to a non-paying base is a variable-cost nightmare — which is exactly why they built their own model, Brush, at 46% lower cost per edit, and why they still had to cut the revenue growth forecast from 30% to 20%. On the demand side, ChatGPT and Gemini now produce a passable poster or deck for free, inside a tool the user already opens for other reasons. Canva's moat was "design is hard and we made it easy." When a general chatbot makes it trivial, the moat is the product category itself.

So the markdown from $42 billion to roughly $30 billion isn't the story — the story is that the terminal value is now genuinely contested. A 20% grower with ballooning inference costs and a free tier being eaten by OpenAI is not an $11x-revenue IPO. It's a mid-single-digit-multiple software company with an AI cost problem, and the T. Rowe mark at $30.4 billion is still pricing in that Perkins pulls off the refounding.

Two things to watch. First, the $10,000 founder grants to departed staff and six months' severance are being praised as humane, and they are — but they also seed an alumni network in Sydney that Blackbird, Airtree and Square Peg will fund. The Australian venture ecosystem Canva created is about to get its second wave of founders from the same event that marked down its anchor asset. That's a quietly bullish signal for those three funds' next vintages even as their flagship position shrinks.

Second, note that Canva is simultaneously building Brush and deepening its OpenAI partnership through GPT-6 Astra. That's the Canva-the-operator tell versus Canva-the-valuation: they're hedging between owning the model to protect margin and renting the frontier to stay competitive on quality. You can't fully win both. Whichever they lean into over the next year tells you whether the IPO is a 2027 event at a real number or a 2029 event at a number nobody currently wants to underwrite. Right now the smart money is assuming the latter.

The other side

The bear case on the refounding is that Canva's problems are product-category problems, not org-chart problems. Merging teams and killing sub-specialization speeds up decision-making, but it doesn't resolve the core tension: generative AI has made 'good enough' design free, and no amount of internal restructuring changes what ChatGPT and Gemini offer at zero marginal cost to the user. Perkins is also asking a workforce that just watched 22% of its colleagues leave to relocate to Sydney and take on broader responsibilities — retention risk among the people who stayed is real, and the engineers Canva most needs to execute the AI pivot have the most outside options.

The detail that sharpens the picture is the sequencing. Canva entered 2026 at a $40 billion valuation with an IPO widely expected to be the largest Australian tech listing ever. By August, Blackbird — its biggest shareholder — had cut its internal valuation by 17% to roughly $35 billion. T. Rowe Price's SEC filing, covering positions as of August 31, put the implied number at $30.4 billion. The markdown cascade is not one institution's view; it is a coordinated repricing by the investors who know the business best.

The mechanism behind that repricing is worth being precise about. Canva's original model was a freemium funnel: give design tools to hundreds of millions of users, convert a fraction to paid subscriptions. That model worked because design was genuinely hard and Canva made it easy. The AI era broke the funnel at both ends simultaneously. On the demand side, general-purpose tools — ChatGPT, Gemini, and image generators embedded in platforms users already live in — now produce a serviceable poster or presentation without requiring the user to open Canva at all. On the cost side, serving generative features to a predominantly non-paying base creates a variable-cost structure that scales against you: the more users engage with AI features, the more inference costs rise, regardless of whether those users ever convert to paid.

Canva's response to the cost side was to build Brush, its own image-generation model, which the company says runs 46% cheaper per edit and up to 69% faster than the tool it replaced. That is a real engineering achievement. But the company simultaneously expanded its OpenAI partnership to include GPT-6 Astra, which means it is hedging — owning the model to protect margin while renting the frontier to stay competitive on quality. Those two strategies pull in opposite directions on the income statement, and the growth forecast cut from 30% to 20% suggests the hedge is not yet resolving cleanly.

Perkins' memo is notable for naming this honestly. Most CEOs running a restructure of this scale describe it as 'sharpening focus' or 'right-sizing for the next phase.' Perkins used the word 'refounding,' which is a much higher-stakes claim. It implies the operating model that generated $3.5 billion in annual revenue — the org structure, the specialization, the distributed remote workforce — is itself the problem, not just a cost line to trim. Whether that diagnosis is correct matters enormously for the IPO timeline. A company executing a genuine refounding is not ready to go public in 2027; the story isn't stable enough to underwrite. A company that completes the restructure, demonstrates that the merged org can ship faster, and shows the growth rate stabilizing above 20% might have a credible 2028 or 2029 path.

The $10,000 founder grants to departing employees are getting attention as a humane gesture, and the intent appears genuine — Perkins herself was rejected by more than 100 investors before Canva got off the ground. But the downstream effect is structural: Canva's alumni, funded by those grants and backed by Blackbird, Airtree, and Square Peg — the three Australian VC funds whose own reputations were built on early Canva positions — will form the next cohort of Sydney-based founders. The same event that marks down those funds' flagship position seeds the deal flow for their next vintage. That is a real, if delayed, offset to the headline damage.

Sagard raised €520 million to back emerging managers nobody else will underwrite directly.

pitchbook.com, sagard.com, themiddlemarket.com
  • The fund closed at €520 million against a €400 million target — roughly three times the size of its 2023 predecessor, which missed its own €300 million target at €176 million.
  • US experienced managers (fourth fund or later) captured 88% of all PE capital raised in both 2024 and 2025, above the 81% ten-year average, leaving first- and second-time managers structurally starved of direct LP commitments.
  • GCM Grosvenor's Elevate Fund, which seeds small and emerging buyout firms, closed near $800 million in 2025 — meaning two intermediaries now hold roughly $1.4 billion in combined dry powder as the effective gatekeepers for the emerging-manager class.

The take

Sagard just closed the largest fund-of-funds ever dedicated to emerging buyout managers — €520 million against a €400 million target, roughly three times its own predecessor. The predecessor, Unigestion's Emerging Manager Choice Fund II, closed at €176 million in 2023 and missed its €300 million target. So the same strategy, same team, went from undershooting to oversubscribed in three years. That reversal is the actual story, and it runs against everything else we've covered in this section for a month.

Here is the apparent contradiction. US experienced managers — fourth fund or later — took 88% of all capital raised in both 2024 and 2025, versus an 81% ten-year average. Specialists took 73.9% of 2025 capital against a 64.1% five-year norm. LPs have been visibly starving anyone without a fourth-fund track record and a DPI story. Yet capital is now flowing into a vehicle whose entire job is to find first- and second-time managers. Both things are true because Sagard's product isn't really a bet on emerging managers. It's a bet that LPs want emerging-manager exposure and have concluded they cannot underwrite it themselves.

Read what Paul Newsome actually said: identifying and evaluating these firms "requires significant resources and insight." That is a sales pitch built on LP capitulation. The institutions pulling back from direct emerging-manager commitments haven't decided the segment is dead — median lower-middle-market returns have historically beaten the megafunds, and everyone knows it. They've decided they can't afford the diligence headcount to source 125 spinouts and pick the three that compound. So they're outsourcing the selection and paying a second layer of fees to do it. Sagard intermediates the exact relationship the direct market can no longer support.

The structural point the triumphant press release skips: a fund-of-funds raising three times its prior vintage into a market where emerging managers are being defunded is not evidence the drought is ending. It's evidence the drought is being securitized. The capital still won't reach most first-time GPs — it reaches the handful Sagard's team blesses, through a gatekeeper with 500 GP relationships and the power to decide who gets a second fund. That consolidates sourcing power with the intermediary, not the managers. A spinout's survival now depends on clearing Sagard's screen as much as on performance.

For comparison, GCM Grosvenor's Elevate Fund closed near $800 million in 2025 seeding small buyout firms. Two large intermediaries with roughly $1.4 billion of combined dry powder are now the de facto draft board for the emerging-manager class. If you run one of those small funds, the economics just got worse — you give up a fee layer and you sell your independence to a platform that can cut you off at Fund III.

The thing worth watching is whether this capital converts to distributions or just recycles through late primaries and co-investments — note Sagard explicitly reserves the right to chase "shorter-duration opportunities" and "cash flow management." That's a fund-of-funds hedging its own liquidity, in a market where everyone upstream is short exactly that.

The other side

The case for this structure is straightforward: emerging and lower-middle-market managers have historically generated stronger returns than megafunds, and that edge doesn't disappear just because LPs lack the internal resources to access it directly. A specialist intermediary with 30 years of sourcing history and relationships across 500 GPs can identify spinouts and sector specialists earlier and more accurately than any single LP investment team could. If Sagard's track record of backing managers who later became established industry leaders holds, the second fee layer is simply the cost of accessing a return stream that would otherwise be unavailable — and the fund's oversubscription suggests institutional investors have done that math and accepted the trade.

Say more →

The predecessor fund's failure is the most important data point in this story and the one the press release buries fastest. Unigestion's Emerging Manager Choice Fund II closed at €176 million in 2023 against a €300 million target — a 41% shortfall. The same strategy, substantially the same team, then raised €520 million three years later. What changed was not the opportunity set. The emerging-manager drought that made Fund II a hard sell has only deepened: experienced managers took 88% of all US PE capital in 2024 and 2025, well above the 81% ten-year average. What changed was the merger. Unigestion's PE platform combined with Canada-based Sagard — a multi-strategy alternatives firm managing $47 billion across venture, private equity, private credit, and real estate — in April of this year, creating a platform with over $23 billion in private equity assets and connections to more than 300 institutional investors and 500 GPs. The fundraise didn't beat its target because the market turned. It beat its target because the distribution network got dramatically larger.

That matters for how you read the capital flows. The €275 million first close was held by Unigestion before the merger completed, meaning a substantial portion of the fund was already committed under the old brand. The post-merger close added the remaining capital, and the oversubscription reflects Sagard's LP base layering in on top of Unigestion's existing relationships. This is as much a story about platform consolidation as it is about emerging-manager appetite.

The fund's mandate also deserves scrutiny. Alongside primary commitments to emerging managers, Sagard explicitly reserves the right to pursue co-investments, late primaries, and what it calls shorter-duration opportunities to support cash flow management. In plain terms: a fund-of-funds whose core pitch is patient, early-stage manager selection is also hedging its own liquidity by chasing faster-returning deal structures. That is a rational response to the broader liquidity squeeze — distributions across private equity remain well below historical averages, and LPs everywhere are managing cash flow carefully — but it means the fund's actual exposure to true first-time managers may be more limited than the headline suggests. Capital allocated to late primaries and co-investments alongside established managers is not the same as backing a spinout's debut fund.

The consolidation dynamic at the intermediary level is worth tracking closely. With Sagard's vehicle and GCM Grosvenor's Elevate Fund together controlling roughly $1.4 billion in dedicated emerging-manager capital, the sourcing power for this segment is concentrating in a small number of platforms. A first-time GP that cannot clear one of these screens faces a market where the direct LP channel has largely closed and the intermediary channel is controlled by two or three gatekeepers. That shifts negotiating leverage toward the fund-of-funds and away from the managers themselves — on fees, on governance, and on the decision of whether a manager gets a second fund at all. Sagard's own language acknowledges this implicitly: the firm describes its role as identifying 'the next generation of lower middle-market private equity leaders,' which is another way of saying it decides who gets to become one.

Financial Markets & Economic News Rates AI Treasury Credit

Treasury's funding plan has an AI drawdown baked in as a feature

home.treasury.gov
  • Ten-year yields peaked near 5.4% and thirty-year near 5.7% this quarter, with the Fed's median dot now at 4.1% through both year-end 2026 and year-end 2027
  • The TBAC's first formal charge this quarter was 'Super Intelligence-related issuance' — the AI financing binge — and the committee found no evidence it has crowded out Treasury auction demand, calling recent results 'generally resilient'
  • Treasury's projected cash balance targets climb to roughly $850 billion by end of Q1 FY27 and $1 trillion by end of Q2 FY27, against a funding gap the TBAC says widens further in FY28 and FY29

The take

The TBAC just put a name on the thing the Fed and Treasury have spent a year not naming. In its quarterly report to the Secretary, the Committee's first formal charge was "Super Intelligence (SI)-related issuance" — the borrowing binge funding the AI buildout — and the headline conclusion is reassuring: no evidence that 2026 technology issuance has crowded out long-end Treasury demand, auctions "generally resilient," households and pensions under-allocated to fixed income and plenty of capacity to absorb more. Sleep well.

Read what they actually conceded on the way to that conclusion. Financing is "expanding beyond long-dated corporate bonds," with "secured and asset-backed structures increasingly bringing issuance inside 10 years." Translation: the AI complex is moving from unsecured 30-year paper to GPU-backed loans, equipment leases, and data-center project finance. That is not a sign of strength. That is what issuers do when the long end gets expensive and skeptical — they pledge the hardware and shorten the tenor to keep the money flowing. The committee advising Treasury on how to fund a widening deficit is telling you the private sector is now competing for the same short-and-intermediate duration bucket Treasury itself leans on, while quietly securitizing depreciating silicon that's on a two-to-three-year obsolescence clock.

Now stack the macro the same report lays out. Ten-year peaked near 5.4%, thirty-year near 5.7%, headline CPI at 3.7% with gasoline doing more than half the work, Warsh hiking into it and the dots sitting at 4.1% through 2027. Three more hikes still priced. The exact rate regime in which project-finance structures underwritten on 2024 assumptions stop penciling. The TBAC even narrates the warning shot: by late October the Nasdaq 100 was off just over 7% from its high amid "greater scrutiny of the returns on increasingly debt-financed technology investment," and that equity wobble is precisely what pulled the safe-haven bid back into Treasuries and capped yields. So the official "no crowding out" verdict partly rests on AI stocks selling off hard enough to send money running into bonds. That's not a clean bill of health. That's one asset bubble temporarily subsidizing the government's funding costs.

The structural point the soothing language buries: Treasury's own funding gap "widens over FY27 and increases further in FY28 and FY29," with cash balance targets climbing toward $1 trillion by Q2 FY27. Treasury needs the long end calm and the buyer base deep right when the AI complex is issuing its own paper into the same pool and shifting toward secured structures that will, when GPU residual values disappoint, generate forced selling and spread-widening that transmits straight into rates. The transmission channel the TBAC waves away as "stronger growth" is the same channel that reverses violently if the growth was actually capex pulled forward on debt priced at rates that no longer exist.

The one line worth underlining: "even modest reallocations from cash and equities could absorb a meaningful share of projected borrowing." They're counting on equity holders rotating into Treasuries to fund the deficit. That only works if equities keep falling. Treasury's funding plan has an AI drawdown baked into it as a feature.

The other side

The TBAC's 'no crowding out' verdict isn't just spin — the underlying demand picture is genuinely more diversified than the bear case assumes. Foreign investors accelerated purchases of U.S. fixed-income assets this year, and the committee is correct that households and private pensions remain structurally underweight bonds relative to historical norms, meaning even a modest portfolio rebalance represents a large absolute dollar flow into Treasuries. If the AI buildout is genuinely lifting productivity and long-run growth — and the data-center and computing investment surge is showing up in real output, not just asset prices — then higher equilibrium rates and a wider deficit may be the right price for a structurally faster economy, and the crowding-out concern dissolves.

Say more →

The TBAC report is careful about what it does and doesn't claim. It does not say AI-related issuance has no effect on rates — it says the transmission runs through stronger growth expectations rather than through direct competition for Treasury auction demand. That is a meaningful distinction, but it is also the most favorable possible framing of the same underlying dynamic. When private-sector borrowers are large enough and active enough to shift growth expectations, they are by definition affecting the rate environment Treasury must issue into. Calling that 'growth' rather than 'crowding out' is accurate in a narrow technical sense and misleading in every practical one.

The shift in financing structure is the detail that deserves more attention than the report gives it. The move from unsecured long-dated corporate bonds toward GPU-backed loans, equipment leases, and data-center project finance is not a sign that the AI complex has found cheaper, more efficient capital. It is a sign that the long end of the credit market has become expensive enough and skeptical enough that issuers are pledging physical collateral to keep the money moving. Project-finance and asset-backed structures require lenders to underwrite residual values — in this case, the value of GPU clusters and data-center infrastructure at the end of a loan term. Those underwriting assumptions were built in a rate environment that no longer exists, and the useful life of current-generation AI hardware is genuinely uncertain. A two-to-three-year obsolescence cycle on the collateral backing multi-year secured loans is not a theoretical risk; it is the central tension in every GPU-backed deal being written right now.

The report's own timeline makes the vulnerability concrete. The Nasdaq 100 fell just over 7% from its early-October high as investors began applying greater scrutiny to the returns on debt-financed technology investment. That equity pullback, the TBAC notes, helped restore safe-haven demand for Treasuries and partially reversed the earlier yield spike. So the mechanism keeping Treasury auctions orderly in the near term is AI equity weakness driving flows into government bonds — a dynamic that requires ongoing deterioration in the asset class to sustain itself. A stabilization or recovery in tech equities removes that bid precisely when Treasury's issuance calendar is accelerating.

The energy dimension compounds the picture. Headline CPI ran at 3.7% in September, with gasoline accounting for more than half of the monthly increase. The TBAC describes supply disruptions that began over the summer as more persistent than initially expected, feeding into transportation and freight costs and keeping core CPI at 2.6%. The Fed's response — a 25 basis point hike in September to 3.75–4.00%, with the median dot at 4.1% through 2027 — was explicitly framed by Chairman Warsh as removing accommodation rather than imposing restriction. Markets are still pricing roughly three additional increases over the coming year. Every one of those hikes raises the hurdle rate against which leveraged data-center and infrastructure deals must perform, and raises the probability that the secured structures being written today become the distressed paper of 2028.

The French sovereign spread widening the report mentions in passing is a useful parallel. Fiscal and political uncertainty in France produced sharp moves in European government bonds, and those linkages transmitted into U.S. rates volatility even as the correlation eventually weakened. The TBAC frames this as an external risk that ultimately supported safe-haven Treasury demand. But the same logic applies domestically: if the AI credit complex experiences a disorderly repricing — forced selling of GPU-backed paper, spread-widening in data-center project finance — the transmission into intermediate Treasury rates would be direct and fast, hitting exactly the duration bucket Treasury is counting on to fund a deficit that by the committee's own projection keeps widening through FY29.

Tech & AI Data Centers Energy Hyperscalers

Hyperscalers routed around the grid queue and handed themselves a 25-year energy liability instead.

trendforce.com
  • Three-quarters of October's +4,170 MW of newly tracked capacity sits on self-supplied campuses — next to dedicated gas or wind generation rather than waiting for utility interconnection
  • TrendForce cut its 2027 forecast for Vantage's $15B Lighthouse campus in Port Washington, Wisconsin from 650 MW to 310 MW after Wisconsin regulators closed ATC's transmission application in September
  • ByteDance's first South American campus in Ceará, Brazil — 200 MW contracted, 300 MW peak draw — is underwritten by a 20-year, roughly $2 billion wind supply deal, with first capacity forecast for 2027

The take

TrendForce's satellite tracker caught something the project-finance debate from last week hasn't absorbed: the hyperscalers have quietly stopped waiting for the grid. Three-quarters of October's +4,170 MW of new tracked capacity sits on campuses built next to their own power — Google's Tembo in Wyoming on new Black Hills gas, AWS's Homer City in Pennsylvania wrapped around a 4.5 GW gas plant on an old coal site, AWS in Greece moving onto a retired lignite station. The pattern isn't a one-off. It's becoming the whole siting logic.

This reframes the leverage problem we've been circling since the Brookings 3.63%-of-GDP number. The bear case assumed the binding constraint was the 2,061 GW sitting in interconnection queues — chips financed today, power in 2028, interest accruing in the gap. The operators heard that and routed around it. Self-supply means they no longer have to clear a multi-year utility queue to light up a campus, which is genuinely bullish for how fast revenue-generating compute arrives.

But look at what they're spending to get there, and the collateral quality of this buildout gets worse, not better. Vantage's $15 billion Lighthouse campus for Oracle and OpenAI has two of four buildings enclosed and the power still stuck in Wisconsin regulatory review — TrendForce just cut its 2027 forecast there from 650 MW to 310 MW while the steel sits finished. Enclosed buildings before approved power is the physical signature of capital committed ahead of certainty. Meanwhile Meta is throwing fabric "tents" — rapid-deploy fabric-roofed halls — onto a legacy Iowa campus it's run since 2014, precisely because it already has power and fiber there and can skip rezoning. Compute now appears with no construction signal at all.

That's the part the lenders asking tougher covenant questions should sit with. A gas-plant-plus-datacenter campus isn't a data center with a power contract; it's a merchant generation asset bolted to a depreciating compute asset, financed as if it were real estate. When AWS builds Homer City around its own 4.5 GW plant, the hyperscaler now owns the fuel-price risk, the environmental-permitting risk, and the 25-year generation asset — against GPUs that are worth half as much in four years and token pricing that Nvidia, Mistral, and the Chinese open-weight labs are actively compressing, as this section has tracked for a month.

So the buildout got faster and the underlying obligations got heavier and longer-dated at the same time. The grid queue was a delay. Self-built gas is a balance sheet. ByteDance's Brazil campus runs on a 20-year, ~$2 billion wind deal with Casa dos Ventos for 300 MW — a two-decade fixed commitment underwriting compute whose useful life is measured in single-digit years.

The speed is real and I'd stop shorting it. The question worth underwriting is who holds the stranded power asset when the revenue curve the lenders are already worried about still doesn't cross the debt-service line — and that counterparty is now the operator itself, not the utility.

The other side

The self-supply pivot may actually improve the credit picture rather than worsen it. A hyperscaler that owns its generation eliminates the single biggest scheduling risk in the old model — the multi-year utility queue — and can match power delivery precisely to compute demand rather than waiting on a grid operator's timeline. AWS, Google, and Meta have balance sheets large enough to absorb a generation asset that a pure-play data center developer could not, and owning the power stack gives them cost certainty that a market-rate grid connection never would. If token demand grows into the capacity, the 25-year wind or gas commitment looks less like a stranded liability and more like a below-market hedge.

Say more →

The structural shift TrendForce is documenting is worth separating into two distinct phenomena, because they have different risk profiles even though both show up as accelerating capacity.

The first is the planned self-supply model: Google's Tembo campus in Wyoming paired with new Black Hills Energy gas generation, AWS's Homer City in Pennsylvania built around a 4.5 GW plant on a former coal site, AWS in Greece occupying a retired lignite station at Agios Dimitrios. These are deliberate co-location decisions where the power asset and the compute asset are conceived together. The capital commitment is large and long-dated by design — ByteDance's Brazil campus illustrates the extreme version, with a 20-year wind contract underwriting a facility TrendForce forecasts at 150 MW in 2027, rising to 300 MW in 2029. The useful life of the GPUs inside that building will be measured in years; the wind contract runs to roughly 2046.

The second phenomenon is Meta's tent strategy, and it operates on entirely different logic. Meta's fabric-roofed rapid-deployment halls — first associated with flagship AI sites like its Prometheus campus in Ohio — are now appearing at legacy campuses the company has operated for over a decade. At Altoona, Iowa, a campus Meta has run since 2014, a roughly 280-metre tent structure was standing by May 2025, and a second parallel structure appeared by August 2026. The reason is straightforward: Altoona already has land, power, and fiber. Meta can add compute capacity without rezoning, without a new building shell, and without triggering the construction signals that normally give observers — and lenders — advance notice. Capacity appears, essentially, overnight.

Those two dynamics together mean the buildout is simultaneously faster and less legible than the project-finance models assumed. The interconnection queue problem that Van Nieuwerburgh's Brookings analysis identified — roughly 2,061 GW waiting, chips financed today, power potentially not arriving until 2028 — was a delay problem. Operators have found two ways around it: own the generation outright, or use existing power at existing campuses. Both solutions compress the gap between capital commitment and revenue-generating compute.

But the Lighthouse campus in Wisconsin shows that self-supply is not a clean solution. Vantage's $15 billion campus for Oracle and OpenAI has two of four data center buildings enclosed. The power infrastructure — more than $2 billion in new ATC transmission lines and five substations — is still in regulatory review after ATC filed 564 additional application documents in September, prompting Wisconsin regulators to close the application. Oracle still guides to operation in the second half of 2027, but TrendForce's revised forecast puts only 310 MW online that year, against a prior estimate of 650 MW. The buildings are finished. The electrons are not approved. That sequence — steel committed before power is certain — is what happens when construction timelines and regulatory timelines run in parallel rather than in series, and it is the physical expression of capital deployed ahead of certainty.

AWS's October additions illustrate how broadly the geographic diversification is running alongside the power diversification. The company added 911 MW across five new campuses in four countries — Pennsylvania, Virginia, Greece, New South Wales, and Germany — with nearly half the capacity sitting outside the US. All five remain at the permitting stage, with first capacity forecast between 2029 and 2030. The diversification is real, but the lead times are long, and every one of those campuses carries the same basic structure: a large capital commitment made years before the asset generates revenue.

The lender concern this section flagged two weeks ago was about whether hyperscaler and startup AI revenue would arrive on the schedule the debt-service models assumed. The self-supply pivot answers the grid-queue version of that concern. It does not answer the revenue version. And it adds a new variable: the operator now holds the generation asset, with its own fuel-price exposure, environmental-permitting risk, and depreciation schedule, stacked against compute hardware whose value compresses rapidly and token pricing that open-weight competition is actively pushing down.

Geopolitics China Taiwan Semiconductors Trade

Democrats swept Congress and the Taiwan risk just got structurally more volatile, not less.

cnbc.com
  • Trump is booked to meet Xi twice before January — Shenzhen this month, Miami next — as a self-described legacy-focused lame duck with no party debt to pay
  • Democrats' stated top priority is removing Trump's tariffs to fight domestic inflation, a direct de-escalation on the trade front that actually moves goods
  • CSIS senior advisor Scott Kennedy says congressional hawkishness will be 'constrained by the executive branch's wide range of latitude and China's growing power and leverage'

The take

The Democrats swept the House, the Senate, and governorships in Texas, Nevada and Iowa, and the immediate read from Beijing-watchers is that this hardens the tech cold war and puts Taiwan back in play. That's half right and misses the structural shift entirely.

The important fact in the CNBC piece is buried in a Teneo line: Trump is now a lame duck optimizing for legacy over the Republican Party's 2028 prospects. That changes the incentive on China more than a Democratic Congress does. A president who no longer owes anything to his own party's hawks, who is already booked to meet Xi twice before January — Shenzhen this month, Miami next — is a president building a deal, not a confrontation. The Democrats inherit a China policy they can shout at through hearings but cannot author, because, as CSIS's Scott Kennedy says plainly, the executive holds the latitude and China holds the rare-earth leverage. Congressional China hawkishness without executive buy-in is a press release.

The other side

The executive-dominates-foreign-policy argument has real limits. Congress controls the authorization and appropriations levers that fund Taiwan arms sales, and a Democratic majority motivated to prove it isn't soft on China could attach Taiwan Security Assistance provisions to must-pass spending bills that Trump cannot simply pocket-veto without a political cost. Export controls are also not as toothless as the routing-around narrative suggests — each new round of entity-list additions raises the cost and delay of Chinese firms accessing frontier chips, and cumulative friction compounds even if no single rule is decisive.

The collision that matters is not a new chip rule or a hearing on Huawei. It is the timing overlap between Trump's two legacy summits and the incentives of a newly empowered Democratic Speaker. Hakeem Jeffries, who met then-Taiwan President Tsai Ing-wen in New York in 2023, is now poised to become the first Black Speaker of the House. Nancy Pelosi's 2022 trip to Taiwan — which triggered Chinese military exercises around the island and a sustained diplomatic freeze — is the institutional template a Democratic Congress has already demonstrated it is willing to use. A high-profile congressional gesture toward Taipei during the exact weeks Trump is trying to close a legacy summit in Miami is not a hypothetical; it is the structurally incentivized move for a new majority that wants to establish its foreign policy credibility fast.

That warning was delivered to a president who, at the time, still had a cooperative Republican Congress. The warning is now addressed to a president whose own legislative branch is controlled by the opposition and has its own reasons to make noise toward Taipei.

The semiconductor exposure here is specific. TSMC — the Taiwan-based manufacturer that produces the majority of Nvidia's advanced chips — sits on the island at the center of this political geometry. Any episode that raises the perceived risk of a Taiwan strait incident touches the supply chain for the chips that underpin the entire AI investment thesis.
