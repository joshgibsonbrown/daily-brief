Private Markets China Asia Secondaries GP-Led

Noveon Capital is buying China's best private assets at 40-50% discounts because Western buyers have left the room.

pitchbook.com, pei-secondariesinvestor.com
  • 95% of LP-led secondary volume in the first half of 2026 went to North America- and Europe-focused funds, per Jefferies — leaving Asian assets structurally undersubscribed
  • Asian fund tails average 11-15 years versus 5-7 in North America, meaning a 30% discount on a China stake can be a worse annualized return than a 13% discount on a US book
  • APAC GP-led secondary volume reached $4.9 billion through October 2026 — less than the single Ares credit continuation vehicle covered in this brief two days ago

The take

Two ex-buyout dealmakers — Sung Ko from Carlyle Asia, Chris Shen from Warburg Pincus — launched Noveon Capital this week to do pan-Asian secondaries, with three China deals already done off their own balance sheet. The headline is the arbitrage: they're buying aged fund stakes at 40-50% discounts to mark, into assets like Xiaohongshu and ByteDance, in a market where 95% of first-half LP-led volume went to North America and Europe. Western buyers won't touch China. That leaves the discounts for whoever will.

The deeper story is what the numbers say about the exit machine in Asia versus everything we've been tracking in the US. Ko's own framing is the point: North American fund tails run 5-7 years, Asian tails run 11-15. It takes eleven years to reach 1x DPI — to return the original capital — in China. So a 30% discount that looks juicy on a screen is a mirage if you're holding another four years to realize it. The real pricing variable isn't the discount, it's the clock. That's why a 40-50% headline discount in China can be a worse annualized deal than a 13% discount on a US book, and why the "cheap China" pitch most allocators have heard is half-wrong.

What Noveon is actually underwriting is the GP's reluctance to sell. The structural insight is that China's best USD managers — Hongshan with ByteDance, Centurium with Luckin, CITIC with McDonald's China — don't want to exit their trophies at all. The continuation vehicle solves that: an old fund unlikely to hit carry gets rolled into a new one where the GP earns 1% on NAV instead of cost, plus 10% carry on fresh upside. That's a strong enough incentive that the supply of CVs in China isn't a liquidity accident — it's a fee reset dressed as a liquidity solution, and it only works for the GP if a buyer like Noveon shows up to price the LP side.

Here's the part the discount-chasers miss. We've spent three weeks documenting the US CV problem: stale marks, buyers flush with a record $139 billion of dry powder compressing discipline, 21 of 56 recent secondaries signals being GP-leds. The Asia version is the inverse risk. There's barely any competition — APAC GP-led volume is running around $5.9 billion for the whole year, less than a single Ares credit CV we covered — so pricing power sits with the buyer, not the seller. Noveon's edge isn't access to discounts. It's that the auction process is so thin for China that going direct to a US endowment holding a 15-year-old stake nobody else will bid on means you set the price.

The vulnerability is the one Ko names and then waves past: exit certainty. Buying ByteDance at 8x against Facebook's 16x is only arbitrage if the exit path — Hong Kong listing, strategic sale, geopolitics permitting — actually opens. Underwrite the asset, fine. But the discount is compensation for an exit timeline no one controls.

The other side

The discount is wide for a reason that doesn't disappear just because Noveon is willing to accept it. Exit paths for China-based private assets — Hong Kong listings, strategic sales, any route that actually returns cash — remain hostage to geopolitics and a domestic capital markets environment that has been unreliable since 2021. Buying ByteDance at 8x against Facebook's 16x is a compelling spread on paper, but the spread only converts if the exit window opens on a timeline the buyer can model. Noveon's own framing — that it took eleven years to reach 1x DPI in China — is also an argument for why the discount should be wider than 40-50%, not a reason to be comfortable with it.

The structural setup Noveon is entering is almost the mirror image of what has been playing out in US secondaries. In North America, a record $139 billion of dry powder raised by secondary buyers last year has compressed pricing discipline — 21 of 56 secondaries signals tracked recently were GP-led continuation vehicles, and the competition among buyers is intense enough that sellers can run real auctions. In Asia, and particularly in China, the auction process barely exists. Ko's description of the dynamic is precise: US endowments and foundations that invested in China funds a decade ago put those stakes into auction processes and find almost no bidders, because the mainstream secondary buyers don't want the China exposure. The result is that going direct to a seller holding a 15-year-old stake produces a negotiation, not a competitive process, and the buyer sets the terms.

The LP base Noveon is targeting reflects how thoroughly the original investor community has rotated. Historically, US endowments, foundations and family offices were the dominant LP base for Chinese private equity. Many of those positions are now more than ten years old, the funds are effectively past their intended life, and the geopolitical pressure to reduce China exposure has added a non-economic motivation to sell. Ko notes that Middle Eastern LPs are stepping in as buyers of these same positions — using the secondary market as a way to gain China exposure without committing to primary funds. Noveon sits at that handoff point.

The continuation vehicle mechanics matter here because they explain why supply will keep coming. China's best USD-denominated managers — the source material names Hongshan Capital with ByteDance, Centurium Capital with Luckin Coffee, and CITIC Capital with McDonald's China — raised their last meaningful US dollar funds in 2019 and 2020. Those funds need to show distributions to have any chance of raising again. But the trophy assets inside them are exactly what the GPs want to keep compounding. A continuation vehicle resolves that tension: the old fund, which may never hit the carry threshold, gets restructured into a new vehicle where the GP earns a management fee on current NAV rather than on original cost, plus carry on value created from the reset price. That is a meaningful economic improvement for the GP regardless of what it does for the LP, which is why Ko frames it as a fee reset as much as a liquidity solution.

Noveon's check size — $5 million to $50 million — and its focus on funds where a single asset represents more than 50% of exposure is a deliberate constraint. The firm is not trying to buy diversified Asian PE portfolios at a discount; it is trying to underwrite specific companies it has already decided it wants to own, then find the secondary route that gets it there. Shen's description of the process is worth noting: the firm tracks a list of private companies in Asia it wants to own, waits until the underlying fund is four or five years old, and then begins conversations with the GP. That is a primary investor's sourcing discipline applied to a secondary market where most buyers are waiting for sellers to come to them.

The $60 billion raised across Asia in the past year, with roughly 85% going to just three large managers — Bain Capital, EQT and Blackstone — is the supply-side pressure that makes the rest of this work. The 1,300 smaller managers sharing the remaining 15% are the ones with aged funds, concentrated positions, and no realistic path to a new primary raise without first demonstrating they can return capital. That is Noveon's pipeline.

ICONIQ Portfolio Companies AI IPO Venture Capital

Anthropic lists Thursday and takes five years of venture returns with it.

cnbc.com
  • Anthropic's IPO order book is seven times oversubscribed, with pricing expected above the $70–$78 range and a target valuation of $1.4 trillion — down from the $2 trillion figure bankers floated weeks ago.
  • Altimeter Capital's Brad Gerstner is committing $5 billion to the deal, five times his largest prior IPO investment, joined by AMD, Broadcom, Nvidia, TSMC, Micron, Google, and Microsoft.
  • Venture funds returned 17.8% in the first half of 2026 after a five-year stretch that returned just 6.19% total — a rebound almost entirely explained by AI marks that Anthropic's aftermarket performance will now either validate or unwind.

The take

Anthropic prices tonight and lists Thursday on the Nasdaq under "ANT," and the number everyone is watching is $1.4 trillion — but that's the wrong number to watch. The order book is seven times oversubscribed, the range may get pushed above $70–$78, and Altimeter's Brad Gerstner is putting in $5 billion, five times his largest prior IPO bet. This is going to price well. The question is what prices alongside it.

Here is the structural bet being made this week, and it goes far beyond one company. CNBC's own framing says it plainly: 86% of US venture dollars in the first half of 2026 — $355.9 billion — went into AI. MSCI says the ten largest holdings now make up 22% of its entire venture index. Venture funds returned 17.8% in six months, a pace that annualizes near 38%, after a five-year stretch that returned 6.19% total. Strip out the AI marks and that rebound evaporates. The asset class didn't get healthy; it got concentrated into a handful of pre-IPO names whose paper value is now about to be tested in public for the first time at scale.

That's why the realization story matters more than the headline valuation. The $350 billion that flowed in is only worth 38% if the exits clear. SpaceX already proved the top of the market can absorb a mega-float — $85.7 billion raised five months ago. Anthropic is the second data point, and unlike SpaceX it carries numbers that cut both ways: $33.24 billion in nine-month revenue, up roughly 1,200%, against a $23.3 billion quarterly net loss and, per its own amended S-1, aggregate infrastructure commitments near $895 billion with $69.8 billion due inside twelve months. The market is being asked to fund that gap voluntarily, in size, into a tape that just voted against data centers in the midterms and is watching the Nvidia CEO scandal spread through the financing plumbing.

So the real event Thursday isn't whether ANT pops. It's the price discovery that follows for everything held behind it. If Anthropic trades well and holds, every late-stage AI mark in every venture fund gets validated, the secondary bids firm up, and the 38% annualized pace looks defensible for another two quarters. If it prices high and fades in the aftermarket, the repricing runs backwards through the entire index — and the funds carrying those 22%-of-index holdings mark down in a correlated move, because they all own variations of the same trade.

Watch who is buying the discipline. Opto's Jake Miller is explicitly rotating out of late-stage and into Seed through Series B, hunting 10-to-20x names at low entry marks. That is the honest read on this moment: the people closest to the data are no longer paying up for the compounder story at the top. They're buying the next cohort cheap and letting the public market stress-test the current one. If you hold AI exposure through venture funds, your portfolio's second-half 2026 mark is being written this Thursday, whether your GP admits it or not.

The other side

The bear case on concentration risk assumes the marks are fiction waiting to be corrected, but Anthropic's underlying revenue growth — $33.24 billion for the nine months ended September 30, up roughly 1,200% year-over-year — is not fictional. An asset generating that kind of top-line velocity at enterprise scale, with more than half of Fortune 500 companies already using its products, has a credible claim on a premium multiple. If the public market absorbs Anthropic at or above $1.4 trillion and the stock holds, the concentration argument inverts: funds that were heavily exposed to the right handful of names will have been proven right, and the critics will look like people who confused risk with outcome.

The structural context here is easy to understate. AI captured $355.9 billion — 86% of all US venture dollars — in just the first half of 2026, according to Clearstead's analysis of PitchBook data. Globally, AI deal value hit $521.4 billion over the trailing twelve months, more than double the prior year, even as deal count fell 21.6% from the previous quarter. That last detail matters: fewer deals, more money, higher average check sizes. The distribution of bets is narrowing at exactly the moment the first major exits are arriving to price them.

The comparison point the source material offers is SpaceX, which raised $85.7 billion in a public offering roughly five months ago. That deal proved the top of the market could absorb a mega-float. Anthropic is the second test, and it is structurally different. SpaceX had a defense and launch revenue base that was legible to generalist investors. Anthropic is asking the public market to fund a company with $895 billion in aggregate infrastructure commitments — per its own amended S-1 — against a business that swung from a $974 million operating profit in Q2 to a $536 million operating loss in Q3, driven by a $5 billion emergency compute charge. The nine-month revenue figure of $33.24 billion is real and the growth rate is extraordinary, but the loss in Q3 alone was $23.3 billion, largely from non-cash accounting charges tied to the company's rising valuation. Public investors are being asked to hold both of those facts simultaneously and decide which one dominates.

The midterm election backdrop adds a layer that is easy to dismiss and shouldn't be. Widespread public opposition to data centers showed up in voting patterns, and that political signal feeds directly into permitting risk for the infrastructure buildout that underpins Anthropic's compute commitments. Private infrastructure funds returned 4.2% in the first half of 2026 — annualizing near 8.5%, second-best among major private fund categories — but MSCI's own data shows the strongest returns have been concentrated in AI-ready and liquid-cooled data centers. If permitting tightens, the infrastructure return story moderates, and the compute capacity Anthropic has committed to paying for becomes harder and more expensive to actually build.

The venture return math is where the stakes become systemic rather than company-specific. PitchBook puts the asset class's ten-year average annual return at 13%. The 17.8% six-month return in 2026 — annualizing near 38% if sustained — is not a mean-reversion story; it is an AI-concentration story. MSCI reports the ten largest holdings now represent 22% of its entire venture index. When a single IPO can move 22% of an index's value through its aftermarket performance, the index has stopped being a diversified measure of startup outcomes and started being a leveraged expression of one theme. That is not inherently wrong, but it means the risk is correlated in a way that most LP quarterly reports are not yet reflecting. PitchBook's Juan Mier has already flagged that venture returns are expected to moderate in Q3 as public market AI valuations adjust — and that adjustment flows into private marks on a lag. Thursday's open is the moment the lag closes.

Tech & AI Microsoft OpenAI Enterprise AI Cloud

Microsoft built the cheap substitute and owns the store where OpenAI sells.

axios.com
  • MAI Code 2 claims 96% of Claude Opus 5.5's performance at 5% of the cost, and MAI Cyber 2 Flash claims to beat GPT-6 Astra on a security benchmark at under 2% of cost per task.
  • The model weights are licensable but Azure-only — framed by Suleyman as feeling 'like open source' while keeping deployment captive to Microsoft's cloud infrastructure.
  • Microsoft is simultaneously pursuing a frontier general-purpose model, with Suleyman claiming state-of-the-art pre-training results but declining to give a release timeline.

The take

Microsoft used its Ignite stage to announce six specialized models — MAI Code 2, MAI Cyber 2 Flash, MAI Image 2.7, plus transcription and voice — and the headline numbers are a direct shot at its own partner. MAI Code 2 claims 96% of Claude Opus 5.5's performance at 5% of the cost, and MAI Cyber 2 Flash claims to beat GPT-6 Astra on a security benchmark at under 2% of cost per task. Suleyman is routing MAI Code 2 into GitHub Copilot.

The pricing claims are the least interesting part, and Microsoft itself tells you why in the fine print: the comparisons rely on selected benchmarks and some of the cost advantage is promotional discounting. We have watched this exact pattern for a month now — Inkling, Mistral Large 4, Nemotron, Jev. Cheap specialized models eat volume, frontier models hold price on the hard tasks. Microsoft is just the largest player yet to say the quiet thing out loud: for everyday coding, the frontier premium isn't worth paying.

What actually matters is where the model runs. The weights are licensable, deployable only inside Azure, and Suleyman describes the experience as feeling "like open source" with more control. That phrasing is doing a lot of work. This is not open weights — it is a captive distribution play dressed in open-source language. Microsoft spent years paying OpenAI for intelligence and renting out the pipes. Now it wants to own both the model and the pipe, and it is using Copilot — a product with existing enterprise seat distribution nobody else can match — as the on-ramp.

That reframes the OpenAI relationship. Microsoft committed tens of billions to OpenAI and sells its models through Azure. Every point of Copilot traffic that moves to MAI Code 2 is inference revenue that stops flowing to OpenAI and starts flowing to Microsoft's own margin. Suleyman declined to give a migration target, which is the answer. You don't announce a number when the number is "as much as we can get away with before the next renegotiation."

The part worth watching is the general-purpose model Suleyman keeps gesturing at — "my team is building true superintelligence" — with no timeline and no benchmark. Specialized models are a hedge, not a destination. Microsoft is telling enterprise buyers they can get 95% of the capability at 5% of the cost today, while telling investors it will match the frontier tomorrow. Those two stories only both come true if the frontier itself stops mattering commercially, which is precisely the bet the cheap-model wave has been making since October.

Here's who absorbs the damage: OpenAI and Anthropic priced their capital structures — Anthropic against a $125 billion Broadcom chip lease, OpenAI against its compute commitments — on the assumption that frontier tokens stay expensive. Their largest distribution partner just started selling the cheap substitute through the channel they were counting on to move volume. The customer became the competitor, and he owns the store.

The other side

Microsoft's benchmark comparisons are self-selected and some of the pricing advantage is promotional — meaning the real-world cost gap may be narrower than the headline numbers suggest once discounts expire. More fundamentally, enterprise buyers who have already built workflows around GPT-6 or Claude face real switching costs, and OpenAI and Anthropic have their own roadmaps: OpenAI announced GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol on the same day, with Astra priced 20% below its prior version, showing the frontier is not standing still while Microsoft catches up.

The six models Microsoft announced at Ignite — MAI Code 2, MAI Code 2 Flash, MAI Cyber 2 Flash, MAI Image 2.7, plus transcription and real-time voice — are not a product launch so much as a structural declaration. Microsoft is telling the market that task-specific models can match frontier performance on defined workloads at a fraction of the price, and it is backing that claim with a distribution channel — GitHub Copilot — that OpenAI and Anthropic cannot replicate. Copilot already sits inside enterprise developer environments at scale. Routing MAI Code 2 through it does not require a new sales motion; it requires a configuration change.

The mechanism Suleyman describes is straightforward: concentrate more training tokens and domain-specific knowledge into a fixed parameter budget, and you get a model that outperforms a larger general-purpose model on the narrow task it was built for. The trade-off he acknowledges openly is that the same model will underperform on advanced math or scientific research. That is an acceptable trade for the majority of Copilot use cases, which are everyday coding completions, not theorem proving.

This pattern has been building for roughly a month in the brief's coverage. Inkling 2 Small beat Claude Sonnet 5 on enterprise workflow benchmarks at $0.30 per million input tokens. Mistral Large 4 dropped open weights and claimed the top spot on agentic coding benchmarks. Nvidia released Nemotron 3.5 Super VL for free, converting inference spend into GPU sales. Jev reached 35% of Vercel's paid teams in two weeks by being deliberately less capable than what it replaced. Microsoft is the largest and most strategically positioned player to make the same move, and it is making it through a product that already has the enterprise relationships.

The Azure-only deployment requirement is the part that deserves more scrutiny than it has received. Suleyman's 'feels like open source' framing is a precise piece of positioning: it signals the flexibility and control that enterprise buyers associate with open weights, while the actual constraint — weights are licensed, not released, and must run inside Azure — keeps the compute revenue inside Microsoft. A genuinely open-weight release, like Mistral Large 4 on Hugging Face, lets customers run inference anywhere. This does not. The customer gets customization rights; Microsoft keeps the meter running.

The general-purpose frontier model Suleyman keeps referencing without dating is the strategic hedge underneath all of this. If specialized models are sufficient for most commercial workloads, Microsoft wins on price and distribution today. If the frontier keeps mattering — for reasoning-heavy agentic tasks, for anything customer-facing where error rates carry legal exposure — Microsoft needs to be there too. Claiming state-of-the-art pre-training results without a timeline is a way of keeping that option open without committing to a delivery date that could embarrass the company if OpenAI or Anthropic moves first.

The OpenAI relationship is the unresolved tension at the center of all of this. Microsoft committed tens of billions to OpenAI and built Azure AI services around OpenAI's models. Every Copilot seat that migrates to MAI Code 2 is inference revenue that stops flowing to OpenAI. Suleyman's refusal to name a migration target is not modesty — it is the correct answer when the honest number would accelerate a renegotiation you are not ready to have publicly.

Family Office & Wealth Planning Estate Tax Tax Policy Trusts

The estate tax exemption is permanent. The tools that make it useful are not.

wealthmanagement.com
  • Democrats chair both the House Ways and Means Committee and the Senate Finance Committee starting January 3, 2027, setting the tax agenda through 2028 even without passing a single bill
  • The IRS has indexed the exemption to $15.48 million per person for 2027 — nearly $31 million for a married couple — but anti-clawback protection only attaches to gifts that are clean and complete, not transfers with retained interests
  • Sen. Warren's Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act, reintroduced with more than 45 co-sponsors, targets trusts as well as households, meaning assets moved into an irrevocable trust would not automatically escape the tax base if the bill ever passed

The take

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act stamped the $15 million estate and gift exemption "permanent," and the IRS just indexed it to $15.48 million per person for 2027 — nearly $31 million for a married couple, with the annual gift exclusion rising to $20,000. The midterms flipped the agenda-setting committees: come January 3, Democrats chair both Ways and Means and Senate Finance, with Trump holding the veto pen through 2028. The clean take is stalemate — nothing passes, so nothing to do. That reading is wrong, and the mistake is expensive.

What matters here isn't the wealth tax everybody wants to argue about. Warren's 2% levy above $50 million is a messaging bill headed for a hearing and then, if it ever passes, straight to a Supreme Court fight over whether an unapportioned tax on unrealized net worth is even constitutional. That litigation runs for years. Treat it as noise.

The real exposure sits in the plumbing, and it's the plumbing that scores well without raising a single rate. The provisions that came closest to passing in 2021 — pulling new grantor trusts back into the estate, taxing sales between a grantor and their trust, a 10-year minimum GRAT term that kills the short rolling GRAT, limits on dynasty-trust GST sheltering, restrictions on valuation discounts for family LLCs, and deemed realization at death that guts the basis step-up — are the ones that get reintroduced, scored, and shelved as ready-to-move revenue. They don't need a courtroom. They need one unified government, which 2028 could deliver.

Here's the structural point the "permanent means safe" crowd misses: the value of the current exemption is not the $31 million number. It's the grandfathering. Every past tightening of these rules has protected transfers already completed under prior law, and the article flags the live risk — anti-clawback protection only attaches to gifts that are clean and complete, not transfers with retained interests. So the window that's actually open isn't the exemption amount, which indexes up regardless. It's the two-year runway to get assets into irrevocable structures before the toolbox gets rewritten, with the structures grandfathered and the appreciation sitting outside the estate.

This runs directly into what this desk has been tracking for a month. DBS found only 20% of Asian family offices use a professionally administered trust and 54% hold key assets in the founder's personal name. Bank of America found 83% named a successor but only 27% wrote a distribution policy. Those families are structurally unready to execute a complete gift inside a 24-month window, because they haven't built the vehicles that would receive it.

So the planning move is boring and urgent at once: fund the trusts now, make the gifts complete rather than clever, and bake in decanting authority, swap powers, and the ability to switch off grantor status — flexibility that survives a rewrite in either direction. The families who treat "permanent" as a reason to wait are the ones who'll be planning in a panic in late 2028, after the window has priced in the election.

The other side

The structural case for waiting is real: Trump holds the veto pen through January 2029, a two-thirds override requires Republican defectors on legislation that cuts exemptions and restricts grantor trusts, and every prior attempt to close these planning techniques — including the 2021 reconciliation push — collapsed before a floor vote. Families who accelerate gifts now bear real costs: liquidity risk on assets transferred irrevocably, potential gift tax exposure if valuations are challenged, and the possibility that a 2029 Congress simply raises the exemption again rather than attacking the toolbox. Locking in a plan around a threat that never materializes is its own form of expensive mistake.

Say more →

The mechanism worth understanding is why the plumbing proposals score so well in Washington relative to the headline wealth tax. A 2% annual levy on net worth above $50 million raises large numbers on paper but faces an immediate constitutional challenge — the direct-tax apportionment requirement in Article I, Section 9, which the Sixteenth Amendment carved out only for income taxes. Unrealized net worth almost certainly does not qualify. That litigation would run for years, and Congress knows it. The grantor trust and GRAT proposals, by contrast, are straightforward income and transfer tax modifications that raise real revenue without touching the Constitution. They get scored by the Joint Committee on Taxation, they show up as offsets in a reconciliation package, and they move when the political window opens.

The specific provisions the source material flags as closest to passage in 2021 are worth naming precisely because they target the techniques most widely used by families in the $15 million to $150 million range — the clients who are not billionaires but who have built meaningful wealth through business ownership, real estate, or concentrated equity. A 10-year minimum GRAT term eliminates the short rolling GRAT strategy, where a grantor transfers assets into a Grantor Retained Annuity Trust for two or three years, captures the upside above the IRS hurdle rate, and repeats the cycle. Deemed realization at death — taxing appreciation at the moment of transfer rather than allowing heirs to inherit a stepped-up cost basis — would, for many families, represent a larger economic hit than the estate tax itself, because the basis step-up is the mechanism that allows decades of unrealized appreciation to pass tax-free across generations.

The grandfathering point in the source material deserves more weight than it typically gets in client conversations. Every major tightening of estate planning rules in recent decades has protected structures already in place. That pattern is not guaranteed to hold — a future Congress could choose not to grandfather — but it has been the consistent legislative approach, and it is the reason that getting assets into irrevocable structures before a rule change matters more than the exemption amount itself. A dynasty trust funded today, with GST exemption properly allocated, sits in a materially different legal position than one funded after the rules change, even if the exemption amount is identical.

The practical constraint is the one this desk has been tracking across multiple recent stories: most families have not built the vehicles that would receive a large, clean gift on a compressed timeline. Holding key assets in a founder's personal name — a pattern documented across family office surveys — is not just a governance problem. It is a structural barrier to executing the kind of complete, irrevocable transfer that earns anti-clawback protection. A gift with a retained interest, or one that can be pulled back into the estate, does not get grandfathered under the proposed anti-clawback regulations flagged in the source material.

The advisory implication is that the two-year window is not primarily about the exemption amount, which indexes upward automatically. It is about completing transfers into structures that are already in place and properly drafted — with decanting authority, trust protector provisions, swap powers, and the ability to toggle grantor trust status on or off as the law evolves. Families who have not yet built those structures face a sequencing problem: they need to establish the vehicle, fund it, and make the gift complete, all before a political environment that could shift sharply after November 2028.

Private Markets Middle Market Co-Investment LP Dynamics

Mid-market PE captured 40% of fundraising by making the fund itself a side door to fee-free deals.

pitchbook.com
  • US PE funds sized $1B–$5B took 40% of all capital raised in Q1–Q3 2026, while mega-fund fundraising slowed from last year's pace, per PitchBook data.
  • LPs are writing larger primary commitments specifically to secure bigger pro rata co-investment rights — deals that typically carry zero management fee and zero carried interest.
  • David Conrod of placement agent FocusPoint Private Capital Group warns that LPs focused on cost reduction are quietly stacking single-asset concentration they have not properly underwritten as risk.

The take

PitchBook's number is the news: US PE funds between $1 billion and $5 billion captured 40% of all capital raised in the first three quarters of 2026, while the mega-funds that owned the fundraising narrative for a decade have slowed. The stated reason — LPs want co-investment access, and mid-market sponsors are more generous with it — is true but told backwards.

The honest version is that co-investment has stopped being a perk and become the actual product. An LP writes a larger primary check into a commingled fund with the explicit intention of securing a bigger pro rata slice of fee-free, carry-free direct deals alongside it. Brian Wheeler at Foley & Lardner says it plainly: the fund commitment is the toll you pay to get at the co-invest. So the headline "mid-market fundraising is up" is measuring the wrong thing. The commingled vehicle is increasingly a loss-leader that exists to generate a pipeline of zero-fee deals. Blended management fees across these relationships are falling even as gross commitments rise, which means the mid-market manager's economics on the flagship fund are quietly deteriorating while the AUM chart looks great.

Here is the mechanism that makes this fragile. Mega-funds offer stingy co-invest because they don't need the equity — they can write the whole check. Mid-market sponsors are generous precisely because they're capital-constrained and need LPs to finance the larger deals they couldn't otherwise close. That generosity is a function of weakness, not strength. The moment a mid-market firm raises enough or deal sizes drift up, the co-invest spigot tightens — exactly when the LP has concentrated most heavily. David Conrod at placement agent FocusPoint flags the real exposure: LPs chasing cost reduction are stacking single-asset concentration they haven't underwritten as risk. A co-invest is one company, no diversification, often on the biggest and most competitively-bid deals in the portfolio.

Now connect it to the plumbing we've been tracking. Exits are seized — a $4 trillion backlog, continuation vehicles running at 14% of sponsor-backed exits, the IPO window shut for anything under $1 billion. In that environment, the co-invest that was supposed to "juice returns" becomes another illiquid single-name mark sitting on an LP's book with no exit path. The LP lowered its blended fee and raised its blended illiquidity and concentration. That's a worse trade than the fee line suggests, and it won't show up until a few of these deals need to clear and can't.

The structural shift that's real: capital is migrating from managers who gather assets to managers who need partners. For a UHNW book with genuine deal-selection capability and the governance to say no, that's a live edge — direct co-invest alongside a disciplined $2 billion sponsor is a legitimately good risk-adjusted entry. For the family office treating co-invest as a fee hack and accepting whatever's offered to keep its seat warm, it's adverse selection dressed as a discount. The 40% number is a measure of who now needs whom — and it's the LPs with underwriting muscle, not the ones chasing cost, who should be leaning in.

The other side

The LP math is not obviously wrong. Fee drag compounds over a fund's life, and if a $200M pension can access a disciplined mid-market sponsor's best deals at zero carry, the blended return on the full relationship — fund plus co-invests — can meaningfully outperform a pure commingled allocation even accounting for concentration. Andrea Schwartzman at Latham & Watkins makes the governance point too: being a financially important partner to a $2 billion sponsor gives an LP real influence over deal selection and terms, something that is structurally impossible at a mega-fund where your check is a rounding error.

The fee arithmetic is worth spelling out because it changes how you read the 40% figure. A co-investment alongside a PE fund typically charges no management fee and no carried interest — the two line items that, at standard 2-and-20 terms, can absorb 300 to 500 basis points of gross return over a fund's life depending on deployment pace and hold period. When an LP writes a larger primary check to secure a bigger slice of those fee-free deals, the blended cost of the whole relationship falls even as the gross commitment rises. The AUM chart at the mid-market manager looks healthy. The fee revenue per dollar managed is quietly compressing.

That compression is the part the fundraising headline obscures. Brian Wheeler at Foley & Lardner describes the dynamic directly: the commingled fund commitment is the price of admission to the co-invest pipeline. If that is the actual transaction, then the flagship fund is functioning as a loss-leader — a vehicle whose economics subsidize a parallel stream of zero-fee deals that LPs value more. Mid-market managers are winning the fundraising competition partly by giving away the margin that makes the business work at scale.

The reason they can afford to be generous is also the reason the arrangement is structurally unstable. Mid-market sponsors need LP equity to close deals that exceed what the fund alone can finance. Mega-funds do not have that problem — they can write the full check and offer co-invest selectively, to the largest and closest relationships, often waiving fees only for that inner circle while smaller LPs sit in a separate vehicle paying full freight. The mid-market manager's openness with co-invest is a direct function of being capital-constrained. As a firm grows — raises a larger successor fund, builds out its balance sheet, attracts more LP capital — the constraint eases and the co-invest spigot tightens. The LP who concentrated most aggressively to secure access will find the access shrinking at exactly the moment the relationship has deepened.

Layer in the exit environment and the risk profile gets harder to defend. The broader brief has tracked a $4 trillion backlog of unrealized private equity value, continuation vehicles running at 14% of sponsor-backed exits, and an IPO window that is effectively closed for companies below $1 billion in enterprise value. Co-investments are single-name, undiversified positions — often in the largest and most competitively bid deals in a sponsor's portfolio, which is where adverse selection risk is highest. In a functioning exit market, a well-chosen co-invest clears in three to five years and the fee savings are real. In the current environment, it sits on the LP's book as an illiquid, concentrated mark with no obvious path to liquidity. The LP lowered its blended fee and raised its blended illiquidity and concentration simultaneously. Those two effects do not net to zero.

Conrod's warning about overexposure risk is the most underappreciated line in the source material. LPs treating co-invest as a cost-reduction tool — accepting whatever deal is offered to maintain their seat — are running a selection process that inverts good underwriting. The sponsor brings the deals it needs help financing, not necessarily the deals it is most confident in. An LP with genuine deal-selection capability, the governance to decline unattractive co-invests, and the analytical bandwidth to underwrite individual companies can extract real value from this structure. A family office or smaller institution treating co-invest as an automatic fee discount and saying yes to maintain the relationship is absorbing the sponsor's capital-raising problem and calling it alpha.
