Tech & AI Anthropic Broadcom IPO Compute

Broadcom will lend Anthropic $42 billion to lease Broadcom's own chips — and can take equity if Anthropic can't pay.

reuters.com
  • Broadcom's lending facility covers up to $42 billion, financing roughly one-third of Anthropic's $125.2 billion five-year TPU lease commitment.
  • Broadcom can convert the debt into Anthropic equity — a conversion right Anthropic's own prospectus flags as a conflict of interest over AI infrastructure access.
  • The total lease obligation is a fixed cost locked in years ahead of the revenue needed to service it, with no flex if token pricing deteriorates.

The take

Anthropic's IPO prospectus just revealed the plumbing underneath its compute ambitions, and one line stands out: Broadcom will lend Anthropic up to $42 billion to lease Broadcom's own chips, financing roughly a third of a $125.2 billion five-year TPU lease commitment. The lender is also the supplier. And per the filing, Broadcom can convert that debt into Anthropic equity — which Anthropic itself flags as a conflict over who gets priority access to AI infrastructure.

Strip the logos off and this is vendor financing, the same structure that inflated the telecom buildout before 2001. Lucent and Nortel lent their customers the money to buy Lucent and Nortel equipment, booked the sales as revenue, and discovered the problem only when the customers couldn't pay. Broadcom isn't quite there — these are leases, not outright sales — but the circularity is identical. Broadcom funds demand for its own silicon, recognizes the lease stream, and the cash never leaves the ecosystem. It's a closed loop that looks like growth from the outside.

Here's the mechanism that matters. A $125.2 billion lease obligation is a fixed cost that compounds whether or not Anthropic's revenue shows up. Goldman's own math, which we covered Sunday, says the hyperscaler cohort needs roughly $300 billion a year in AI revenue to justify 2027 capex, and that OpenAI and Anthropic may not clear their share fast enough. Anthropic is now locking in the cost side of that equation years ahead of the revenue side, and borrowing from its chip vendor to do it. If the token-revenue curve disappoints — and Chinese open-weight models crossing 50% developer share on OpenRouter and Vercel says the pricing power is already eroding — the lease doesn't flex. The debt-to-equity conversion is Broadcom's parachute: if Anthropic can't service the facility, Broadcom ends up owning a slice of the company instead of getting paid. That's not a financing term, it's a hedge against the borrower's own business model.

The more interesting question is why Anthropic is routing this through Broadcom's custom TPUs at all rather than Nvidia. The answer is that Nvidia won't finance your dependence on Nvidia the way a merchant-silicon partner will — Broadcom is buying strategic lock-in, and Anthropic is paying for diversification away from the Nvidia tax with a different kind of dependence. Either way the lab is a price-taker on compute, now with a balance sheet to match.

What this says to anyone sizing AI exposure: the capital stack behind the frontier labs is getting reflexive. Suppliers are financing customers, customers are pre-committing to multi-year fixed costs, and equity convertibility is being used to paper over credit risk that would otherwise be priced as what it is. The clean version of the bull case — labs grow into their compute — depends on none of these loops unwinding at once. The prospectus is telling you the loops are now load-bearing. Price the lease obligation as debt, not ambition, and Anthropic's IPO looks a lot more leveraged than the headline valuation will suggest.

The other side

The bear case on vendor financing assumes the borrower is fragile, but Anthropic is entering its IPO with a disclosed capital structure, not hiding the arrangement. A $42 billion credit facility from a strategic partner is also cheaper and faster than raising equivalent equity, and the TPU lease gives Anthropic a credible compute roadmap independent of Nvidia's allocation queue — which has real option value. If Anthropic's revenue scales as the bull case projects, the debt-to-equity conversion clause never triggers, and Broadcom ends up as a well-compensated infrastructure partner rather than a distressed creditor turned reluctant shareholder.

The prospectus disclosure does something unusual: it names the conflict explicitly. Anthropic flags that Broadcom's dual role — lender and chip supplier — creates potential conflicts over who gets priority access to AI infrastructure. That's a remarkable sentence to put in a public filing. It means Anthropic's legal team concluded the arrangement was material enough to require disclosure, which is a different thing than saying it's manageable.

The telecom parallel in the take is worth extending. When Lucent and Nortel financed their own customers' equipment purchases in the late 1990s, the arrangement worked as long as traffic growth validated the buildout. The moment growth slowed, the circularity reversed: customers couldn't service the debt, the vendors couldn't collect, and the booked revenue turned out to be a loan in disguise. Broadcom's structure differs in one important way — these are leases, not equipment sales — but the economic logic is the same. Broadcom is effectively pre-buying demand for its own silicon by funding the buyer's ability to commit.

The TPU angle matters here. Broadcom designs custom AI accelerators (TPUs, or tensor processing units) for hyperscalers and, in this case, for Anthropic directly. Nvidia dominates the merchant GPU market, but Broadcom's custom silicon business operates on a different model: the chip is designed around the customer's workload, which creates deeper integration and, as a consequence, deeper lock-in. Anthropic is paying to escape Nvidia's pricing power, but the escape route runs through a single alternative supplier who is now also its creditor. The dependency doesn't disappear — it changes shape.

The scale of the commitment relative to Anthropic's current revenue position is the number that deserves the most scrutiny, and the prospectus doesn't resolve it cleanly. What the filing does confirm is that $125.2 billion in lease obligations over five years is a fixed-cost structure that doesn't compress if token revenue disappoints. As covered here two days ago, Goldman's analysis of the broader hyperscaler cohort puts the revenue bar needed to justify 2027 AI capex at roughly $300 billion annually across the group — a bar Goldman itself questions whether OpenAI and Anthropic can clear fast enough. Anthropic is now pre-committing to a cost structure sized against a revenue projection that hasn't materialized yet.

The equity conversion right is the term that changes the risk profile most. In a standard credit facility, a lender who isn't repaid pursues the borrower through conventional remedies. Here, Broadcom has a path to becoming an Anthropic shareholder instead — which means Broadcom's downside in a stress scenario is partial ownership of a frontier AI lab, not a write-off. From Broadcom's perspective, that's a rational hedge. From Anthropic's perspective, it means the IPO's cap table could shift materially depending on how the revenue ramp goes, and public shareholders buying at the headline valuation may be pricing in a cleaner balance sheet than the filing actually describes.

The broader pattern this fits is one where the capital stack behind frontier AI is becoming increasingly self-referential. Suppliers finance customers, customers pre-commit to multi-year fixed costs, and convertible instruments are used to manage credit risk that the market hasn't yet priced as credit risk. Anthropic's prospectus is the clearest public window yet into how that stack is actually assembled.

Private Markets Software Secondaries SaaS GP Marks

Software secondaries are finally clearing, but only after sellers abandoned the fiction of 2021 valuations.

pitchbook.com
  • Buyers at Campbell Lutyens now accept portfolios with up to 35-40% software exposure, up from a hard 20% cap immediately after the selloff.
  • Median SaaS EV/EBITDA multiples fell to 11.7x from 20.4x per the Forvis Mazars/PitchBook H1 2026 report — the entire decade-long software premium erased in one cycle.
  • Ardian, which closed its last dedicated secondaries fund at a record $30 billion, is selectively buying into LP-led software transactions where it can exclude funds it doesn't want.

The take

Six months after a software selloff wiped more than $1 trillion off listed software stocks, LP portfolios heavy in private software are finally trading on the secondary market. The unlock, per buyers at Ardian, Campbell Lutyens and Monument Group, isn't that software recovered — it's that sellers quit pretending it would. Buyers who wanted diversified books capped at 20% software are now taking 35-40%.

This is the same seller capitulation we've been tracking across the secondaries beat all month, just finally reaching the ugliest sleeve. CVC raising $10 billion, GIC and KIC each dumping $1 billion through PJT, Blackstone running a secondary on its own $11 billion real estate fund — the through-line is that institutional sellers stopped defending stale marks and started accepting clearing prices. Software was the last holdout because the gap between carrying value and bid was the widest. Median SaaS EV/EBITDA multiples fell to 11.7x from 20.4x per the Forvis Mazars/PitchBook work. That's not a correction, that's the erasure of the entire decade-long software premium. A 2021-vintage fund that bought at 20x and hasn't written it down is carrying fiction.

Here's the mechanism worth sitting with. The buyers quoted are explicit that pricing now depends on how honestly a GP marked its own book. Managers who wrote software down early "receive less skepticism and therefore higher bids." Managers who held the line are being punished with wider discounts. That is the secondary market repricing GP integrity directly into the transaction. For years a GP could smooth marks and face no consequence because nobody transacted on them. Now the bid is the audit. A fund that marked slowly isn't just carrying overstated NAV — it's carrying a credibility discount that follows every future interaction with that LP base.

Which creates a nasty incentive flip. The honest marker — who took the paper pain in H1 2026 — now trades near its carrying value and looks competent to LPs. The optimist who protected its IRR optics is discovering that protection was a loan against future trust, and the loan just came due at a worse rate. Over a full cycle, aggressive marking is negative carry.

The structural problem underneath all of this is the one Benzinga surfaced from the same PitchBook data: 33% of 2017 US buyout deals are still unsold, and at the current exit pace it would take 10.8 years to clear the inventory of 13,325 PE-backed companies. Software secondaries trading is a release valve on a tank that is still filling faster than it drains. The reason buyers' software appetite is "recovering" isn't conviction — it's that Ardian's $30 billion secondaries fund and CVC's $10 billion need deployment, and discounted 2021 software at a reset multiple is where the capital can actually go to work. The buy-side firepower is real and the sell-side desperation is real. What's missing is any exit path for the underlying companies that doesn't rely on an IPO window that remains shut.

The other side

The bear case on this recovery is that it's buyer-driven deployment pressure masquerading as restored conviction. Ardian's $30 billion fund and CVC's $10 billion need to put capital to work, and discounted software is one of the few places with enough volume to absorb it — which means clearing prices may reflect supply-demand mechanics more than any genuine re-rating of software's long-term value. If the underlying companies still face a shut IPO window and a strategic buyer market that has already done its AI-driven M&A (global SaaS M&A hit a decade-high $439.7 billion in H1 2026 largely on AI deals), the secondary trades happening now could simply be passing the duration problem from one set of institutional hands to another.

The detail that deserves the most attention is the one about GP marking behavior. Buyers at Campbell Lutyens are explicit: managers who wrote down software holdings earlier in the year are receiving less skepticism and higher bids. That is not a soft reputational observation — it is the secondary market converting GP honesty into a direct pricing input on every transaction. For most of the past decade, a GP could defend an aggressive carrying value indefinitely because the mark only mattered when someone transacted on it, and transactions were rare enough to avoid. The SaaSpocalypse changed that by forcing a wave of LP-side selling. Suddenly the bid is the audit, and the audit is public in the sense that every buyer in the room can see which managers held the line and which ones didn't.

The 2021 vintage is the sharpest illustration. Nick Best at Campbell Lutyens — a secondary advisory and placement firm — identifies those funds as the hardest sell, and the reason is straightforward: managers who bought aggressively at peak multiples in 2021 and haven't marked down are asking buyers to accept both the valuation risk and the credibility risk simultaneously. Buyers are declining, or demanding discounts wide enough to compensate for both. The GP that protected its IRR optics through 2025 by marking slowly has now discovered that the protection was effectively a loan against future LP trust, and the loan is being called at a punishing rate.

The vintage structure also matters for a less obvious reason. More recent funds — those with significant uninvested capital still to deploy — are trading at smaller discounts because dry powder is easy to value and the value-creation clock hasn't fully run. Older vintages, particularly pre-2020, carry steeper discounts even absent the 2021 problem, because the exit window for their underlying companies is narrowing and the path to liquidity increasingly depends on either an IPO market that remains largely closed or a strategic acquirer willing to pay. Ardian's Quentin Hugot is explicit about what he needs to get comfortable: reasonable entry valuations, durable growth and retention metrics, appropriate leverage, and exit paths that do not rely solely on an IPO or a large strategic acquisition. That last clause is doing a lot of work. It is essentially a description of the assets that don't exist in most 2021-vintage software funds.

Zoom out and the inventory problem is severe. Per PitchBook data surfaced by Benzinga, 33% of U.S. buyout deals from 2017 remain unsold nearly a decade later. The total count of PE-backed U.S. companies reached 13,325 in Q1 2026, and at the annualized exit pace recorded that quarter, clearing the backlog would take more than 10.8 years. Software secondaries trading is a pressure-release valve, but the tank is still filling. The secondary market can reprice LP exposure and move it from motivated sellers to better-capitalized buyers — it cannot manufacture exits for the underlying portfolio companies. Until the IPO window reopens or strategic M&A broadens beyond AI-driven mega-deals, the secondaries market is managing the symptom rather than the condition.

Private Markets Secondaries GP Dynamics Ares

Ares raised $4.2 billion to become the balance sheet behind GP alignment.

news.bloomberglaw.com
  • Ares Global Structured Solutions Fund closed at $4.2 billion against a $1 billion initial target — more than quadruple — on its debut raise.
  • The fund provides preferred equity directly to general partners for GP commitments, seeding new strategies, and succession planning — not LP stake purchases.
  • Ares has deployed nearly $9 billion across structured solutions transactions since 2013 but only now dedicated a standalone pool to the strategy.

The take

Ares just closed a debut secondaries fund at $4.2 billion against a $1 billion target — more than quadruple. This is the tenth secondaries or continuation-vehicle story in this section inside three weeks, so the volume is not the news. What matters is what AGSS actually finances: not LP stakes bought at a discount, but preferred equity written directly to GPs so they can make bigger commitments to their own funds, seed new strategies, and fund succession.

Follow who the customer is. Every other secondaries story we've covered has been about an LP trying to get out — GIC and KIC each selling a billion through PJT, Blackstone running a process on its own $11 billion property fund, Morgan Stanley gating North Haven for a fourth straight quarter. The seller is always a limited partner who needs cash the fund can't return. Ares is selling the mirror image of that trade. The buyer here is the general partner, and what they're buying is the ability to keep writing checks into a market where their own LPs have stopped funding the GP commitment.

That's the quiet admission. A GP commitment is supposed to be the partners' own money on the line — alignment paid for out of pocket. Permira made a show of exactly this three weeks ago, with employees putting up roughly €1 billion, three to ten times the industry norm. AGSS exists because most firms can't or won't do that anymore. Ares is now the balance sheet behind the alignment. The GP looks committed to its LPs; the commitment is financed by preferred equity sitting senior to the GP's own economics. Alignment becomes a structured product.

The $1 billion target versus $4.2 billion close tells you Ares priced this blind and the demand was four times what the smartest secondaries team in the market guessed. When an experienced desk misses its own forecast by that margin, it's not conservatism — it's evidence of a funding gap that opened faster than anyone modeled. Firms across the mid and upper-mid market need outside capital just to stand still: to hit their next-fund GP stake, to launch the credit or infra strategy the board demanded, to buy out a retiring founder whose equity nobody internal can afford. Ares deployed nearly $9 billion across these structures since 2013 and only now dedicated a standalone pool. The timing matches the DPI drought exactly.

Where this goes: preferred equity into GP-level balance sheets is the next leg of leverage stacking onto a private-markets system already borrowing against NAV at the fund level. When distributions eventually normalize, the GP pays Ares first. When they don't, Ares owns a growing claim on the management companies themselves. The firms taking this money are telling you their own partners won't fund their growth — and that's the position from which they're raising your next flagship.

The other side

Preferred equity to GPs is not inherently predatory — it's a rational response to a real capital need, and LPs in AGSS are getting a senior, structured return in a market where unsecured exposure to private managers has historically been well-compensated. If the DPI drought is temporary and distributions normalize over the next two to three years, GPs pay Ares back, the alignment fiction holds, and everyone moves on. The $4.2 billion close could simply reflect sophisticated LPs rotating from discount-driven secondaries — where competition has compressed returns — into a less crowded part of the capital structure with better downside protection.

The mechanism deserves unpacking because it is genuinely different from everything else in the secondaries market right now. Traditional LP-side secondaries — the CVC fund that closed at $10 billion last week, the GIC and KIC processes running through PJT — involve buying an existing fund stake from an LP who wants out, typically at a discount to net asset value. The buyer acquires exposure to a portfolio already in motion. AGSS does none of that. It writes preferred equity to the general partner itself, meaning the capital sits at the management company or GP entity level, senior to the partners' own carried interest and co-investment economics. The GP gets the cash it needs to write a credible commitment check into its next fund; Ares gets a preferred return and a claim that ranks ahead of the GP's upside.

The structural implication is that LP due diligence on GP alignment just got harder. When an LP evaluates a new flagship fund, one of the first questions is how much of the GP's own money is in the vehicle. AGSS means the answer — whatever number appears in the fund documents — may be partially financed by a third-party preferred equity provider. The GP is technically committed; the commitment is leveraged. This is not illegal or even unusual in isolation, but at scale, across the mid and upper-mid market, it changes what GP commitment actually signals.

Ares head of secondaries Nate Walton said the firm underestimated LP demand and was uncertain how investors would react. That admission from an experienced desk that has deployed nearly $9 billion in these structures since 2013 is worth sitting with. Ares knows this market better than almost anyone, and they still missed by a factor of four. The most plausible explanation is that the funding gap at the GP level widened faster than even practitioners expected — driven by the combination of a prolonged distribution drought, rising GP commitment expectations from LPs demanding more skin in the game, and a wave of succession events at firms founded in the 2000s whose founding partners need liquidity.

Succession is the underappreciated driver here. A retiring founder at a mid-market firm typically holds a large equity stake in the management company and a carried interest position across multiple vintage funds. Buying that out requires either a sale of the management company, a third-party recapitalization, or exactly the kind of structured preferred equity AGSS provides. As the cohort of managers who built firms during the 2000s and 2010s approaches retirement age, the succession problem becomes a recurring capital need — not a one-time event.

The leverage-stacking concern raised in the take is real and worth quantifying directionally. Private equity funds already borrow at the portfolio company level. Many now use NAV facilities — loans secured against the fund's net asset value — to manage liquidity and fund distributions. Add preferred equity at the GP entity level, and you have three layers of debt-like claims before an LP sees a dollar of return: portfolio company leverage, fund-level NAV borrowing, and now GP-level preferred equity owed to Ares. Each layer is individually defensible; together they represent a system where the equity cushion absorbing any downturn has quietly thinned.

Family Office & Wealth Planning Family Office Private Equity AI Governance

North American family offices turned greedy in twelve months. The exit data says they should not have.

institutionalinvestor.com, prnewswire.com, spearswms.com
  • 47% of family offices that tried to exit a private-market fund position this year could not complete the exit as planned — the same asset class they are now expanding
  • 75% of offices expect the AI bubble to burst within five years, yet 41% intend to increase AI exposure and almost none plan to reduce it
  • Cybersecurity concern jumped from 16% to 59% of respondents in a single year, the sharpest single-year operational risk shift in the survey

The take

RBC and Campden Wealth surveyed 155 North American family offices and found sentiment flipped from defensive to greedy in twelve months. A year ago the group expected a 5% average return and one in seven braced for a loss. After every asset class closed 2025 in the green, 84% now expect direct private equity to match or beat last year's numbers. Campden's own research director describes the mood in plain terms: "Even patient capital can be caught up in the fear of missing out."

That line is worth more than the whole optimism headline. The entire selling point of a family office — the reason you build the infrastructure instead of handing the money to a wirehouse — is that it does not chase. It compounds across generations and ignores the quarterly tape. When the people running $2.25 billion on average revise their forward expectations up by a factor of three because last year was good, they are not exercising a structural edge. They are performance-chasing with better stationery.

Look at where they're pointing the money. Direct investments are now 45% of the private markets book, ahead of pooled funds at 36%. Direct deals are the least liquid, most relationship-dependent, hardest-to-mark assets a family can hold — and this crowd is overweighting them into a cycle it privately thinks is late. Same survey: 75% expect the AI bubble to burst within five years, 59% think AI returns won't meet current expectations, and yet 41% intend to increase AI exposure anyway. Ratner calls it the "AI paradox." Call it what it is — buying an asset you expect to fall because you can't stand watching others ride it up.

The liquidity data is where the optimism curdles. Nearly one in five private-market investors tried to exit a fund position this year, and 47% of them could not complete the exit as planned. Almost half the people who wanted their cash back couldn't get it. That is the actual state of the private book these offices are now expanding, not the forward return they've penciled in. Patient capital is a virtue right up until it's involuntary.

Then stack the governance picture on top. Half these offices have an incomplete or non-existent succession plan, 23% have already been through a generational transfer in the last five years, and cyber jumped from a 16% concern to 59% in a single year. So the composite here is a family office levering into illiquid directs and AI, with no exit certainty, no finished succession plan, and a security posture it only just noticed was exposed. That's a lot of fragility wrapped in a surprisingly upbeat press release.

For the house, the signal is contrarian in the simple direction: when the structurally patient money turns into the marginal buyer of illiquid directs at the top of a cycle it expects to break, that is the moment to be the seller to them, or at minimum the holder of their liquidity when they come looking to exit and 47% of the door is already shut.

The other side

The bull case is not irrational on its face. Every asset class posted positive median returns in 2025, and a family office with a genuine 20-to-30-year horizon has more room to ride out a correction in illiquid directs than a pension fund facing quarterly redemptions. If AI does follow the internet arc — bubble, bust, then decade-long structural transformation — being out of the asset class entirely during the transformation phase is the more expensive mistake. And direct investing at 45% of the private book is not reckless in isolation; it reflects a deliberate shift away from fee-heavy fund structures toward assets families can actually influence. The optimism may be poorly timed, but the structural logic behind the allocation shift is not obviously wrong.

Say more →

The RBC and Campden Wealth report covers 155 single-family offices and private multi-family offices across the Americas, with an average operating net worth of $2.25 billion — so this is not a sample of cautious inheritors sitting on Treasury bills. These are sophisticated, well-staffed pools of capital that built their infrastructure precisely to avoid the behavioral errors that retail and institutional money make. The fact that their research director is voluntarily invoking FOMO as an explanatory variable is the most important sentence in the whole document.

The AI paradox deserves more unpacking than the headline optimism gets. Three data points sit in the same survey: 75% expect the AI bubble to burst within five years, 59% believe AI returns will not meet current expectations, and 85% still name AI as their top investment pick for the next twelve months. That is not a group that has done the analysis and concluded the consensus is wrong. That is a group that has done the analysis, concluded the consensus is probably right about a correction coming, and decided to buy anyway because the social cost of missing the run-up feels worse than the financial cost of catching the drawdown. Campden's Ratner draws the internet parallel explicitly — boom, bubble, long-term transformation — but that parallel cuts both ways. The families who loaded up on Pets.com and Webvan in 1999 because they believed in the long-term internet thesis did not recover on the same timeline as the technology itself.

The liquidity picture is the structural problem that the optimism headline papers over. Nearly one in five private-market investors attempted a fund exit this year. Of those, 47% could not complete it as expected. That failure rate is not a rounding error — it means roughly one in ten of all private-market investors in this survey tried to get liquid and found the door partially closed. These offices are now increasing their direct allocation, which is less liquid than fund exposure, into a market where secondary exits are already failing at nearly half the attempt rate. Direct deals have no secondary market infrastructure comparable even to the limited LP-stake market, so the exit problem compounds as the allocation shifts.

The governance fragility sitting underneath all of this is underappreciated. Half of surveyed offices have an incomplete or non-existent succession plan. One in five offices was formed within the past six years, meaning a meaningful share of this optimistic cohort has never managed through a full private-market cycle. Twenty-three percent have already experienced a generational transfer in the last five years — a transition that typically disrupts investment committee continuity, risk tolerance calibration, and manager relationships simultaneously. Layering a concentrated illiquid book and a cybersecurity posture that went from a fringe concern to the top operational risk in a single year on top of that governance picture produces a fragility profile that the upbeat sentiment numbers do not reflect.

The cyber jump alone — 16% concern last year, 59% this year, with 59% of offices reporting phishing attempts — suggests these offices are discovering exposures that were always there, not responding to a new external threat that materialized overnight. That is a different and more uncomfortable reading: the risk was present while they were busy being optimistic about private equity returns.
