Private Markets Secondaries GP Dynamics Fundraising

The secondary market just hit a record. Most of that volume is GPs selling to themselves.

  • H1 2026 secondary market volume hit $121 billion, up 19% year over year — a record high per Evercore's mid-year review
  • Single-asset continuation vehicles (structures where a sponsor moves one company from an old fund into a new one instead of selling it outright) reached $34 billion, up 88% in a single half
  • Dry powder — the undeployed capital available to buy deals — fell 10% to $194 billion, and the market needs $154 billion in fresh fundraising in H2 just to sustain current deal flow

The take

Evercore's H1 2026 secondary review just landed with a headline number the industry will parade around: $121 billion in volume, up 19% year over year, a record. The line everyone will highlight is GP-led deals surging 35% to $65 billion and finally overtaking traditional LP portfolio sales. What almost nobody will say out loud is what that composition actually means: the secondary market is no longer a place where LPs get liquidity — it's a place where GPs refuse to sell.

Look at the single asset continuation vehicles — structures where a sponsor rolls one prized company out of an aging fund into a new one rather than exit it. Those did $34 billion, up 88%, and now make up 53% of all GP-led volume. Eighty-eight percent growth in a single half is not a market maturing, it's a market discovering a workaround. The consensus reads "sponsors retaining high-conviction companies" as a bullish signal of quality. Read it the other way: the trade sales aren't there, the IPO window is a rumor, and the honest exit price is below the carrying mark. A continuation vehicle is what you build when you can't or won't sell to a real buyer. It's a related-party transaction dressed as conviction, and the GP prices both sides of it.

The tell is in the capital math, not the volume. Dry powder in secondaries fell 10% to $194 billion, leaving roughly a 1.0x overhang — one dollar of buying power for one dollar of expected deal flow. That cushion is gone. Evercore then quietly notes the market needs $154 billion of fresh fundraising in H2 just to keep the machine fed. That is the real story: the demand side has to raise almost as much as it deployed all half, or the bid disappears. When the marginal buyer of your continuation vehicle has to go raise the money to buy it, you don't have a deep liquidity market. You have a chain letter with better lawyers.

For an allocator, the read-across is uncomfortable because you're on both sides. The DPI you're finally receiving is increasingly your GP selling an asset to a fund your peers just seeded — liquidity manufactured inside the system, not extracted from it. Ask the sharper question before you re-up: who is the third-party price-setter on these single-asset deals, and what independent bid cleared the mark? If the answer is "the same secondary funds who need $154 billion to stay open," you're not being handed a distribution. You're being handed the exit risk of the prior vintage, repackaged as a fresh commitment, and asked to thank them for it.

The other side

The bull case deserves a real hearing: sponsors holding their best assets rather than force-selling them into a closed IPO window or a weak M&A market is rational capital stewardship, not accounting sleight of hand. The best continuation vehicles do involve arm's-length secondary buyers — large, sophisticated funds like Lexington Partners or Ardian — who run real diligence and reject deals where the price doesn't clear. And from an LP perspective, the alternative to a continuation vehicle at a negotiated markup is often a fire-sale exit that destroys the returns the whole fund was built around. The structure isn't inherently corrupt; the question is always whether genuine price discovery happened, and sometimes it does.

The 88% surge in single-asset continuation vehicles is worth sitting with for a moment, because it didn't happen in a vacuum. It happened against the backdrop of an IPO market that has been functionally closed to venture and growth-backed companies for the better part of three years, and a leveraged buyout exit environment where strategic buyers are still digesting prior acquisitions and rising debt costs have made financial sponsors cautious acquirers. When the traditional exit channels clog, capital finds a new pipe — and in this cycle, that pipe runs through the secondary market. The problem is that moving an asset sideways is not the same as monetizing it. It just resets the clock and resets the basis for whoever buys into the continuation fund.

The pricing mechanism here is the part that should make any sophisticated allocator pause. In a standard secondary transaction, an LP sells a portfolio of fund interests to a buyer who applies a discount to net asset value — the buyer negotiates hard, takes a haircut, and the market sets a price. The GP has no control over what their fund's assets are worth in that exchange. In a single-asset continuation vehicle, the GP is effectively the seller, the deal architect, and the party who set the last carrying value. Independent fairness opinions are required by most fund documents, but the secondary buyers providing the clearing price are the same pool of capital that needs to deploy $154 billion in the back half of this year. That is not a buyer with the luxury of walking away. It is a buyer with a fundraising pitch that requires active deployment metrics.

The capital overhang number — 1.0x — is the tell that the Evercore report buries in the footnotes but should be the headline. Historically, secondary buyers have operated with an overhang closer to 1.5x to 2.0x, meaning they had one and a half to two years of runway before needing to raise again. That cushion gave them the credibility to be selective. At 1.0x, the marginal secondary fund is essentially a rolling vehicle — raise, deploy, raise again — which compresses their ability to hold out for better entry prices or reject structures that don't meet a genuine return threshold. The buyer discipline that made secondaries a respected asset class starts to erode precisely when volume is highest and everyone is congratulating themselves.

There is a historical parallel worth naming. The CLO market in 2006 and 2007 also reported record issuance, record demand, and a narrative about sophisticated institutional buyers pricing risk efficiently. The record issuance was real. The sophisticated pricing was not. The structural similarity is not that secondaries are about to blow up — the leverage profiles are incomparable — but that record volume is being used as proof of market health when it is at least partially proof of market circularity. GPs need exits. Secondary funds need deployment. LPs need DPI — the actual cash distributions that prove a fund worked, not just paper markups. All three parties have short-term incentives to transact, and none of them has an obvious incentive to slow down and ask whether the price is real. The allocator sitting across from their GP at the re-up meeting is the only one without a conflict. They should act like it.

Private Markets GP Stakes Blue Owl Fundraising Secondaries

Blue Owl missed its own GP stakes target by 18%. The press release called it sustained interest.

  • Blue Owl Capital — a $270 billion alternatives manager and the dominant player in GP stakes investing, where firms buy minority ownership in private equity firms to clip their fee income — raised $10.6 billion against a $13 billion target for its sixth fund, a shortfall of roughly $2.4 billion after multiple deadline extensions.
  • Blue Owl's own related-article feed surfaced two tell-tale headlines simultaneously: one about buying out its own LPs looking to exit, another asking whether continuation vehicles are the answer to GP stakes exit challenges.
  • Median PE fund closes now take 22-plus months, and first-time fund launches are collapsing — meaning the underlying GP businesses Blue Owl owns stakes in are growing AUM far more slowly than when the strategy was underwritten.

The take

Blue Owl just told you its sixth GP stakes fund is closing at $10.6 billion against a $13 billion target — an 18% miss, after multiple timeline extensions, from the firm that essentially invented institutional-scale GP stakes. The headline is "sustained investor interest." The number says something less flattering: the flagship strategy of a $270 billion alternatives manager couldn't hit its own goal in a market where LPs are supposedly starved for the exact liquidity this fund provides. That gap is the story, and nobody's writing it.

Here's the tell buried in the related-articles list on Blue Owl's own release: "Blue Owl to Snap Up Stakes From Fund Backers Looking to Cash Out" and "Are CVs the answer to GP stakes exit challenges?" Read those two lines together. The GP stakes model was sold as permanent capital clipping a coupon on management fees forever. But the LPs in the earlier GP stakes funds now want out, and there's no natural exit — you can't IPO a minority interest in Vista or Silver Lake at a clean multiple, and selling it to a strategic is a decade-long orphan process. So the exit mechanism being floated is continuation vehicles: Blue Owl selling stakes from its own old funds into its own new vehicles. That's not a liquidity strategy. That's a manager marking its way out of a maturity mismatch.

The second-order effect nobody is pricing: GP stakes was a bet that private-market fee streams would compound indefinitely. That bet was underwritten in the zero-rate era when every buyout shop was raising a bigger Fund N every 30 months. That flywheel has stalled — median PE funds take 22-plus months to close and first-time funds are collapsing. When the underlying GPs stop growing AUM, the "growth capital for GPs" thesis inverts. Blue Owl isn't buying into compounding fee machines anymore; increasingly it's buying into mature businesses whose fee base is flat and whose carry is stuck in aging vintages that can't distribute.

The read-across for allocators is uncomfortable, because Blue Owl trades as a proxy for the whole permanent-capital religion Marc Rowan and the big four are building. If the premier GP stakes franchise misses target by 18% and is quietly building a secondaries desk to buy out its own LPs, the asset class isn't a durable coupon — it's a levered bet on private markets never slowing down, dressed up as diversification. That's fine at the top of a cycle. It is a genuinely bad place to be holding illiquid minority stakes when fundraising stays cold for three more years.

The polite word is "final stretch." The honest word is: they ran out of buyers at $10.6 billion and stopped calling it a shortfall.

The other side

GP stakes is still a structurally defensible asset class: Blue Owl raised $10.6 billion in a genuinely difficult fundraising environment, which would be a landmark close for almost any other fund on earth. The exit problem is real but early — most LP commitments in Blue Owl's older vehicles are not yet at their natural liquidity horizon, and the continuation vehicle structure, while imperfect, is a tested mechanism that has worked in secondaries for over a decade. A single fund missing target during a prolonged rate-driven LP squeeze is not the same as a broken model.

The GP stakes concept was elegant when it launched. Blue Owl and a handful of competitors — HarbourVest, Dyal before its merger into Blue Owl, Petershill at Goldman — would buy 10 to 20 percent of an established private equity firm, collecting a share of that firm's management fees in perpetuity. Management fees are contractual, recession-resistant, and grow automatically as the GP raises bigger funds. In a zero-rate world, where every buyout shop was reliably graduating to a larger fund every two to three years, you were essentially buying a toll road with a built-in growth escalator. The model looked like infrastructure dressed up as private equity.

The problem is that the growth escalator assumption is now empirically shaky. The big established GPs — Vista, Silver Lake, Thoma Bravo — are still raising capital, but the pace has slowed and the competition for LP dollars has intensified sharply. Smaller and mid-market GPs, the ones Blue Owl bought into in its earlier vintages when valuations were more attractive, are facing genuine fundraising distress. When an underlying GP's Fund VI takes 28 months to close at 80 percent of target, Blue Owl's stake in that firm isn't compounding — it's going sideways on the fee line and hoping carry from older vintages eventually realizes. Carry, unlike management fees, is neither contractual nor predictable, and it requires exits. Exits require either IPO markets or strategic buyers, both of which have been effectively closed for two years.

The continuation vehicle angle deserves more scrutiny than it's getting. Blue Owl's debut GP-led strategy — a fund that helps GPs move assets from old vehicles into new ones — closed above three billion dollars, according to its own release. That is a real business. But there is a conflict sitting in plain sight: if Blue Owl is both the holder of GP stakes in a firm and the arranger of that firm's continuation vehicle, it is sitting on multiple sides of the same transaction. Regulators and LP advisory committees have started pushing back on exactly this structure elsewhere in the market, and it is only a matter of time before the question gets asked loudly of Blue Owl specifically.

The broader read-across matters because Blue Owl is not just a fund manager — it trades publicly and has spent three years marketing itself as a permanent-capital compounder, the fee-stream-forever story that Marc Rowan at Apollo and Steve Schwarzman at Blackstone have also built their investor narratives around. When the flagship strategy of that narrative misses target by 18 percent and the firm quietly stands up a secondaries desk to solve its own LP liquidity problem, it is worth asking whether the permanent-capital pitch was always partly a rebranding of illiquidity risk. The coupon is real. The permanence was always contingent on private markets continuing to grow. That contingency is now being tested, and the $2.4 billion gap in Blue Owl's sixth fund is the first clean data point showing what happens when it fails.

Family Office & Wealth Planning China Tax Policy Trust Structuring Family Office

China just sent a collection notice to every offshore trust with mainland money in it. The deadline is October 22.

  • China's new rules impose a 20% individual income tax on Chinese tax residents who fund, control, or receive distributions from any trust not established under Chinese law — effective retroactively to 2021, with unpaid amounts due by October 22, 2026.
  • The 'primary economic interests' residency test means a US citizen living in New York whose wealth derives from mainland China operations can be deemed a Chinese tax resident, regardless of passport or physical address.
  • There is currently no foreign tax credit mechanism between China's individual income tax and US federal tax, meaning the same asset gain can be taxed in full by both countries — as illustrated by a $15M zero-basis asset transfer triggering a $3M Chinese tax bill with no US offset.

The take

On July 24, China's Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration quietly issued the most aggressive piece of cross-border tax law nobody in Singapore is talking about: a 20% individual income tax on Chinese residents who fund, control, or receive distributions from offshore trusts — meaning any trust not established under Chinese law. A Delaware dynasty trust set up by a Shanghai-born green card holder is now squarely in scope. And it's retroactive to 2021, with a report-and-pay deadline of October 22, 2026. That last detail is the tell. This isn't a policy trial balloon. It's a collection notice with a due date.

The consensus read is that this is a China problem for China-domiciled families, solved by the US-China treaty tie-breaker. That's lazy. The dangerous phrase in the rules is "primary economic interests derived from within China." That's a residency test based on where your money comes from, not where you sleep. A US citizen living in New York whose fortune traces back to a mainland manufacturing empire or a Hong Kong-listed holdco can be deemed a Chinese tax resident regardless of passport. The treaty tie-breaker requires the "competent authorities" of both countries to actually cooperate on your case — and if you think Beijing and a second-Trump-term IRS are going to sit down and amicably allocate taxing rights on your family's wealth, you haven't been reading the room since 2018.

Here's the structural point nobody is pricing. There is currently no foreign tax credit mechanism bridging the IIT and US tax. The article's own example is brutal: transfer $15M of zero-basis assets to a Delaware trust, pay China 20% on the built-in gain today with no US event, then pay US tax again on the same gain when it's sold. That's double taxation by design, not by accident. Beijing built a regime that punishes the exact structure the entire offshore wealth-planning industry has been selling to wealthy Chinese families for two decades. Every trustee in Singapore, Jersey, and South Dakota holding assets for a settlor with mainland economic ties just became a compliance liability.

Follow the incentive. This rule does two things at once. It taxes the flight capital that's already left, and it makes leaving prospectively expensive — the exit tax on relinquishing Chinese residency crystallizes all built-in gains. That is capital controls dressed as a trust code. For allocators, the read-across is that the pool of deployable mainland-linked family office capital just got smaller, stickier, and legally radioactive to onboard without a full source-of-wealth and residency forensic.

The families who win are the ones who restructure before October 22 and eat the basis step-up. The ones who wait for "additional guidance" — which the article politely notes is needed everywhere — are the ones who'll be litigating this in two jurisdictions at once. Guidance is not coming before the deadline. That's the point.

The other side

The US-China Income Tax Treaty tie-breaker provision exists precisely for situations like this, and sophisticated families with competent legal counsel in both jurisdictions will use it to establish clear non-Chinese residency before the rules fully bite. Beyond that, the New Rules themselves acknowledge that substantial additional guidance is needed across nearly every operative provision — distributions from multi-beneficiary trusts, sourcing rules for nonresident settlors, what 'inheriting' a trust even means at death — and regulators who issue rules with this many open questions rarely enforce aggressively before the interpretive framework is settled. The October deadline may be more opening bid than hard hammer.

Start with the historical parallel, because it's clarifying. The United States spent roughly fifteen years building FATCA — the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act, the 2010 law that forced foreign banks worldwide to report American account holders or face punishing withholding on US-source income. The architecture was aggressive, the extraterritorial reach was unprecedented, and the initial reaction from international advisors was that it couldn't possibly be enforced. It was. China watched that playbook carefully, and what landed on July 24 borrows its essential logic: define residency broadly, claim worldwide income, and make the cost of non-compliance higher than the cost of paying up.

The mechanism Beijing chose is more surgical than it first appears. By taxing the transfer of assets into a trust as a gain-recognition event, China effectively makes every contribution to an offshore structure a taxable exit from the mainland tax base. By then taxing annual trust income currently to the resident settlor — with no ability to carry forward capital losses or offset them against interest and dividends — they've made holding appreciated assets inside a foreign trust more expensive on an ongoing basis than simply holding them directly. And by crystallizing all built-in gains when a settlor relinquishes Chinese residency, they've installed an exit tax that mirrors, and in some scenarios exceeds, the US expatriation tax on covered expatriates. The entire structure is designed to make the offshore trust — the primary vehicle the Singapore and Jersey private banking industry has sold to mainland-linked wealthy families for twenty years — economically painful to maintain and prohibitively expensive to exit cleanly.

The double-taxation exposure deserves more attention than it's getting. The US taxes its citizens on worldwide income regardless of residence. China now taxes its residents on worldwide trust income regardless of where the trust is sited. For the dual-status individual — a US citizen or green card holder who is also deemed a Chinese tax resident under the 'primary economic interests' test — there is no credit mechanism that offsets one country's tax against the other's in most trust distribution scenarios. The treaty between the two countries contains a tie-breaker, but that tie-breaker requires both competent tax authorities to actively cooperate and reach a mutual agreement on residency. In the current geopolitical environment, betting your family's wealth structure on timely US-China administrative cooperation is not a plan. It is a hope.

For family offices and private banks onboarding new clients, the compliance surface just expanded dramatically. Any prospective client whose wealth has a meaningful connection to mainland China — operating businesses, real estate, equity stakes in Hong Kong-listed companies, historical ties to state-owned enterprise contracts — now requires a full source-of-wealth and tax residency forensic before assets move. Trustees in South Dakota, Singapore, and the Channel Islands who are already holding assets for settlors with mainland economic connections need to be asking whether those settlors have filed anything with Chinese tax authorities and whether any upcoming distributions could trigger a Chinese tax event the trustee is facilitating. That is not a theoretical risk. It is a current liability.

The families that come out ahead are the ones that move before October 22, accept the basis step-up the rules offer on previously transferred assets, pay the tax, and restructure around a clean residency determination going forward. Waiting for guidance that the rules themselves admit hasn't been written yet is a losing strategy — the deadline was set before the guidance existed on purpose.

ICONIQ Portfolio Companies AI Microsoft Anthropic Cloud

Microsoft's Anthropic gain is a number it built itself. The OpenAI write-down is the honest one.

  • Microsoft booked a $3.2B gain on its Anthropic stake in a single quarter — a position it took in November 2025 as part of a deal where Anthropic simultaneously agreed to buy $30B of Microsoft's Azure cloud services
  • The OpenAI mark, a $600M write-down this quarter, is the more independently derived number — Microsoft owns ~27% of OpenAI, a stake large enough to be triangulated by the broader market rather than set by a round Microsoft just participated in
  • On a full-year basis, Microsoft's OpenAI position generated a $5B gain and $0.67 in EPS — but in Q4 alone, Anthropic nearly matched that entire annual return in one disclosure Microsoft chose to surface voluntarily

The take

Microsoft's fiscal Q4 buried a number worth pulling out of the footnote: a $3.2 billion gain on its Anthropic stake in a single quarter, versus a $600 million write-down on OpenAI. On a full-year basis OpenAI still delivered a $5 billion gain, so the headline is directionally fine. But the fact that Microsoft chose to disclose the Anthropic mark at all — when it doesn't routinely revalue that position quarterly, unlike OpenAI — is the tell. Companies don't voluntarily surface a number unless they want you to draw a conclusion from it.

Here's the conclusion Satya Nadella wants you to draw: Microsoft is no longer a one-lab bet. The $5 billion it put into Anthropic in November 2025 came bundled with Anthropic's commitment to buy $30 billion of Azure. That's the same circular structure the whole industry now runs on — the investor books a gain on the equity while the investee routes the cash straight back through the investor's cloud. The $3.2 billion "gain" and the $30 billion Azure commitment are the same dollars wearing two different accounting costumes. Microsoft is marking to a valuation that Microsoft's own capital helped set. This isn't investing; it's vendor financing with a mark-to-market kicker.

The second-order read that nobody is pricing: the OpenAI write-down is the more honest number, and Microsoft knows it. Microsoft owns roughly 27% of OpenAI, a position large enough that the mark actually reflects something like independent reality — and reality said down $600 million this quarter. The Anthropic position is small, illiquid, and freshly struck against a round Microsoft participated in, which is exactly the kind of asset you can mark up 64% and call it a gain. When the confident, precise number is on the position you helped price last November and the soft number is on the position you've held long enough to be triangulated by the market, the story is being managed, not reported.

For an allocator, the read-across is about how to treat every AI mark on a balance sheet right now. Nvidia into OpenAI, OpenAI into Oracle, Anthropic into Azure, Microsoft into Anthropic — the entire frontier stack is a set of interlocking commitments where each party's revenue is another party's capex and each party's equity gain is another party's spend. These marks are not independent observations; they are the same money counted three times. When it unwinds, it won't unwind on one balance sheet — it unwinds across all of them simultaneously, because the collateral is circular.

The practical instruction: discount AI-lab equity gains reported by strategic investors by the size of the attached commercial commitment. A $3.2 billion gain that arrives with a $30 billion purchase order is not a gain. It's a rebate you paid for yourself.

The other side

The circular-capital critique assumes the Anthropic valuation is purely self-referential, but Anthropic is also being marked by other arms-length investors who don't have Azure commitments attached — if the broader round supports the valuation, Microsoft's mark isn't necessarily manufactured. And the $30B Azure commitment is a real contract with real revenue consequences; Anthropic buying cloud compute it actually needs isn't structurally different from any large enterprise deal where an investor also becomes a customer. Calling it circular financing may understate the genuine commercial logic underneath.

The accounting maneuver at the center of this story isn't new, but the scale is. What Microsoft has constructed with Anthropic is a version of what the oil majors used to do with pipeline companies — you invest in the infrastructure your own product flows through, book the equity appreciation as a gain, and collect the throughput fees as revenue. The circularity is real, but it's also legal, common, and in some ways rational. What makes the AI version unusual is the speed at which the marks move and the degree to which every major player in the frontier stack is doing the same thing at the same time.

Consider what the full loop looks like. Microsoft puts $5 billion into Anthropic. Anthropic commits to spending $30 billion on Azure. Microsoft marks its Anthropic stake up based partly on a valuation set in the round it just led. The gain on that stake — $3.2 billion in one quarter — flows through earnings and boosts Microsoft's stock, which makes future capital raises cheaper, which lets Microsoft write more of these deals. Meanwhile Anthropic uses Azure credits it effectively received from Microsoft to train models, which it then sells back to enterprise customers, some of whom are also Microsoft customers running on Azure. The money is not exactly the same dollars counted three times; it is more like the same dollars creating the appearance of three separate economic events when the underlying risk exposure is closer to one.

The detail that should make allocators pause is which position Microsoft chose to revalue and which it kept opaque. Microsoft updates its OpenAI stake every quarter — it has to, because the position is large enough and old enough that the market has independent views on what OpenAI is worth. The Anthropic position Microsoft does not routinely mark. It surfaced the $3.2 billion number this quarter as a voluntary disclosure. Voluntary disclosures are rarely neutral; CFOs surface favorable numbers when they want a narrative to land. The quarter that gets the big Anthropic win announced just happens to be the same quarter where OpenAI went the other direction. That sequencing is not an accident.

The deeper structural issue is what happens when these arrangements begin to normalize on every major balance sheet simultaneously. Nvidia has equity stakes in AI labs whose primary compute spend flows back to Nvidia. OpenAI has invested in companies that depend on OpenAI's own models. Anthropic's valuation is supported in part by Microsoft's commitment, which is supported in part by Anthropic's commitment, and so on. None of these are fraudulent in isolation. Together, they mean that a stress event at any single node — a major customer pulling Azure spend, a lab failing to hit a capability milestone that justifies its valuation, a regulator deciding that circular investment structures constitute related-party transactions requiring different disclosure — does not stay contained. The collateral isn't diversified. It's the same bet expressed in six different accounting line items across six different balance sheets.

For anyone trying to read AI-lab health from public earnings, the practical discipline is simple: treat any equity gain on a position where the investee has a large commercial commitment to the investor as partially offset by that commitment. The Anthropic gain is real in the sense that the marks say so. It is not real in the sense that Microsoft could sell the position tomorrow at that price without moving the market that priced it.

Wildcard Sports FIFA Private Equity

FIFA is selling the toll booth. The Kushners are first in line.

  • FIFA's proposal would spin off its commercial operations to a private vehicle with Thrive Capital — the venture fund run by Josh Kushner, brother of Jared Kushner — as lead investor
  • FIFA books roughly $11 billion per four-year World Cup cycle, the majority tied to the men's tournament, run by a 211-member association structurally built to distribute money rather than maximize it
  • UEFA, European football's governing body representing 55 member associations, voted unanimously to boycott the World Cup if the plan proceeds — but the next men's World Cup is four years away, and the first real pressure test is the far lower-revenue women's U-20 tournament in September

The take

FIFA published a proposal this week to spin off part of its commercial operations into a private vehicle, with Thrive Capital — Josh Kushner's fund, brother of Jared — as the lead investor. UEFA responded by voting unanimously to boycott the World Cup if it goes through. The headline reads as a governance fight. It's actually a fight over who captures the terminal value of the single most-watched recurring event on Earth, and the Kushner name is the tell that tells you everyone already knows the answer.

Here's what's actually going on. The World Cup is a wildly under-monetized asset sitting inside a non-profit-shaped Swiss association. FIFA books roughly $11B per four-year cycle, most of it lumped into the men's tournament, run by a body structurally incapable of maximizing it because it has to buy the loyalty of 211 member associations with development handouts. Any competent allocator looks at that and sees an obvious carve-out: take the crown jewel, professionalize the rights sales, streaming, and sponsorship stack, and re-rate the multiple. Thrive isn't buying football. It's buying the arbitrage between how a bureaucracy prices the World Cup and how a private owner would.

UEFA's "the World Cup is not for sale" line is not principle, it's protection of an existing rent. Europe has won most men's World Cups since 1930 and, more importantly, its clubs and leagues sit on top of the global talent and broadcast pyramid. A privatized FIFA commercial arm optimizing for global revenue means more tournaments, more calendar congestion, and value migrating away from the UEFA-controlled club game toward FIFA-controlled events. That's the real threat. This is Ligue and Premier League economics defending their turf against a new capital structure that would compete for the same finite hours of attention and the same players' legs.

The boycott threat is weaker than it reads, and the timing proves it. The next men's World Cup is four years out. The pressure point they've identified is the women's U-20 World Cup in September — a rounding error in revenue terms. You threaten with the small event because you can't credibly threaten with the big one: no European federation is walking away from 2026 or 2030 broadcast money, and Infantino knows it. Watch instead for the opposition candidate senior UEFA officials are quietly floating against him next year. That's the actual mechanism.

The read-across for allocators: sports rights are being institutionalized as an asset class in real time — Arctos, RedBird, Silver Lake, now Thrive reaching for the governing body itself. The frontier isn't buying a club or a league anymore. It's buying the toll booth. Whoever privatizes the World Cup's commercial engine owns a cashflow with no real substitute and a captive global audience. The EU competition probe won't kill that logic. It just sets the entry price.

The other side

There's a real case that FIFA's commercial operations are genuinely underdeveloped and that private capital could unlock value that benefits the whole sport — better broadcast infrastructure, expanded streaming reach in underpenetrated markets, and a more professional sponsorship stack. If the deal is structured with revenue-sharing back to member associations and development funds, many of FIFA's 211 members — especially smaller football nations that depend on those handouts — may have more to gain from a higher-performing commercial engine than from preserving the status quo. UEFA's veto threat could leave the vote to a majority that sees this differently.

Say more →

The structural story here is bigger than one deal. Sports rights have been moving toward institutionalization as a formal asset class for most of this decade. Arctos Partners, a sports-focused private equity firm, has taken minority stakes in NFL and NBA franchises. RedBird Capital, another sports-focused fund, owns stakes in AC Milan and the French media group Canal+. Silver Lake, the large-cap tech-focused buyout firm, bought into the City Football Group and New Zealand Rugby. Each step in that progression has moved further up the value chain — from a single team, to a league stake, to a governing body's commercial engine. Thrive reaching for FIFA is the logical endpoint of that progression: stop buying individual assets and buy the entity that sets the rules for all of them.

The reason FIFA is even susceptible to this is structural. It is formally a non-profit Swiss association, which means it is constitutionally oriented toward distributing money to its 211 members rather than compounding it. Every four years it runs what is effectively the highest-revenue single sporting event on Earth, prices the rights conservatively enough to preserve political peace among its members, and then hands most of the proceeds back out as development grants to keep those members loyal to the incumbent leadership. That is not how you maximize the terminal value of an asset. It is how you maintain a political coalition. Any outside investor who spots that gap sees the same trade: extract the commercial rights from the political structure, run them like a media and events business, and re-rate the multiple accordingly.

The EU competition probe, flagged by the European Commissioner for Sport this week, is worth watching but probably not fatal to the underlying logic. EU competition law could theoretically challenge arrangements that distort the market for broadcast rights or lock out competing tournament structures, and a privatized FIFA commercial arm optimizing aggressively for revenue would likely run into those questions eventually. But investigations take years, the legal theory is genuinely novel, and the entry price for Thrive gets set well before any ruling. The probe functions more as a negotiating lever for European football than as a structural block.

The deeper read-across is about what it means when the governing body itself becomes the acquisition target rather than any individual property inside it. A privatized World Cup commercial engine is a cash flow with no real substitutes — there is no second product competing for the same global audience on the same scale — and a captive distribution network in the form of mandatory broadcast deals across every major territory. That is a remarkably clean asset profile by private markets standards, and it explains why the Kushner connection, whatever the politics around it, is not incidental. This is not a speculative bet on football growing. It is a bet that someone competent will eventually professionalize an asset that has been deliberately kept small by the people running it.
