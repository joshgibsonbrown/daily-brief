Private Markets Private Credit Secondaries GP-Led

Ares moved $3.6 billion in private credit into a new vehicle where the loans never have to clear.

privatecrediteurope.com, inforcapital.com, convergentassets.com
  • Ares closed a €3.3bn ($3.6bn) credit continuation fund — its largest — with Coller EQT as lead investor and Evercore advising, shut in early November.
  • The underlying portfolio, the funds it came from, and the terms offered to existing LPs were all undisclosed; pricing was set by the manager and the lead buyer together.
  • Twenty-one of 56 secondaries signals tracked in the past 30 days were GP-led continuation funds — a format that originated in private equity but is now moving aggressively into private credit.

The take

This is the eighth or ninth time this section has touched secondaries and continuation vehicles in three weeks, so the pattern is well established. What's new in this batch: the model has crossed decisively into credit. Ares closed a €3.3bn ($3.6bn) credit continuation fund — its largest — with Coller EQT writing the lead ticket, Evercore advising, closed in early November. Add Pantheon's $6bn flagship close, Blackstone's $3bn real estate secondaries target, and Coller's reported $17bn platform raise, and you have the machinery of secondaries quietly rebuilding itself around assets that never traded at all.

Here is the part worth dwelling on. In equity continuation funds, you are moving a company — a thing with comparable transactions, EBITDA, and the occasional strategic buyer to price against. Ares is moving loans. The source is explicit that the underlying portfolio, the funds it came from, and the LP terms were all undisclosed, and that "investors who sell are relying on the manager and the lead buyer to set a fair value for loans that are rarely traded." That is the whole deal in one sentence. The GP who originated the loan, the GP rolling it, and the buyer leading the price are either the same house or its closest counterparties. Coller EQT backing a portfolio it did not originate, at a mark it is helping set, is not arm's-length price discovery. It is two large firms agreeing on a number and calling the agreement a market.

This matters more in credit than equity for a mechanical reason. A mismarked buyout eventually meets an exit and the gap prints. A performing private loan can be rolled from vehicle to vehicle indefinitely as long as it keeps paying coupon — and a loan that is being extended through a continuation fund is, by definition, one the manager chose not to let mature. We flagged on October 26 the documented, systematic gap between PE NAV marks and actual exit prices. Credit CVs are the structure that lets you avoid ever testing the mark. The loan never has to clear.

The framing being sold — "standard tool for the largest direct lenders," credit secondaries going mainstream — is the same liquidity-dressed-as-strategy story this section has traced since the $226bn volume record. The honest version: a $4 trillion backlog of unexited private assets now includes a private credit book that raised aggressively in 2021-2023 and is reaching end-of-life with loans still outstanding. Continuation funds are how you avoid declaring whether those loans are money-good. For the selling LP, €3.3bn of liquidity is real. For everyone rolling, the question of what the paper is actually worth has simply been deferred to the next vehicle.

Watch who leads the next three credit CVs. If it keeps being the same handful of secondaries shops cross-backing each other's originations, this is not a market deepening. It is a closed loop of marks, and the first genuine credit cycle — not a rate wobble, a real default wave — is what finally forces the price.

The other side

The bear case on credit continuation funds assumes the loans are worse than marked, but the Ares portfolio is described as performing — loans still paying coupon, not distressed paper being quietly buried. Coller EQT has spent three decades buying secondhand private market exposure and has every incentive to price conservatively, because overpaying on a lead ticket at this scale is an institutional embarrassment. The LP selling into the vehicle gets real liquidity at a moment when distributions across private markets have been slower than expected; that is a genuine service, not a fiction. If the loans keep performing through the new vehicle's life and exit at or above the continuation price, the closed-loop pricing concern turns out to be noise.

The Ares deal does not exist in isolation. Private Credit Europe tracked a string of credit continuation vehicles closing this year — Partners Group, Bridgepoint with Pantheon, Eurazeo, KKR with Allianz Global Investors, PGIM with LGT — and noted that none of those came close to €3.3bn. The Ares close is not just the largest credit CV on record; it is large enough to function as the reference point that sets expectations for every deal that follows. When a structure this size closes without disclosing the underlying portfolio or LP terms, it does not just set a size benchmark — it sets a disclosure benchmark too.

The mechanism deserves a plain-language walk-through, because the conflict structure is more layered in credit than in equity. In a standard GP-led equity continuation fund, a private equity sponsor moves a portfolio company into a new vehicle. The company has revenues, EBITDA, and occasionally a strategic acquirer willing to bid — external anchors that constrain how far the mark can drift. In a credit continuation fund, the asset is a loan. Loans are priced off models: default probability, recovery assumptions, spread to benchmark. The manager who originated the loan built those models. The manager rolling the loan into the continuation vehicle is typically the same firm. The lead buyer setting the transaction price — here, Coller EQT — is a sophisticated counterparty, but it is working from the originator's data on a portfolio that was never publicly traded. That is not a corrupt process, but it is not arm's-length price discovery either. It is two large institutions negotiating a number and then calling the result a market transaction.

The timing of this wave is not accidental. Private credit raised aggressively through 2021 and 2023, when rates were low and direct lending spreads were wide enough to attract enormous LP capital. Those vintages are now approaching the end of their contractual lives with loans still outstanding — loans the manager chose to extend rather than let mature or sell into the secondary loan market at whatever price a real buyer would pay. Continuation funds are the structural answer to that maturity wall. They let the manager keep the fee-earning asset, give selling LPs a liquidity exit, and defer the moment when the loan's actual value has to be tested against an external bid.

Coller Capital's reported $17 billion platform raise — the source describes it as covering flagship secondary investment strategies with commitments from pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and insurers — shows how much dedicated secondaries capital is now chasing exactly this kind of deal. That capital needs to deploy. When the pool of buyers is large and the pool of sellers is concentrated among a handful of the largest direct lenders, the pricing dynamic runs in the seller's favor. This is the same compression dynamic this section flagged in October when secondary funds had raised a record $139.4 billion and were sitting on dry powder that needed a home.

The equity continuation fund market took roughly a decade to develop the governance norms, LP protections, and pricing discipline that make it function reasonably well. Credit CVs are compressing that learning curve into two or three years, at deal sizes that dwarf the early equity precedents. ILPA's continuation fund guidance — which this section covered in October — was written with equity structures in mind. Whether it applies cleanly to a €3.3bn portfolio of leveraged loans and direct lending positions is an open question the guidance does not answer.

ICONIQ Portfolio Companies Fintech Stripe Lending

Stripe bought Parafin to lend inside platforms it doesn't process payments for.

dealroom.co, fintechlabs.com
  • Parafin has originated over $3 billion to 60,000 small and midsize US businesses through embedded lending inside platforms including DoorDash, Amazon, and Gusto — none of which are core Stripe payment customers.
  • Only 41% of US small-business loan applications were approved in 2025, down 18 percentage points from 2015, creating the structural gap Stripe is positioning to fill.
  • Parafin had raised $190 million in total funding; no acquisition price was disclosed, marking this as a capability acquisition rather than a landmark exit.

The take

Stripe closed its acquisition of Parafin on November 11, folding an embedded small-business lender that has originated over $3 billion to 60,000 businesses into Stripe Capital. No price disclosed. Parafin's own backers are listed at $190 million raised, which tells you this was not a landmark acquisition by dollar value — it's a capability tuck-in. The interesting part is what capability.

The framing everywhere is "Stripe deepens embedded finance." True but shallow. What Stripe actually bought is a distribution graph it did not own. Parafin's loans run through DoorDash, Amazon and Gusto — platforms where Stripe isn't the payment rail and never will be. Stripe Capital historically could only lend to businesses already inside Stripe's own funnel. Parafin lends to businesses inside everyone else's funnel. That turns Stripe from a lender to its own merchants into a credit underwriter-of-record across platforms it doesn't process payments for. The 18,000-platform number in the press release is a vanity metric; the DoorDash and Amazon relationships are the asset.

Here's the mechanism that matters. When you underwrite a loan off a merchant's real-time sales data and collect repayment as a percentage of future revenue, the lending risk is low precisely because you see the cash flow before anyone else does. Parafin sees DoorDash sales data. That data does not belong to Stripe — it belongs to DoorDash, which now has a credit product powered by a company its own competitor-adjacent payments giant just bought. Amazon lending its sellers money through infrastructure Stripe owns is a strange arrangement that gets re-examined at the next contract renewal. The quiet risk in this deal isn't integration; it's that the crown-jewel platform relationships were built when Parafin was a neutral Switzerland. They're now owned by the largest payments company on earth, and large platforms do not love routing their merchant lending through a strategic partner with ambitions on their own payment volume.

The macro backdrop is real and worth the trade. Stripe's own figure — 41% of US small-business loan applications approved in 2025, down from the 2015 run-rate — describes a structural retreat by banks from sub-$250k business credit. Someone underwrites that gap, and the party with live transaction data underwrites it best. Embedded lending against cash-flow visibility is genuinely a better mousetrap than a bank pulling a stale credit file. That's why the sector is hot: Imprint just raised at roughly $1.9 billion on $400 million of expected 2026 revenue, and the embedded-finance league table now runs to a dozen unicorns.

What to watch is whether Stripe keeps the platforms or keeps the product. If DoorDash, Amazon and Gusto stay on through the integration, this was a cheap way to buy a credit book and a distribution channel banks can't replicate. If one of them starts shopping for a new neutral provider within eighteen months, Stripe paid for a technology stack and inherited a renewal problem. The underwriting edge is durable. The partnerships are the thing that can walk.

The other side

The platform-relationship risk cuts both ways. DoorDash, Amazon, and Gusto chose Parafin precisely because it was independent — a neutral infrastructure provider with no stake in their payment volumes or merchant relationships. Those platforms are sophisticated enough to have negotiated contract terms that protect them, and switching costs for embedded lending are real: re-underwriting 60,000 merchants on a new system is not a weekend project. The more grounded read is that Stripe inherits stable, sticky relationships and the platforms stay put because the product works and the migration pain isn't worth it.

Say more →

The structural retreat of banks from small-business credit is the foundation this deal is built on, and it's been underway for a decade. Stripe's own data puts the 2025 approval rate at 41%, down 18 percentage points from 2015 — a gap that didn't open because small businesses got riskier. It opened because the regulatory capital treatment of sub-$250k commercial loans made them uneconomical for large banks to originate manually. The party that fills that gap isn't the one with the best credit model in the abstract; it's the one with the best real-time data. Parafin's underwriting runs off live sales figures from the platforms it sits inside, which means it sees cash flow before a borrower even applies. That is a structurally different risk posture than a bank pulling a quarterly tax return and a stale credit file.

The embedded-finance market has been pricing this advantage aggressively. Imprint — a startup that helps brands issue co-branded credit cards and manage loyalty programs — just raised at roughly $1.9 billion on $400 million of expected 2026 revenue, up 150% year over year, according to the Fintech Labs stream. The broader embedded-finance unicorn count now runs to at least a dozen companies, per New Market Pitch's current ranking. Stripe is not buying into a niche; it is buying into the fastest-growing distribution channel in consumer and small-business credit.

The integration question is more interesting than the technology question. Parafin's product — a single API integration that lets any platform offer pre-approved financing, funded in as little as a day, with repayments that flex with sales — is not complicated to fold into Stripe Capital. Stripe already runs a financing product for its own merchants; Parafin's underwriting logic and data pipelines are the incremental piece. What is not simple is the commercial relationship with platforms that now find their lending infrastructure owned by a company that also wants to process their payments. DoorDash and Amazon both run substantial in-house payments operations. Amazon, in particular, has its own lending history with marketplace sellers. The question at the next contract renewal is whether those platforms treat Parafin-inside-Stripe as a trusted infrastructure vendor or as a Trojan horse for a competitor's expansion ambitions.

Stripe's 18,000-platform figure is the right context for the long-term opportunity but the wrong frame for evaluating this deal's immediate value. The DoorDash, Amazon, and Gusto relationships are what Stripe actually paid for, because those are the platforms with the merchant density and transaction volume to make embedded lending economically meaningful at scale. Keeping all three through the integration period is the real test of whether this acquisition delivers on its strategic logic or simply adds a technology stack with a renewal problem attached.

Family Office & Wealth Planning Venture Capital Family Office Tax Strategy

Family offices chasing seed-stage returns are buying duration their governance cannot support

cnbc.com
  • Megadeals of $100M-plus absorbed 87.5% of U.S. venture capital deployed in the first half of 2026, per PitchBook
  • Shea Ventures — backed by the family whose construction company helped build the Golden Gate Bridge — has invested in venture capital since the late 1960s and screens specifically for funds whose LPs prioritize investment multiple over IRR
  • Blanck Capital, a family office started in 2024, holds Anthropic but describes pre-seed positions like Longshot Space Technology — which has since won contracts with the U.S. Air Force, DARPA, and the U.S. Navy — as the actual return engine

The take

A handful of family offices — Shea Ventures, Jason Blanck's year-old shop — are telling CNBC they'd rather write seed checks into garbage-collecting space robots than crowd into the $100M-plus AI rounds that now soak up 87.5% of US venture dollars. The story reads like a quaint contrarian flex. It's actually the cleanest statement yet of why family capital and institutional capital are structurally incompatible buyers of the same asset, and why that gap is about to widen.

John Morrissey said the quiet part plainly: he screens for funds whose LPs prioritize the multiple over IRR. That's not a style preference, it's a tax and duration arbitrage. A sovereign fund or an endowment lives on annualized IRR because it has spending obligations, benchmark comparisons, and a career-length clock. A family office compounding across generations wants the money to stay invested and untaxed — every forced distribution is a realization event and a reinvestment problem. So the family office is a natural holder of exactly the illiquid, slow-to-exit, tax-deferred position that everyone else is now desperate to get out of.

Hold that against what this section has been tracking all autumn. 47% of offices that tried to exit a private-markets position this year couldn't. 76% cite the exit logjam as their top threat — and keep raising PE allocations anyway. The late-stage AI megaround is the apex of that trap: you pay peak multiples, as Blanck says, for slower growth and a liquidity event that may be years past your fund's life. The institutions writing those checks need the exit. The families don't. Which means when the AI window narrows, the marginal buyer of a $300M secondary is a family office that can afford to be patient and will demand a brutal discount for it.

Here's the part the barbell crowd is underselling. Blanck owns Anthropic and volunteers that it won't move his needle — the signal purchase sits in the portfolio precisely so it can be talked about, while the actual return engine is pre-seed positions nobody will dinner-party you about. That's an honest description of how most family venture programs really work: a liquid public-equity core, a small illiquid sleeve, and a social overlay that quietly degrades returns. The discipline isn't going early. It's going early and not letting the brand-name late-stage round creep from 5% of the book to 30% because it's the only thing your peers want to discuss.

The uncomfortable read: the smaller-check strategy only works for the offices that already have governance to hold through a decade of no distributions — and this section's own data says most don't. 54% hold key assets in the founder's name, 27% have a trust distribution policy, 18% have no succession plan at all. Going early into space guns is a fine trade if the structure can carry an illiquid position across a generational handoff. For the office where the founder is the plan, it's just adding duration risk to a book that can't survive the founder getting hit by a bus. Pick the trade that matches the plumbing you actually have.

The other side

The case for late-stage AI rounds isn't just herd behavior — it's that early-stage venture has its own brutal math. Most pre-seed funds return nothing, and the ones that do often take 12-plus years to do it, which is a long time to be wrong about a thesis. Shakti, the fund Shea Ventures backs, invests before companies have a product or revenue; that's not unconventional discipline, it's maximum binary risk. And the multiple-over-IRR preference only looks elegant in retrospect — if the AI window closes and the space-gun portfolio needs another five years of runway, the family office that skipped the liquid late-stage round has no hedge and no exit.

The structural argument Morrissey is making is more precise than it sounds. When he says he screens for LPs who prioritize the multiple over IRR, he is describing a tax architecture, not just a patience preference. An endowment or sovereign fund measures itself against an annualized benchmark, reports to a board on a calendar cycle, and has real spending obligations — university budgets, government programs — that require capital to come back on a schedule. Every year the money stays locked up is a year the institution is explaining an unfunded commitment to someone with a spreadsheet. The family office running a generational mandate has none of those clocks. A dollar that compounds inside a fund for twelve years without triggering a distribution is a dollar that never got taxed on the interim gains. That asymmetry is real and it is large, and it explains why the same illiquid asset is genuinely worth more to one type of buyer than the other.

The barbell structure Blanck describes — public equity core, small illiquid sleeve, early-stage direct investments — is the honest version of how most family venture programs actually operate. The public core provides liquidity and covers operating costs. The illiquid sleeve is where the generational compounding is supposed to happen. The problem is that the social gravity of late-stage brand-name rounds pulls capital from the illiquid sleeve toward positions that look like venture but behave like expensive growth equity. Blanck is explicit that Anthropic won't move his needle. What he is describing, without quite saying it, is that the Anthropic position exists partly as a social credential — something to discuss with peers — while Longshot Space Technology is where the actual multiple has to come from. That is an honest and fairly rare admission. Most family offices running the same book would not volunteer it.

Set that against the structural data this section has been tracking. Forty-seven percent of offices that tried to exit a private-markets position this year could not complete it. Seventy-six percent name the exit environment as their primary threat to returns, and they are still raising PE allocations. The late-stage AI megaround is the sharpest version of that trap: peak entry multiple, compressed growth relative to earlier stages, and a liquidity event that may arrive years after the fund's stated life. The institutions writing those checks need the exit to happen on schedule. A family office with genuine generational governance does not — which means when the AI funding window narrows and secondary prices reset, the patient family buyer becomes the marginal price-setter and can demand a significant discount.

The caveat the source material does not address, but this section's own autumn data makes unavoidable: the early-stage strategy only works for offices whose structure can actually hold an illiquid position across a decade and a generational handoff. Fifty-four percent of family offices hold key assets in the founder's personal name. Only twenty-seven percent have a trust distribution policy. Eighteen percent have no succession plan at all. Going pre-seed into a garbage-collecting space robotics company is a sound trade if the legal and governance plumbing can carry that position through a founder's death, a divorce, or a forced liquidity event. For the office where the founder is effectively the plan, adding a ten-year illiquid sleeve is adding duration risk to a structure that was already fragile. The discipline is not just going early. It is going early only after you have confirmed the architecture can survive the wait.

Tech & AI China Export Controls Nvidia Chips

China is buying Nvidia chips it can't legally have and training models that don't need them.

datacenters.economictimes.indiatimes.com, slicast.com
  • Reuters identified over $3 billion in Chinese computing projects specifying Nvidia H100 and H200 chips — the exact processors US export controls are designed to block — with one Jilin province project arranging bank loans for 80% of its 2.26 billion yuan cost.
  • Meituan released LongCat-2.5, a 1.6-trillion-parameter agent model trained entirely on Chinese domestic hardware with no Nvidia silicon involved.
  • Bernstein estimates domestic GPU share in China has passed 41% as of November 2026, meaning Nvidia still holds the majority — but the gray-market demand and the domestic training milestone are pulling in opposite directions.

The take

Two things landed in the same 48 hours that most people are reading as separate stories. Reuters tallied up more than $3 billion in Chinese computing projects that specify Nvidia H100 and H200 chips — the exact silicon US export controls are supposed to have cut off — while Meituan released weights for LongCat-2.5, a 1.6-trillion-parameter agent model trained entirely on Chinese hardware that never touched an Nvidia GPU. Read together, they describe a market hedging in both directions at once, and that hedge is the actual information.

Start with the smuggling. Tender documents in Fujian, Shaanxi, Gansu and Inner Mongolia naming H100s and H200s mean Chinese developers are still willing to pay a gray-market premium — and arrange bank loans for 80% of the cost, per the Jilin prospectus — for Nvidia's stack rather than buy domestic. That is not a vote of confidence in Huawei's Ascend line. It is the opposite: the people building these campuses still think the CUDA software moat is worth breaking the law for. Bernstein putting domestic GPU share past 41% is the number the bulls on Chinese self-sufficiency quote. The $3 billion of contraband is the number that says the remaining 59% is defended by the one asset export controls can't touch — a decade of developer tooling.

Now the counter-fact. If LongCat-2.5 actually trained a trillion-parameter-class model end to end without Nvidia silicon, the moat has a documented crossing point. You don't need parity. You need "good enough that I stop risking a Commerce Department letter to get the better chip." Every quarter of tighter curbs — and Cotton is now pushing Lutnick to ban CXMT outright — raises the gray-market premium and subsidizes exactly the domestic training runs Washington says it wants to prevent. Export control is functioning as an industrial policy for Huawei and Meituan, paid for by Nvidia's lost China revenue.

Which is the backdrop against which someone decided this was the week to float that Jensen Huang has resigned over a China espionage scandal, with CFO Colette Kress named interim CEO. Treat the resignation report as unconfirmed — it is sourced to a Titanium Media digest and a Livemint write-up, and Musk has reportedly signed a 91,000-name petition to reinstate him, which is not how confirmed CEO departures usually read. But the fact that a Huang-resignation story is even plausible, the day before earnings, tells you how radioactive the China revenue line has become. Nvidia's stock premium was never about this quarter's China sales; it was about China as a permanent growth lane. Controls are closing that lane, and the smuggling proves the demand is real while the LongCat release proves it's replaceable.

The position that's actually exposed is anyone underwriting Nvidia's valuation on Chinese structural dependence. The $3 billion of gray-market H100s is the last gasp of lock-in, not evidence of it. Each tightening turn of the screw buys Huang another quarter and buys Huawei another year.

The other side

The 41% domestic GPU share figure actually understates how fast Chinese alternatives are maturing: Huawei's Ascend line has been closing the performance gap on training workloads for two years, and LongCat-2.5 is evidence the gap is now crossable at scale. If you believe the remaining 59% Nvidia share reflects inertia and developer habit rather than genuine technical superiority, then the $3 billion of gray-market procurement is a lagging indicator — the last cohort of builders who haven't yet trusted domestic silicon — not proof that CUDA's moat is durable. Tighter controls accelerate the transition rather than prevent it, and Washington may be handing Huawei a captive market it couldn't have won on merit.

The mechanism worth understanding is how the two data points interact structurally rather than just sitting in tension. Gray-market procurement at scale — tender documents in Fujian, Shaanxi, Gansu, and Inner Mongolia naming specific Nvidia part numbers — requires a supply chain. Chips under US export controls don't teleport into Chinese data centers; they move through intermediaries, and those intermediaries charge for the risk. The Jilin prospectus detail about 80% bank financing is particularly revealing: Chinese developers are leveraging up to pay a gray-market premium for hardware they could theoretically replace with domestic alternatives. That is a strong revealed preference, and it tells you something the Bernstein share figure alone does not — that the buyers who know both options best are still choosing the more expensive and legally precarious one.

But LongCat-2.5 changes the calculus at the margin. Meituan — the Chinese food-delivery and commerce giant, which has been expanding aggressively into AI — releasing weights for a 1.6-trillion-parameter model trained without a single Nvidia GPU is not a benchmark result. It is a proof of concept for an entire procurement strategy. The question for any Chinese AI developer is no longer whether domestic hardware can train large models in theory; it is whether the quality gap justifies the gray-market premium plus the legal exposure plus the construction-delay risk that tighter controls create. Each incremental tightening — and Senator Cotton's push to ban CXMT, the Chinese memory chipmaker, suggests the tightening cycle is not done — raises the break-even point at which domestic silicon becomes the rational choice.

This is the dynamic that makes export controls self-undermining at the margin. Washington's stated goal is to prevent China from building frontier AI infrastructure. The actual effect, as the controls tighten, is to raise the cost of Nvidia dependence and directly subsidize the domestic training runs and chip development programs that would eliminate that dependence. Huawei's Ascend line did not reach 41% domestic GPU share because it was cheaper or better than Nvidia's H-series; it reached 41% because procurement of the alternative became legally and logistically difficult. Every percentage point of share that migrates to domestic silicon under duress is a percentage point that stays there even if controls loosen, because the software stack and the operational expertise follow the hardware.

The Nvidia leadership story — a Titanium Media digest and a Livemint write-up reporting Jensen Huang's resignation over a China espionage scandal, with CFO Colette Kress named interim CEO — is unconfirmed and should be read cautiously given the sourcing. But its timing is not random. It landed the day before earnings, when the China revenue line is the most sensitive number on Nvidia's income statement. Whether or not Huang has actually resigned, the fact that a story framing Nvidia's China exposure as a personal legal liability for its CEO is circulating at all reflects how thoroughly the narrative around that revenue has shifted. Nvidia's valuation was never priced on this quarter's China sales; it was priced on China as a durable, growing market. The gray-market $3 billion shows the demand is real. LongCat-2.5 shows it is becoming detachable from Nvidia specifically. Those two facts together are what the market has not yet fully priced.

Financial Markets & Economic News Treasuries AI Capex Credit

Treasuries are cushioning equities again. The cushion is built on a bear steepener.

am.jpmorgan.com, bbh.com, noodlesresearch.com
  • The 10-year Treasury yield rose 20bps on the week to 5.10% and the 2-year rose 28bps to 4.86%, even as bonds rallied on equity down-days — a bear steepener, not a flight-to-safety bull move
  • BBH cites analysis putting the GDP level boost from the entire data-center buildout at just 0.3% — the growth scare now driving money into bonds is a bet that AI capex won't pay off at the scale implied
  • Credit markets show no stress despite the rates carnage: IG and HY spreads remain compressed, the Fed's reverse repo facility is fully drained to zero, and the Fed balance sheet sits at $6.76 trillion with the prior liquidity buffer gone

The take

This is the eighth time we've been on the AI-capex-meets-bond-market story since September, and the frame has just inverted. Through October, hot inflation data kept producing dovish price action because tech selloffs sent safe-haven flows into Treasuries that capped yields. J.P. Morgan is now flagging that Treasuries have, for the past month, reliably rallied when equities drop more than 1% — the stock-bond correlation turning negative again after most of 2026 broke it. The old diversification trade is back. For about three weeks.

The reason this matters is what's driving it. BBH and Noodles Research tell the same story from opposite ends: the market isn't suddenly worried about inflation, it's worried about growth, and specifically about the AI buildout's growth payoff being smaller than the capex implies. BBH cites analysis putting the GDP level boost from the data-center boom — one of the largest peacetime investment expansions in US history — at just 0.3%. If that number is even roughly right, the entire capex cycle is a transfer of balance-sheet risk in exchange for a rounding error of output. That's the growth scare now sending money into bonds.

Here's where the comfortable read breaks down. The bond that's supposed to be cushioning you is itself being repriced violently. Noodles has the 10-year at 5.10%, up 20bps on the week, the 2-year up 28bps to 4.86%, real 10-year yields at 2.86% — a bear steepener, which is the curve shape you get when the long end is demanding term premium for inflation persistence and fiscal risk, not the bull-steepener you'd want in a genuine flight to safety. Treasuries can only cushion equities if yields fall when stocks fall. In a stagflation regime with sticky breakevens anchored above target, that relationship is conditional and fragile. One hot CPI print and your hedge sells off alongside your equities, which is exactly what happened for most of this year.

The thing actually holding the structure up is credit, and credit is asleep. Noodles has IG and HY spreads compressed with zero stress despite the rates carnage, RRP fully drained to zero, and the Fed balance sheet at $6.76T with the liquidity buffer that absorbed prior shocks now gone. We wrote on November 9 that cloud builders spent 116% of cash flow on capex and the IG market is the only thing keeping that math solvent. That's the same fault line. If the growth scare that's currently pushing money gently from stocks into Treasuries ever reaches investment-grade spreads, the refinancing math on the hyperscaler debt stack breaks, and the bid for the AI complex goes with it.

So treat the restored stock-bond hedge as a one-month artifact of which fear is dominant this week, not a regime. The dollar at an 18-month high and spreads this tight are telling you the market still believes the AI capex is money good. The real signal to watch isn't Treasuries — it's the first basis point of IG spread widening.

The other side

The stock-bond correlation turning negative again is not nothing, even if the mechanism is impure. Growth fears are a legitimate and durable driver of Treasury demand — if the AI capex cycle genuinely disappoints, the deceleration in business investment could be sharp enough to pull inflation expectations down with it, validating the bond bid and giving the Fed room to cut. In that scenario, the bear steepener resolves as a bull steepener, real yields compress, and the hedge works exactly as advertised. One month of data is thin, but the directional shift in what the market is pricing — from inflation risk to growth risk — is a real regime signal, not noise.

The J.P. Morgan observation is precise: over the past month, Treasury prices generally rose on days when a broad US equity index fell by more than 1%. That is the mechanical definition of the stock-bond hedge working. The problem is the word 'generally' and the word 'month.' The correlation flipping back toward negative after most of 2026 broke it is welcome, but J.P. Morgan itself flags that one month of trading does not establish a lasting regime. The source of the equity anxiety matters enormously for whether the hedge holds.

Through most of this year, the anxiety was inflation, which meant hot data pushed yields up and stocks down simultaneously — both legs of the portfolio losing at once. What has changed, per BBH and Noodles Research, is that the dominant fear has rotated toward growth, and specifically toward whether the AI buildout will generate the output its capex implies. BBH cites analysis putting the GDP level boost from the data-center construction cycle — described as among the largest peacetime investment expansions in US history — at just 0.3%. That figure, if even directionally correct, reframes the entire capex story: not a productivity revolution being financed at scale, but a massive balance-sheet commitment in exchange for a rounding error of incremental output. Growth fears of that shape send investors toward government bonds, which is what you're seeing.

But the Treasury market receiving those flows is not a calm one. Noodles Research has real 10-year yields at 2.86%, up 22 basis points on the week, with 5-year breakeven inflation at 2.30% and 10-year breakevens at 2.24% — both anchored above the Fed's 2% target and not moving lower. The 2s10s curve has steepened to plus 24 basis points as the long end leads higher. This is a bear steepener: the market demanding term premium for inflation persistence and fiscal risk, not a bull steepener driven by rate-cut expectations. Treasuries can cushion equities when yields fall as stocks fall. In a bear steepener, yields are rising structurally even when there are short-term flight-to-safety bids on bad equity days. One sufficiently hot CPI print reactivates the inflation fear, the flight-to-safety bid disappears, and the hedge evaporates — which is the pattern that dominated for most of 2026.

The deeper fragility sits in credit, which has not moved. IG and HY spreads remain compressed with no sign of stress despite the violent rates repricing. The Fed's reverse repo facility — the short-term liquidity buffer that absorbed prior rate shocks — is fully drained to zero. The TGA, the Treasury's operating account, fell $43 billion on the week to $914 billion, injecting some reserves, but that is a temporary offset, not a structural cushion. This brief noted on November 9 that cloud builders were spending 116% of operating cash flow on capex with the investment-grade market as the only thing keeping that arithmetic solvent. Nothing in this week's data changes that picture. If the growth scare that is currently nudging money gently from equities into Treasuries ever transmits into investment-grade spreads, the refinancing math on the hyperscaler debt stack — the companies building the data centers at the center of this entire cycle — becomes acutely difficult. The equity bid for the AI complex follows spreads, not the other way around.

China's October export growth of 35.2% year-on-year adds one more variable: a global economy still generating demand that keeps commodity and goods prices from collapsing, which limits how far inflation expectations can fall even if US growth disappoints. That is another reason the breakevens are sticky. The stagflation quadrant Noodles describes — rising real yields, softening labor market at 4.1% unemployment, inflation expectations above target — is the environment where the stock-bond hedge is most conditional and least reliable. Watch IG spreads. That is where the market will signal whether the growth scare is being taken seriously or is still being treated as a rotation opportunity.
