Rillet just raised $100mn to make corporate accounting software irrelevant. The incumbents haven't noticed yet.

  • Rillet — an AI-native accounting software startup founded in 2021 — raised $100mn at a $1bn valuation in its third funding round in just 14 months, bringing total capital raised past $200mn
  • Customer Mercor, an AI hiring marketplace, runs a business scaling past $2bn in annual recurring revenue with a finance team of only three people using Rillet's software
  • Agent activity across Rillet's 600-plus customers is growing roughly 70% month over month, and 40% of those customers now sit outside the tech sector entirely

The take

ICONIQ just led a $100mn Series C into Rillet at a $1bn valuation — the company's third raise in fourteen months and its second billion-dollar milestone reached inside a category, corporate accounting software, that nobody has called exciting since the Clinton administration. The headline read is "another AI unicorn." The more interesting read is that Rillet is the first credible test of whether AI actually eats the enterprise system of record, and the tell isn't the valuation. It's the customer list.

Look at what Rillet is bragging about. Mercor running a business scaling past $2bn in ARR with a finance team of three. The claim isn't "we automate reconciliation." The claim is that the ratio of revenue to finance headcount just broke by an order of magnitude, and the early customers proving it are themselves AI-native firms — Neuralink, Skild, Mercor — that have no legacy NetSuite implementation to rip out. That's the important structural fact. Rillet isn't winning by displacing Oracle and SAP. It's winning at companies that never installed them, and then waiting for those companies to become large. The incumbents' installed base is a moat against replacement; it is not a moat against new formation.

Here's what the market is mispricing. The bull case treats this as a $92.6bn ERP TAM that AI will redistribute from SAP to startups. Wrong frame. If a $2bn-ARR company needs three accountants instead of thirty, the value of ERP software per company doesn't grow 13% a year — the number of seats collapses even as the software gets more capable. That's deflationary for the whole category. Rillet can 10x and the aggregate ERP spend can still shrink. The winner captures a bigger slice of a smaller pie, priced on outcomes rather than seats. Anyone underwriting this on legacy seat-count logic is modeling the wrong business.

Then there's the credibility play everyone should watch: the EY alliance, KPMG, RSM, half the Accounting Today top 20. Kopp is doing the smart thing — buying the audit and advisory establishment's blessing before asking a public-company CFO to trust an agent with the general ledger. But that's also the vulnerability. The Big Four don't do exclusive. The moment agent-first ERP is proven, EY partners with Campfire too, and NetSuite ships its own agents to a base it already owns. Rillet's window is the 18–24 months before the incumbents stop calling this a toy.

The honest tension is the one Kopp keeps dancing around. "We're not coming after jobs" and "we believe in lean, impactful finance teams" cannot both be the pitch. The product's entire value proposition is fewer accountants per dollar of revenue. That's fine — it's why the round happened. Just don't pretend the 340,000 missing accountants are a coincidence rather than the business model.

The other side

The incumbents — Oracle, SAP, Workday, NetSuite — aren't asleep. They have existing contracts with the Fortune 500, armies of implementation partners, and the single most powerful force in enterprise software: the sunk cost of the last migration. Any CFO who survived a NetSuite rollout isn't eager to repeat it with a 14-month-old startup, no matter how impressive the demo. And if agent-first ERP genuinely works at scale, SAP ships the feature inside its existing platform before Rillet can land the logo — the same playbook Salesforce has run against CRM challengers for twenty years.

The structural bet ICONIQ is making here is not really about accounting software. It is about whether the unit economics of running a finance function just permanently reset, and whether the company that proved it first gets to own the next generation of the category before the old guard figures out what happened.

The clearest signal is the customer mix. Rillet has 600 customers today, but the ones doing the work in the pitch deck are all AI-native — Neuralink, Skild AI, Mercor. These are companies that were never going to install SAP. They launched in the last few years, they run lean by default, and they have no legacy system to protect. The go-to-market logic is essentially the same one Rippling used against ADP and Paychex: start with the new-formation companies who have no incumbent relationship to disrupt, grow with them as they scale, and let the installed base age out. Rippling is now reportedly valued at over $13bn. Rillet is betting the same trajectory applies to the general ledger.

The 40% of customers now outside tech is the number to watch most carefully. Waste recycling firms and film studios do not buy software because a VC told them it was cool. They buy it because someone ran the numbers on headcount and the case was obvious. That cross-industry pull, if it sustains, is the evidence that Rillet's pitch works on total-cost grounds, not just on novelty grounds.

The EY and KPMG partnerships are clever but double-edged in a way the funding announcement glosses over. Accounting firms make a significant portion of their revenue from the implementation and audit work that surrounds ERP systems. If Rillet's agents genuinely collapse the complexity of closing the books, they are also compressing the billable hours the Big Four collect downstream. The alliance only holds as long as the accounting firms believe Rillet creates more advisory work than it destroys. The moment that calculus flips, the partnership becomes a competitive threat dressed up as a channel.

There is also a timing risk embedded in the model that the 18-to-24-month incumbent window does not fully capture. Rillet's agent activity is growing 70% month over month today, but that metric will mean something very different once the customer base is measured in thousands rather than hundreds. Compounding that fast on a small base is a product story. Sustaining it on a large base is an operations and reliability story, and that is where enterprise software companies historically hit their first wall. Public-company CFOs signing off on an AI agent running their close process will want audit trails, error-rate disclosures, and contractual liability language that a 14-month-old startup has not yet been forced to negotiate at scale.

The deflationary dynamic in the take is the part of this story that the $92.6bn ERP market figure obscures. If the software gets dramatically more capable while the headcount it requires collapses, the spending per company does not scale with revenue the way legacy seat-count pricing assumed. The prize Rillet is competing for may be structurally smaller than the TAM suggests, even in a scenario where Rillet wins. That is not an argument against the round — it is an argument for pricing on outcomes rather than users, which is exactly the direction Kopp appears to be heading. Getting that pricing model right before the incumbents copy the product is probably the most important thing Rillet does in the next two years.

Anthropic's profitability is real and fragile at the same time. Know the difference before you buy the IPO.

  • Anthropic reported $11.5B in preliminary Q2 2026 booked revenue — up from $787M in Q2 2025, a 14x increase in four quarters — and its first quarter of positive adjusted operating income
  • OpenAI posted $6.7B in Q2 2026 revenue, an 18% sequential gain from Q1, but its operating losses widened rather than narrowed despite the growth
  • Over 80% of Anthropic's revenue comes from enterprise API contracts — companies paying per-token to run Claude inside their own products — giving it more predictable unit economics than OpenAI's consumer subscription model

The take

Anthropic just reported $11.5B in preliminary Q2 2026 booked revenue — up roughly 14x year-over-year — and, more importantly, its first quarter of positive adjusted operating income. OpenAI, over the same period, grew to $6.7B and lost more money doing it. Both sets of numbers are unaudited and dropped to investors ahead of dueling IPOs, which tells you exactly what they are: valuation anchors dressed as disclosures.

The consensus read is that Anthropic won the argument — enterprise API discipline beats consumer subsidy, recurring revenue beats churny subscriptions, profit beats promises. That's mostly right and mostly beside the point. The word doing all the work here is "adjusted." Adjusted operating income strips out stock-based comp, and at a company where equity is the primary currency for retaining researchers who can walk to Meta for a nine-figure package tomorrow, SBC is not a non-cash accounting nicety — it's the single largest real cost of staying in the game. Anthropic is profitable in the same way WeWork was "community-adjusted EBITDA" positive. The direction of travel is genuinely good. The headline number is a construct.

Here's the second-order effect nobody's pricing. Anthropic's margin story is built on 80%-plus enterprise API revenue, and that revenue is only durable if the price-per-token premium over Claude holds. It won't. The same GLM-class Chinese open-weight models undercutting the frontier on cost are landing inside the exact enterprise workflows — code completion, extraction, summarization — that make up the bulk of API volume. Anthropic's profitability today is a snapshot taken at the moment of maximum pricing power, right before the commodity layer eats the floor. Turning profitable at the top of your pricing curve is not the same as having a moat.

The tell for allocators is the timing. You don't leak a maiden profitable quarter unless you're about to sell equity against it. Anthropic is anchoring a public valuation to a metric that is flattering today and structurally fragile over the next four quarters. The smart position isn't Anthropic-versus-OpenAI — that's the retail framing. It's recognizing that both companies are racing to IPO before the token-pricing compression that's already visible in the developer stack shows up in the revenue line. Amazon and Google, sitting on the cap table, understand this perfectly, which is why they'd rather Anthropic be a public liquidity event than a permanent balance-sheet dependent.

If you're getting the allocation, underwrite the DPI, not the narrative. A frontier lab that turned adjusted-profitable at peak pricing, going public into a market that's about to reprice the entire cost curve, is a trade with a defined exit — theirs, not yours.

The other side

The bull case for Anthropic's durability is that enterprise API contracts aren't won on price alone. Safety benchmarks, reliability, and the trust of regulated industries — finance, healthcare, legal — are real switching costs that cheap Chinese open-weight models can't immediately overcome, especially for buyers with procurement restrictions. Anthropic has also moved faster than any frontier lab in history from sub-scale to operating leverage, which suggests its cost structure is genuinely improving, not just flattered by one strong quarter. Betting against that trajectory, heading into an IPO backed by Amazon and Google, is a harder short than it looks.

The accounting distinction at the center of this story is worth dwelling on, because it will determine how public market investors eventually price both companies. Adjusted operating income — the metric Anthropic used to declare profitability — strips out stock-based compensation, typically abbreviated as SBC. For most mature companies, SBC is a modest line item that analysts reasonably set aside to assess core operating performance. For a frontier AI lab competing for researchers who routinely receive nine-figure equity packages from Meta, Google DeepMind, and xAI, it is nothing of the sort. SBC is the mechanism by which Anthropic retains the people whose work is literally the product. Excluding it to declare a profit milestone is not fraudulent, but it is aggressively optimistic accounting, and public market investors who price the company on that adjusted figure without adjusting back will be buying a story, not a business.

The historical parallel that comes to mind is not WeWork — that comparison gets overused — but rather the 2014-to-2016 SaaS wave, when a generation of cloud software companies learned to report non-GAAP operating income that excluded SBC, and Wall Street largely played along until rates rose and the discount window closed. The companies that survived that reckoning were the ones with genuine net revenue retention and real pricing power. The ones that didn't were the ones whose adjusted profitability masked structurally negative unit economics. Anthropic's enterprise API model has better bones than most of those SaaS businesses did, but the pricing power question is the same one.

The token-pricing compression dynamic the take flags is already visible if you track the developer stack rather than the press releases. Open-weight models from Chinese labs — smaller, cheaper, increasingly capable on the workhorse tasks that dominate enterprise API volume, things like document extraction, code completion, and summarization — are being embedded into the same enterprise workflows that generate the bulk of Anthropic's revenue. The premium that Claude commands today exists because enterprise buyers are still in evaluation mode and switching costs feel high. That window does not stay open indefinitely. Once a procurement team has run a six-month pilot and confirmed that a cheaper model handles 80% of their volume at acceptable quality, the renegotiation conversation starts.

For ICONIQ, which holds Anthropic through its Strategic Partners VII vehicle, the relevant question is not whether the company is impressive — it clearly is — but whether the IPO is the right moment to be adding exposure or harvesting it. A company going public at peak adjusted profitability, into a market that hasn't yet priced the token cost curve repricing that is already underway in the developer ecosystem, is offering allocators a defined entry with uncertain duration. Amazon and Google sitting on the cap table are sophisticated enough to know this, and the fact that both strategic backers appear comfortable with an IPO path rather than continued private funding rounds is itself a signal worth reading carefully. They would rather crystallize a public valuation now than keep funding a balance sheet that gets harder to defend as the competitive pricing floor drops. That's not a reason to avoid the stock entirely — the growth trajectory is real. It is a reason to be precise about what you're underwriting and at what multiple.

Four Western banks just put a Chinese-affiliated AI model inside their FX operations. The provenance is the story.

  • Barclays, Citi, Deutsche Bank, and Standard Chartered have each integrated FalconTST 2.0 — a currency and cash-flow forecasting model from Ant International, the Singapore-based international arm of Jack Ma's Ant Group — directly into their live foreign-exchange systems.
  • FalconTST 2.0 posted a Mean Absolute Scaled Error of 0.666 on a public time-series benchmark, with claimed forecast accuracy above 93 percent — though the benchmark testing was conducted by Ant International itself.
  • Standard Chartered's deployment runs inside a Monetary Authority of Singapore regulatory sandbox called PathFin.ai, giving the rollout an official MAS imprimatur that signals regulatory acceptance, not just commercial curiosity.

The take

Four global banks — Barclays, Citi, Deutsche Bank, and Standard Chartered — just plugged an AI forecasting model built by Ant International, the Singapore-headquartered international arm of Jack Ma's Ant Group, into the plumbing of their foreign-exchange operations. The model predicts cash flow and currency exposure for cross-border payments, and version 2.0 reportedly tops a public time-series benchmark. That last detail is the one everyone will ignore, and it's the whole story.

Because the benchmark is Ant's own. FalconTST 2.0's headline number — a 0.666 mean absolute scaled error, "above 93 percent accuracy" — is self-reported on Ant's own testing. That's not a knock on the math; it may well be excellent. It's a knock on how this gets narrated. The interesting event isn't that an Ant model won a leaderboard. It's that four of the most compliance-paranoid, model-risk-obsessed institutions on earth agreed to run a Chinese-affiliated model inside their FX stacks — BARX, Citi's Fixed FX Rates, Standard Chartered's SCALE FX under the Monetary Authority of Singapore's PathFin.ai sandbox. Getting a new forecasting engine past a tier-one bank's model risk committee takes 12 to 18 months. This wasn't a vibe purchase.

Here's the second-order move nobody's pricing. The Western AI conversation is stuck on chatbots and frontier reasoning — will GPT beat Gemini, can Anthropic hold enterprise. Meanwhile the highest-value enterprise AI isn't generative at all. It's boring numerical forecasting that feeds a live hedging book, where a one-percent accuracy gain on currency exposure is worth more than every marketing-copilot deployment combined. Ant went straight for the part of the balance sheet that actually moves capital. And the distribution channel is genius: each bank runs it inside its own systems, under its own brand, so the customer never sees "Ant." The banks get the accuracy, Ant gets the reference logos and the reusable model it plans to push into aviation, logistics, and e-commerce.

Watch what this does to sovereignty theater. For two years the assumption has been that geopolitics walls Chinese AI out of Western finance. This is the counterexample, and it's happening through Singapore — the neutral-ground jurisdiction where a Chinese-lineage firm can sell to American and British banks under a MAS regulatory blessing that launders the provenance. That's the template. Not confrontation, arbitrage.

The allocator read: the enterprise AI winners won't be the labs selling tokens by the million. They'll be the firms that embed a specialized model directly into a workflow where the output is a treasury decision, and price against the P&L improvement, not the compute. Ant already ran this internally on its own cash management before selling it. That's the tell — this is a battle-tested internal tool being monetized, not a demo chasing customers. The token-pricing labs are selling shovels. Ant is selling the mine.

The other side

The sovereignty concern cuts both ways. These banks aren't naive — each institution runs the model inside its own infrastructure, under its own brand, with its own model risk validation layered on top. Regulators in the UK and US have not objected, and MAS's explicit sandbox blessing is meaningful cover. If four of the most legally cautious banks on earth ran independent validation and signed off, the assumption that Chinese-affiliated AI is categorically blocked from Western finance may already be outdated. The real risk isn't the model's origin; it's that self-reported benchmark numbers become the de facto standard of proof in an industry that used to demand years of live back-testing.

The model risk management process inside a tier-one bank is genuinely brutal, and understanding that is what makes this story legible. Before any new quantitative model touches a live trading book — whether it prices derivatives, forecasts cash positions, or flags settlement risk — it has to pass through a model risk committee staffed by quants and compliance officers whose explicit job is to reject things. They want to see documentation, independent validation, out-of-sample testing, and evidence that the model behaves predictably under stress. The typical cycle from initial submission to production approval runs twelve to eighteen months. Four banks clearing that process for the same external model, on roughly the same timeline, is not an accident. Someone senior made a decision that the accuracy gain was worth the vendor relationship.

The accuracy gain in question is also not abstract. Foreign-exchange hedging works like this: a corporate treasurer or bank desk estimates how much foreign-currency exposure they'll carry over a given period, then buys forward contracts or options to offset that risk. If the forecast is wrong by even a few percentage points, they end up over-hedged or under-hedged, and the mismatch costs real money — not theoretical money, money that shows up in quarterly earnings. A forecasting model that materially improves that prediction is worth a great deal, and the math is directly auditable against P&L. That's a very different value proposition than a chatbot that generates marketing copy faster. The improvement is measurable, the dollar value is calculable, and the decision to pay for it is easy to justify internally. That's exactly the kind of AI purchase that doesn't require a leap of faith.

Ant International's go-to-market structure is also worth examining carefully. By embedding FalconTST inside each bank's own named product — BARX NetFX at Barclays, Fixed FX Rates at Citi, SCALE FX at Standard Chartered — the end customer never encounters the Ant brand. The banks own the relationship and carry the regulatory accountability. Ant, in exchange, accumulates reference deployments at globally recognized institutions, which it can use to accelerate sales into aviation, logistics, and e-commerce — the sectors it has explicitly flagged as next targets. This is a classic platform land-and-expand play, except the land is happening inside the most credentialed distribution network in global finance.

The Singapore dimension deserves more attention than it typically gets. Ant International is legally distinct from Ant Group's China-domiciled operations, and MAS has spent years building Singapore into the preferred jurisdiction for exactly this kind of arrangement — where a firm with Chinese lineage needs a neutral regulatory home to transact with Western counterparties. PathFin.ai, the MAS sandbox where Standard Chartered's deployment sits, is specifically designed to give regulators visibility into fintech experiments before they scale. That's not laundering risk; it's a genuine accountability structure. But it also functions as a template other Chinese-affiliated technology firms can follow. Establish a Singapore entity, obtain MAS engagement, use that as proof of regulatory legitimacy for Western bank procurement. The geopolitical walls that were supposed to contain Chinese AI in financial services may have always had a door, and it opens in Singapore.

Financial Markets & Economic News Rates Fiscal Policy Treasuries

The Fed wants to hike. Treasury is buying bonds anyway. Pick one.

  • July FOMC minutes show a meaningful bloc of Fed governors favoring rate hikes if inflation stays elevated — not the cuts markets have been pricing in
  • Treasury is simultaneously running bond buybacks — purchases of its own outstanding long-dated debt — to push long-term yields down even as rate-setters signal tighter money
  • With roughly $9 trillion in federal debt rolling over within twelve months and the deficit above 6% of GDP, every half-point move on the long end translates into tens of billions in additional annual interest expense

The take

Two things happened in the same 48 hours that shouldn't coexist. The July FOMC minutes revealed that a meaningful bloc of governors wants to hike, not cut, if inflation doesn't cool — and simultaneously, Treasury under Kevin Warsh, now installed as both Fed chair and the administration's rate whisperer, is accelerating bond buybacks to pull down long yields. One arm of policy is signaling tighter money; the other is quietly financing it easier. That contradiction is the story, and the market is pricing it as noise.

Here's what's actually going on. When Treasury front-runs its own issuance calendar with buybacks, it's doing fiscal QE — buying back long paper to suppress the term premium — while the Fed's own committee is telling you the neutral rate is higher than the front end implies. That's not coordination. That's a government arbitraging the gap between what it wants long rates to be and what its own central bankers think they should be. The tell is the timing: you don't rush buybacks into a market when your rate-setters are floating hikes unless someone above the FOMC has decided the debt-service math matters more than the inflation mandate. With roughly $9 trillion rolling over inside twelve months, every 50bp on the long end is real money against a deficit already north of 6% of GDP.

The consensus read is "mixed signals, wait for clarity." Wrong. The signal is perfectly clear once you accept that Warsh wearing two hats has collapsed the independence premium that used to sit inside Treasury yields. Fiscal dominance — where debt-service needs quietly override the inflation target — isn't a tail risk anymore; it's the operating model, and it's being executed in plain sight. The bond market keeps treating a hawkish FOMC as bullish for the long end because hawks fight inflation. But if the buybacks succeed in capping nominal yields while the underlying inflation impulse is real, you get negative real long rates by stealth. That's a tax on every fixed-rate holder, and it's the whole point.

For allocators the trade is the curve, not the level. A steepener — long the front, short the very long end — is the cleanest expression of "Warsh caps the belly, but the term premium eventually reprices for fiscal risk you can't buyback away." Own inflation breakevens and gold against nominal Treasuries; the buyback program is a tell that Washington would rather inflate the debt than default on the political cost of a recession. And watch the 30-year auction tails. If foreign bidders keep fading and Treasury has to buy back more to hold the line, the mask slips, and the repricing won't be orderly.

The other side

Treasury buybacks are a routine liability-management tool, not a conspiracy to override the Fed — the program has precedent from the early 2000s and is explicitly designed to improve market liquidity in off-the-run securities, not to set a yield ceiling. And Warsh, whatever his dual influence, cannot actually instruct the FOMC how to vote; the committee is institutionally independent and the minutes themselves prove governors are willing to push back publicly. If inflation does cool over the next two quarters, the buybacks and a rate hold end up being the right call simultaneously, and the contradiction dissolves on its own.

The concept worth dwelling on here is fiscal dominance — the condition where a government's debt load becomes so large that the inflation-fighting mandate of the central bank quietly yields to the debt-service math. Economists have debated it as a theoretical risk for years. What's unusual about this moment is how openly the mechanism is being executed. Treasury isn't hiding the buyback program; it's just betting that most observers won't connect the dots between two simultaneous policy moves that point in opposite directions.

Historically, the clearest precedent is the post-World War II yield curve control era, when the Fed was formally directed to cap Treasury yields at 2.5% while inflation ran considerably hotter. That arrangement held from 1942 until the Treasury-Fed Accord of 1951, when the central bank finally reasserted independence. The political cost of breaking it was enormous, and it took years of behind-the-scenes conflict before the Fed got its mandate back. The difference today is that nobody is admitting the peg exists. The fiction of independence is being preserved even as the functional reality is eroding.

Kevin Warsh's position is the institutional tell. Warsh was confirmed as Fed chair — meaning he runs monetary policy — but he has also been the administration's most trusted voice on rates throughout this cycle, having served informally in that capacity before his appointment. That dual role matters because it eliminates the friction that normally exists between Treasury and the Fed. In a healthy separation, Treasury issues debt and the Fed sets rates, and the two occasionally conflict in ways that produce honest price signals for the market. When the same person is effectively managing both, those signals get smoothed over before they reach the public.

The market's failure to reprice this risk is partly mechanical. Bond investors have been trained over the past fifteen years to treat Fed hawkishness as a suppressant on long yields, because tight money slows growth and reduces the inflation premium. That logic holds in a world where the central bank is genuinely independent and fiscal constraints are secondary. It breaks down when the entity buying long bonds and the entity setting short rates are operating from the same playbook. In that environment, hawkish FOMC rhetoric becomes theater — useful for anchoring expectations on the front end while the buybacks do the real work on the long end.

The 30-year auction results are the cleanest early warning system available. Foreign central banks and sovereign wealth funds — historically the marginal buyer of very long U.S. paper — have been showing up with less appetite over the past several cycles. If they continue to fade and domestic buybacks have to fill the gap, Treasury is essentially monetizing its own debt in slow motion, without calling it that. The breakeven inflation rate on 10-year TIPS — Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, which price in expected inflation — would be the confirmation signal: if buybacks suppress nominal yields while breakevens stay elevated, real yields go negative, and the inflation tax on fixed-income holders becomes explicit in the data even if it stays implicit in the policy language.

Private Markets Venture Secondaries Liquidity

The IPO is dead as a default exit. The secondary market is the real clearing mechanism now.

  • US private secondary volume hit $138 billion in 2025, up from $52 billion in 2021, with company-led tenders — fixed-price buybacks a company runs across its own cap table — accounting for 41 percent of that flow
  • Median time from Series A to IPO has stretched to 10.7 years in 2025 from 6.9 years in 2014, and 1,247 US venture-backed companies have been private for eight or more years with no announced exit path
  • Sovereign wealth funds participated in 47 US private-company tenders in 2025, up from just 11 in 2021, as the buyer base for secondary stock has broadened well beyond dedicated secondary funds

The take

US private-company secondary volume hit $138 billion in 2025, triple the 2021 figure, and the number worth staring at is 41 percent — the share driven by company-led tenders, the fixed-price buyback a company runs across its own cap table under SEC Rule 13e-4. The research comes from Yanne Capital, a growth-stage advisory shop, so read the framing with the usual skepticism a sell-side white paper deserves. But the underlying data from PitchBook and Carta is real, and it says something the venture industry has been very quiet about: the IPO is no longer the exit. It's optional.

Here's what nobody in the LP world is pricing correctly. Median time from Series A to IPO has stretched to 10.7 years, and 53 percent of eight-year-plus unicorns have no public exit path at all — up from 14 percent a decade ago. The tender market didn't emerge because founders got sophisticated about liquidity. It emerged because the public market stopped clearing the paper. Secondaries aren't a feature of a healthy venture ecosystem; they're the pressure-release valve on a plumbing system that's backed up for a decade. When 65 percent of tenders clear at 70 to 90 percent of the last primary mark, that discount is the market telling you the primary marks are 10 to 30 percent fiction.

The real story is who's buying, and it's the same convergence trade playing out in PE secondaries. Sovereign wealth funds ran 47 US tenders in 2025 versus 11 in 2021. Family-office pooled vehicles absorbed roughly $19 billion. Crossover managers are now anchoring secondary blocks in the exact names they hold in public books. That's not a diversified buyer base — it's the same long-duration capital that got starved of IPO allocations rerouting itself into the private clearing mechanism. For an allocator, that means your "private" and "public" venture exposure is quietly becoming the same position, priced twice, marked once.

The tell in the Yanne framing is the pairing argument: run a tender alongside a live primary and it clears 8 to 14 points higher than a standalone. Translated, that means the discount is almost entirely an information problem, not a value problem. Buyers pay up when there's a fresh price signal and gap down when they're pricing against a stale mark and their own diligence. Which tells you the stale mark on your 2021 vintage is not conservative — it's the number that hasn't been tested against a real bid.

The read-across for Singapore capital: if you hold late-stage venture through funds still carrying 2021 marks, the tender data is your mark-to-market whether you look at it or not. The honest DPI is 78 cents on the dollar. Everything above that is a story waiting for a listing that, for half these names, is never coming.

The other side

The bull case for the existing structure is that private markets are doing exactly what they should — keeping high-growth companies away from the quarterly-earnings treadmill until they are genuinely ready for public scrutiny, and the secondary market is simply the liquidity mechanism that makes that patience feasible for founders and employees. If the 65 percent of tenders clearing between 70 and 90 cents of the last primary round are still generating strong absolute returns for 2018 and 2019 vintage investors, the 'discount is fiction' argument overstates the problem. And the broadening buyer base — sovereign wealth, crossover funds, family offices — suggests real demand at real prices, not a market propped up by forced sellers and credulous capital.

The arithmetic here is worth walking through slowly, because the scale is now large enough to have systemic implications for how allocators think about their venture books. Triple the volume in four years is not a marginal shift in market structure — it is a wholesale repricing of the exit model. The 1,247 companies sitting private for eight or more years with no announced path are not anomalies. They are the median outcome for a cohort funded at peak 2019 to 2021 valuations that never found a public market willing to validate those marks.

The tender structure that has won out — a fixed-price offer filed under SEC Rule 13e-4, the same rule public companies use for self-tenders — is interesting precisely because it forces price discovery in a market that has systematically avoided it. When a board sets a clearing price at 80 percent of the last round, they are making an implicit admission: the last round was priced for a world where the IPO window opens on schedule, the preference stack gets paid out cleanly, and employees see liquidity within three to four years of joining. None of those assumptions held.

The pairing insight from Yanne Capital — run a tender alongside a live primary and it clears 8 to 14 percentage points higher — is not just a tactical observation. It is a structural diagnosis. The discount in a standalone tender is almost entirely an information problem. Buyers, pricing against a mark that may be 18 months old, gap down to compensate for what they do not know. Add a live primary with fresh diligence and a new lead setting price, and the same asset clears materially higher. The implication is that every fund carrying a 2021-vintage position that has not been retested against a live transaction is holding a number, not a mark.

The buyer convergence is the second-order story that has not gotten enough attention. Sovereign wealth funds, crossover managers, and family-office pools did not enter the secondary market because they developed a sudden philosophical appreciation for illiquid small-cap venture stock. They entered because the IPO pipeline — where they would normally receive allocations in pre-IPO and early-public-float rounds — dried up, and the secondary tender is the closest functional substitute. The stock is often the same; the entry mechanism is different. That means allocators who think they are diversified across private and public venture exposure may be running a more concentrated bet than their portfolio construction suggests, with the private sleeve marked at primary and the public sleeve marked daily.

Historically, this kind of dislocation resolves one of two ways. Either the public market reopens — IPO windows do not stay closed forever, and a few high-quality listings tend to pull the rest of the backlog through — or the secondary market becomes the permanent clearing mechanism and the valuation gap between primary marks and secondary bids becomes the accepted cost of staying private. The current data suggests both are happening simultaneously, with different companies in different tiers resolving differently. The names with strong revenue growth and credible paths to profitability are still threading the IPO needle. The rest are quietly clearing through the tender market at whatever the sovereign funds and secondary desks will pay. For a portfolio manager, the honest question is which bucket your 2021 vintage falls into, and whether your current marks reflect the answer.
