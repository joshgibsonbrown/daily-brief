Private Markets Secondaries PE Liquidity Europe

CVC raised $10 billion to profit from private equity's inability to return cash.

theedgemalaysia.com
  • Secondary Opportunities Fund VI closed at $10 billion, up from $5.8 billion in 2023 and $2.7 billion in 2019 — three fund cycles, nearly quadruple the capital.
  • Secondaries deal volume hit a record $121 billion in H1 2026 per Evercore, with large sovereign funds identified as the dominant sellers.
  • CVC now manages €20 billion ($23.2 billion) across its private equity and credit secondary funds after acquiring secondaries specialist Glendower Capital in 2021.

The take

CVC's Secondary Opportunities Fund VI closed at $10 billion, nearly double the $5.8 billion it raised in 2023 and almost quadruple the $2.7 billion vintage from 2019. That trajectory — 2.7 to 5.8 to 10 in three fund cycles — is the story, and it slots directly into what this section has been tracking all month: sovereigns dumping $1 billion PE books through PJT, Partners Group building continuation vehicles across its entire credit legacy, Blackstone unable to sell a CFO built on its own aging fund stakes.

Here's the part that gets sold as bullish and is actually a warning. Carlo Pirzio-Biroli, who runs CVC's secondaries book, told Bloomberg there are "several trillions of capital trapped in unsold private equity holdings." He means that as demand — a giant opportunity set. But read it from the other side of the trade. Trillions trapped means the primary machine is broken: capital went in, assets didn't exit, and DPI — cash actually returned to LPs — stalled. A $10 billion secondaries fund isn't a bet on private equity working. It's a bet on private equity staying jammed long enough to buy the exits cheap. CVC is underwriting the failure of its own asset class as a growth business.

That's why the H1 record matters more than the headline raise. Evercore clocked $121 billion in first-half secondaries volume, and our own coverage flagged that the dominant sellers are large repeat sovereigns — GIC, KIC — not distressed minnows. When Singapore's and Korea's investment corporations are the marginal sellers, this isn't a boutique arbitrage anymore. It's the release valve for a market that can't self-clear, and the sovereigns are paying discounts to the CVCs and Ardians of the world to get liquidity that the primary funds promised and didn't deliver.

Pirzio-Biroli's other line is the honest one: "The days of the exciting standalone boutique at the higher end of the market are over. There's a premium to being part of a larger platform." Translate that. Secondaries pricing is thinning as the money floods in — $121 billion in six months chasing the same fund stakes — so the edge is no longer clever underwriting, it's balance-sheet scale, proprietary sourcing off your own portfolio, and the recurring management fee on $23.2 billion of assets. CVC bought Glendower in 2021 for exactly this. The boutiques get bought or squeezed because the return per deal is compressing and only scale survives it.

So the thing to watch isn't whether CVC deploys the $10 billion — it will. It's the entry discounts on 2026-2027 deals versus the 2022-2023 vintage. When too much dry powder chases trapped assets, discounts narrow, and the buyers who raised at the top of the cycle inherit the same illiquidity they were supposed to be arbitraging. The secondaries market solves the primary market's exit problem right up until it becomes the primary market — and then who buys the secondaries' secondaries?

The other side

The bull case here is straightforward and not wrong: if several trillions of dollars are genuinely trapped in unsold PE holdings, a $10 billion fund is still a rounding error against the opportunity set, and early-mover scale in a consolidating market is exactly the right position to hold. CVC's fund-over-fund growth also reflects real LP demand — more than 200 investors committed, and the asset class has earned its own allocation bucket at major institutions precisely because it delivers liquidity when primary funds cannot. If the exit drought persists for another two to three years, the 2026 vintage of secondaries funds may look like the 2009 vintage of distressed debt: the right strategy, raised at the right moment, with plenty of room to run before the market self-corrects.

The structural story inside CVC's raise is the consolidation dynamic Pirzio-Biroli named directly. When he says the standalone boutique is finished at the high end, he is describing a market that has already repriced the edge. In the early years of secondaries — when the asset class was genuinely obscure and sellers were stigmatized for wanting out — a small, specialized buyer could extract wide discounts because there were few competing bids. That era is over. At $121 billion in first-half volume, secondaries is no longer a niche arbitrage; it is a liquid-ish market with institutional capital on both sides of every trade.

The implication for returns is direct. As more capital chases the same pool of trapped fund stakes, the discount to net asset value that buyers can demand compresses. The secondaries funds that raised in 2021 and 2022 — when volume was lower and competition thinner — likely bought at wider discounts than the funds deploying in 2026 and 2027 will. CVC's $10 billion will be put to work in a market where sovereign wealth funds from Singapore and Korea are the marginal sellers, not distressed endowments forced to liquidate at any price. Sophisticated, patient sellers negotiate harder, and the buyer's edge shrinks accordingly.

This is why the Glendower acquisition in 2021 matters beyond the AUM it added. Proprietary deal flow — sourcing fund stakes through relationships with GPs whose primary funds CVC also backs — is now the primary moat, not analytical skill in pricing a portfolio of aging buyout assets. When you manage €20 billion across secondaries and credit, you see deal flow that a $2 billion standalone shop never gets invited into. Scale also lets you write larger checks, which matters because the sovereign-sized sellers moving $1 billion blocks need a counterparty who can absorb the whole position without syndicating it to ten co-investors and leaking price.

The longer-run risk is the one the take flags but is worth extending: the secondaries market is now large enough to have its own liquidity cycle. In 2022 and 2023, when public markets sold off and PE marks were slow to follow, LP portfolios became overweight private assets — the so-called denominator effect — and secondaries volume surged as institutions rebalanced. The current surge has a different driver: DPI, meaning the actual cash distributions that PE funds return to investors, has been structurally low because the IPO market was effectively closed from 2022 until recently and M&A exit multiples compressed. LPs need cash to fund new commitments, and selling fund stakes on the secondary market is how they get it when the primary funds won't or can't distribute.

If the IPO window reopens durably and M&A volumes recover, primary fund distributions pick up, LP cash needs ease, and the supply of secondary deal flow shrinks just as the funds raised in 2025 and 2026 are trying to deploy. That is the vintage risk embedded in a $10 billion raise at the top of a volume cycle. CVC will deploy the capital — the fund is closed and the fees are running — but the question is whether the assets bought in 2026 and 2027 clear at the returns the model underwrote when LP appetite for the strategy was at its peak.

Tech & AI Hyperscalers Capex Debt Markets

Goldman's $1.2 trillion AI capex forecast arrives at the exact moment the growth curve rolls over.

the-decoder.com
  • Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Oracle, and Meta are projected to spend $1.2 trillion on AI infrastructure in 2027 — 50% above this year's ~$800 billion and above the $1.1 trillion Wall Street consensus.
  • Capex growth decelerates from ~100% in 2026 to 54% in 2027 and 12% in 2028, meaning the peak of the cycle is being announced as the cycle matures.
  • To recoup the outlay, the five companies need roughly $300 billion a year in AI revenue — a bar Goldman itself says OpenAI and Anthropic may not clear fast enough.

The take

Goldman now expects Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Oracle, and Meta to spend a combined $1.2 trillion on AI infrastructure in 2027 — over 50 percent above this year's ~$800 billion, and above the ~$1.1 trillion Street consensus. Relative to GDP, that's the biggest build cycle since 19th-century railroads. The interesting part isn't the number. It's what Goldman quietly admits underneath it.

Three things in this note deserve more attention than the trillion-dollar headline. First: growth is already decelerating hard — from roughly 100 percent in 2026 to 54 percent in 2027 to 12 percent in 2028. So the "biggest cycle since railroads" is being announced at the exact moment the curve rolls over. That's not the start of a supercycle. That's the peak being marked.

Second: Goldman says spending now exceeds what these companies generate from ongoing operations, so the marginal dollar is debt-financed. We've watched this in real time in this section — BlackRock's Meta bond in El Paso cleared north of 7 percent despite one of the best balance sheets on earth, and Nvidia is lending its own credit to make Lambda's buildings financeable. The hyperscalers are no longer self-funding this out of cash flow. They're terming it out. That changes who absorbs the loss if utilization disappoints: bondholders and structured-credit buyers, not just equity.

Third, and this is the one the rail analogy hides: to recoup $1.2 trillion you need roughly $300 billion a year in AI revenue, and the entire thesis rests on OpenAI and Anthropic scaling fast enough to fill it. Goldman's own line — "it's still unclear whether revenue growth at AI labs is fast enough" — is doing enormous work. Cloud growth reaccelerating from 25 to 48 percent is real demand, but a large slice of that is the labs renting capacity from the hyperscalers using money the hyperscalers and their investors put into the labs. Microsoft funds OpenAI, OpenAI buys Azure, Azure books the revenue that justifies more capex. Circular financing doesn't make the demand fake, but it does mean a single lab stumble unwinds both the revenue line and the collateral value at once.

Here's the part that should worry anyone underwriting the equity at these multiples: the capability lead the whole premium depends on is compressing. Chinese open-weight models now trail by roughly 4.4 months, Xiaomi trained a top open model for $2.62 million, and blended frontier pricing doubled to $11.25 per million tokens while mid-tier prices fell 36 percent. You're being asked to finance $1.2 trillion of depreciating silicon on the assumption that the output stays scarce and premium-priced. The pricing data says the opposite is happening.

Railroads is the right comparison, just not the way Goldman means it. The rails got built, transformed the economy, and bankrupted most of the people who financed the first wave. The infrastructure survives the capital that funds it. Own the power, the memory, and the land. Be very careful renting the equity to whoever's holding the debt when 12 percent growth arrives.

The other side

Cloud revenue growth accelerating from 25% in 2024 to 48% in Q2 2026 is not a rounding error — that's a genuine demand signal, and the hyperscalers have been wrong about AI demand being overstated at every prior inflection. If agentic workloads compound the way early cloud did, $300 billion in annual AI revenue by 2028 is conservative, not heroic, and the companies locking in capacity now will have priced out any late entrant. Debt financing at 7% is expensive, but it's still cheaper than missing the window entirely if the demand curve proves steeper than Goldman's base case.

The railroad analogy Goldman reaches for is historically apt in ways the note probably doesn't intend. The first great American rail buildout in the 1870s produced exactly this pattern: a genuine infrastructure transformation, a financing structure that outran the underlying economics, and a wave of defaults that wiped out the capital while leaving the tracks intact. The Panic of 1873 was, at its core, a railroad capex cycle that hit the debt wall before revenue caught up. Goldman is invoking the comparison as a measure of ambition. The structure of the cycle it describes is closer to a cautionary tale.

The deceleration math is the part that deserves the most scrutiny. Going from 100% growth in 2026 to 12% in 2028 is not a soft landing — it is a near-vertical drop in the rate of new spending. That means the marginal data center being commissioned today is being underwritten against a demand environment that will look very different by the time it comes online. Data center construction timelines run 18 to 36 months from commitment to operational capacity, so a facility greenlit now on 2026 demand assumptions will open into a 2028 market growing at 12%. The gap between the assumption baked into the financing and the reality at ribbon-cutting is where the credit losses will live.

The circular financing dynamic flagged in the take has a specific structural implication worth naming. When Microsoft funds OpenAI, OpenAI buys Azure compute, and Azure books that as revenue justifying further capex, the system is internally consistent only as long as the labs keep growing. A lab that stumbles — on revenue, on a capability plateau, on a pricing war with open-weight alternatives — doesn't just miss its own numbers. It simultaneously reduces the hyperscaler's revenue, weakens the collateral supporting the structured debt, and removes a buyer from the compute market the hyperscaler just spent billions building out. The contagion path is tighter than a standard enterprise customer relationship because the equity and the revenue are on the same balance sheet.

The power, labor, and memory chip bottlenecks Goldman flags as risks to the timeline are also worth reading as a partial brake on the downside scenario. If physical constraints slow deployment, some of the excess capacity problem self-corrects before it fully materializes — though it does so by making the capex even less productive per dollar spent, not by improving the return profile.

What the source material doesn't resolve — and what matters most for anyone pricing the equity of these five companies — is whether the 48% cloud growth rate in Q2 2026 reflects durable end-customer demand or is itself partially inflated by lab spending funded by hyperscaler investment. Goldman's note raises the question without answering it. That ambiguity, sitting underneath a $1.2 trillion headline, is the actual story.

Tech & AI China AI Geopolitics Developer Tools

Chinese models crossed 50% token share on two major developer platforms in seven months.

cnbc.com
  • On OpenRouter, Chinese AI models went from 6–13% of tokens in February to 57–67% in the week of September 14, 2026.
  • On Vercel, Chinese model share rose from 11% in January to 55% in August — the majority of traffic on both platforms developers actually route through.
  • Two-thirds of tokens from OpenRouter's 82-country 'Global South' cohort now run on Chinese models, with two House committees now investigating the trend.

The take

This is the eighth time this section has tracked the same migration in three weeks, and the numbers have now crossed a line that changes the conversation. On OpenRouter, Chinese models went from 6-13% of tokens in February to 57-67% in the week of September 14. On Vercel, 11% in January to 55% in August. This is no longer a benchmark story or a pricing footnote. It's a majority-share event on the two gateways that most enterprise developers actually route through, and it happened in roughly seven months.

The framing everyone reaches for — US labs keep the high-value frontier work, China takes the commodity floor — is exactly the segmentation defense that gets repeated when a premium vendor is losing the volume that pays for its R&D. It was flagged here on September 16 that US frontier had been repriced as a specialty vendor, and this is what specialty vendor actually looks like in the usage data: the American labs still capture most of the dollars because they charge more per token, but they're losing the workload count that determines who the next generation of developers builds habits around. Spend is a lagging indicator. Token share is the leading one.

Watch what OpenAI and Anthropic did the same week this data dropped. Both shipped cheaper models. Anthropic's product head described the goal as making answers "more efficient, so it uses less tokens." Read that plainly: the frontier labs are now optimizing to consume fewer of their own tokens per task, which is another way of saying they've accepted the price ceiling China set and are cutting cost to defend share. That's a margin-compression move dressed up as an efficiency feature. It's the same reactive pattern as Anthropic cutting Opus 5.5 pricing the day Xiaomi's MiMo launched.

The part worth underwriting is the geography. Two-thirds of tokens from OpenRouter's "Global South" cohort — 82 countries from Lagos to Jakarta to São Paulo — now run on Chinese models. This is where the CNAS worry is actually correct, and where Washington's response is actively counterproductive. Export controls stop China from buying advanced Nvidia chips; they do nothing to stop a Jakarta startup from calling a DeepSeek endpoint. You cannot sanction an API that's cheaper and good enough. And every House committee investigation into "the impact of rising adoption" is free marketing for the proposition that Chinese models are now important enough to fear.

If you're allocating against this, the frontier-lab equity story only works if the capability lead keeps widening faster than the price gap. Mozilla's own report put that lead at 4.4 months and shrinking. A four-month edge does not support a premium on the 80% of enterprise work — extraction, code completion, drafting — where the cheap model already clears the bar. The distribution layer is being lost in the developing world right now, quietly, one default routing choice at a time. That's the asset that doesn't come back.

The other side

Dollar share still favors US labs, and that gap is not trivial — American frontier models charge more per token and still capture the majority of enterprise AI spending. More importantly, regulated buyers in financial services, healthcare, and defense face real procurement constraints around Chinese-origin software that won't dissolve because a model is cheaper; for those segments, the frontier premium is structural, not just habitual. If the capability lead widens again — one major benchmark break by a US lab — the volume migration could reverse before the habit layer fully sets.

The mechanism here is worth spelling out precisely, because the 'US keeps the high-value work' defense sounds reasonable until you trace where developer habits actually form. Enterprise developers don't choose a model category in the abstract; they choose a default endpoint, and defaults are sticky. The workload that gets routed to a Chinese model for code completion today is the same workload that trains the next generation of developers to treat that model as the baseline. Spend follows habit, not the other way around. Token share is the upstream variable.

The pricing dynamic reinforces this. OpenRouter's head of insights told CNBC that Chinese open-source models released this year 'can credibly perform in advanced agentic use cases, especially in regards to coding, in a way that was just not true in late 2025.' Vercel's head of agentic infrastructure put it more bluntly: once a model meets the quality bar for the job, the price difference becomes compelling. That's not a niche observation — coding and extraction are the two highest-volume enterprise AI workloads. The cheap model doesn't need to win on every task; it only needs to clear the bar on the tasks that generate the most volume.

The US labs' response this week is instructive. Both OpenAI and Anthropic shipped cheaper models in the same week the OpenRouter and Vercel data surfaced. Anthropic's product head framed the goal as making answers 'more efficient, so it uses less tokens.' Translated: Anthropic is now engineering its models to consume fewer of their own tokens per task. That is a margin-compression move — the company is accepting a price ceiling set by Chinese competitors and cutting internal cost to defend share rather than expanding it. The pattern matches what this section flagged on September 23, when Anthropic cut Claude Opus 5.5 pricing the same day Xiaomi's MiMo launched. Reactive pricing is not a strategy; it's evidence that the price anchor has already shifted.

The geography is where the long-run structural damage is clearest. OpenRouter's Global South cohort covers 82 countries across Central and South America, Africa, and Asia. Two-thirds of those companies' tokens now run on Chinese models. CNAS senior fellow Daniel Remler told CNBC that the deeper concern is not just market share but geopolitical alignment — that integrating Chinese AI pulls countries into a Chinese technology sphere that hardens over time. That framing is correct, and Washington's current toolkit is almost entirely mismatched to the problem. Export controls on advanced Nvidia chips constrain what Chinese labs can build at the frontier; they do nothing to stop a startup in Lagos or Jakarta from calling a DeepSeek API endpoint. You cannot sanction a cheaper, capable model that is already deployed and publicly accessible.

The House committee investigations are, if anything, counterproductive on the margin — every headline about Washington scrutinizing Chinese AI adoption functions as confirmation that these models are now consequential enough to threaten US interests, which is exactly the signal that accelerates adoption among governments and entrepreneurs looking for alternatives to US-controlled infrastructure. The CNAS concern about Southeast Asia is particularly well-grounded given the close economic ties Remler cited; the region's digital infrastructure buildout is happening now, and the default routing choices being made today will be expensive to reverse in five years.

Geopolitics Iran Middle East Energy

Trump rejected Iran's seven-day Hormuz offer. The midterm calendar explains why.

reuters.com
  • Iran's proposal would reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days, with the transit-fee demand moved to a side attachment — the first structural concession on that issue in eight months
  • The WSJ reported Trump has rejected the offer; the 30% odds of a deal before the November 3 midterms cited last Thursday are now lower
  • Saudi Arabia and the UAE brokered the proposal Trump walked away from, exposing how thin their leverage over Washington actually is

The take

Ninth time on the Hormuz file since early August, and the delta is clean: Iran put a seven-day reopening offer on the table with the transit-fee demand moved to a side attachment, and per the WSJ, Trump has now rejected it. Last Thursday the framing was odds of a deal before the midterms at 30%. That number is now lower, and the reason matters more than the headline.

The instinct is to read the rejection as Trump holding out for better terms. That's the wrong lens. Iran spent eight months treating the strait as a policy lever rather than a logistics problem — refusing to activate a technical lane it already had with Oman, sorting the region into compliance and resistance buckets. Now Tehran has blinked far enough to offer a hard seven-day clock and to demote its fee demand to an annex. And Washington walked. That sequence tells you the deal itself isn't the objective on either side.

Look at what a reopening would actually resolve versus what Trump is optimizing for. A November 3 midterm is five weeks out. A signed Iran deal that lifts the naval blockade and eases pressure is a five-week gift to Tehran with no time to verify a single commitment before voters go to the polls — the same structural flaw as the August Truth Social "framework" and the 2025 nuclear talks: relief up front, verification left blank. Trump has already floated "wiping Iran out" on Fox. Between a soft deal that reads as capitulation and a continued blockade that reads as strength, the campaign math points one direction, and it isn't peace.

So the real question is who absorbs the cost of the strait staying shut through the election. It isn't primarily America. Traffic already collapsed to the low thirties in vessels per week back in August, and the market has repriced Gulf risk accordingly. The people bleeding are the Gulf brokers — Saudi Arabia and the UAE, whose ADNOC tankers keep taking missile hits and whose capital is deeply tied to a de-escalation Washington just declined to sign. Riyadh got hit directly last week. They brokered the offer Trump rejected. They now discover their leverage over their own security guarantor is thinner than the "Mideast allies did it" language implied.

For anyone with Gulf energy or sovereign-fund exposure, the operative fact is that a reopening is now hostage to a US electoral calendar rather than to Iranian conditions. The one-week clock Tehran offered means nothing until a US administration wants the file closed — and this one has a five-week reason to keep it open. Price the strait as structurally shut through early November, and treat any pre-election reopening as the surprise, not the base case. The Gulf states will spend that window learning how much of their premium was borrowed against a deal Washington was never in a hurry to make.

The other side

The case for rejection on the merits is real: Iran has spent eight months weaponizing the strait as a policy lever, refused to activate a technical lane it already had with Oman, and has a documented pattern of pocketing relief before verification catches up. A seven-day clock with fee terms demoted to an annex is still a deal where Iran gets naval blockade relief and frozen-asset movement before a single commitment is confirmed — the same structural flaw that made the August Truth Social framework and the 2025 nuclear talks hollow. Holding out for a harder verification architecture before any pressure lifts is a defensible position, not just campaign theater.

The sequencing here is worth sitting with. Iran's concession — moving the transit-fee demand off the main agreement into a side attachment — was described in last Thursday's coverage as the first structural give on that issue in eight months of talks. That is a meaningful move. Tehran was not offering a vague framework; it was offering a hard seven-day operational clock on reopening the strait, which handles roughly 20% of global oil and a third of seaborne LNG daily. The rejection lands differently depending on which actor you are watching. For Iran, the damage is limited in the short run. The strait closed as a policy choice, and it stays closed as a policy choice — Tehran loses nothing it was already holding. The IRGC keeps its leverage, the frozen assets stay frozen, and the naval blockade continues to function as a recruiting argument domestically.

For the Gulf states, the exposure is sharper. Saudi Arabia and the UAE positioned themselves as the brokers who could move Washington — the 'Mideast allies' language in the August Truth Social post was their calling card. Riyadh took a direct missile strike on King Khalid International Airport last week. ADNOC, Abu Dhabi's state oil company, has had 15 tankers hit since the conflict began. They assembled an offer, got Iran to make a structural concession, and then watched the US decline to sign. That sequence does not strengthen their hand in the next round of negotiations; it advertises the ceiling of their influence.

The electoral math driving this is straightforward. November 3 is five weeks out. A deal that lifts the naval blockade and eases military pressure on Iran in the next five weeks gives Tehran tangible relief with no time for any verification to register before voters go to the polls. Trump has already said publicly he is considering 'wiping Iran out.' The distance between that posture and a signed reopening agreement is not a negotiating gap — it is a campaign positioning gap, and those do not close on a seven-day clock.

The practical implication for anyone with Gulf energy or sovereign-fund exposure is that the reopening timeline is now governed by the US electoral calendar, not by Iranian conditions. Iran already made the concession that was supposed to unlock talks. Washington declined anyway. That means the next credible window for a deal opens after November 3, assuming the administration wants the file closed at all — and a post-midterm Trump with a strong result has less pressure to close it quickly than a pre-midterm Trump with a race to win.

Shipping traffic through the strait had already collapsed to the low thirties in vessels per week by August, down from fifty the prior week, and the market has had months to reprice Gulf risk. The marginal news here is not that the strait is shut — that is priced. The marginal news is that the party with the most to lose from it staying shut, the Gulf broker states, just learned their leverage over the reopening decision is thinner than the diplomatic language suggested.
