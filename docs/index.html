Family Office & Wealth Planning China Tax Policy UHNW Asia

Beijing's offshore tax probe reaches back to 2000 and enforcement is deliberately uneven

ft.com
  • From October 22, China begins collecting a 20% tax on offshore trust dividends and interest — but officials are already probing capital gains as far back as 2000, well beyond the announced 2023 start date
  • Enforcement is explicitly discretionary across provincial authorities: the National Development and Reform Commission personally contacted a Beijing internet executive to pressure compliance, turning tax exposure into a political instrument
  • Barclays analysts describe the trust rules as potentially the first step toward taxing exporter earnings held offshore, overseas investment income, overseas employment income, and eventually estate or inheritance transfers

The take

Fourth time on this since late August, and the deadline is now effectively here — collection begins October 22. What's new in the FT's reporting isn't the trust tax itself but the scope: officials are probing offshore capital gains back to 2000, not 2023, and enforcement is explicitly discretionary across provincial authorities. That changes the nature of the problem for every family office touching Chinese money.

The retroactivity to 2000 is the part the advisory industry hasn't absorbed. A 20% levy on offshore trust appreciation from 2023 is a known, quantifiable liability — painful, but you can write the check. A review that reaches back a quarter-century turns this from a tax bill into an open-ended discovery process where the family office is reconstructing records it never kept because, in 2004, there was no rule requiring them. Harry Yu at Fung Yu calls it "archaeology." That's precisely right, and archaeology has no fixed price. You cannot reserve against a liability whose base year is unknown and whose enforcement is, by Beijing's own practice, selective.

Discretionary enforcement is the mechanism that matters here, and it's being read as leniency. It's the opposite. When the National Development and Reform Commission is personally nudging a Beijing internet executive to pay, the tax stops being a rule and becomes leverage — a lever Beijing can pull against any individual entrepreneur it wants to pressure for unrelated reasons. For UHNW Chinese families, the binary isn't "comply or don't." It's "stay inside a system where your tax exposure is a political variable, or exit tax residency and eat the one-off 20% departure charge." Expect a wave of the latter — naturalizations, residency changes, the deliberate severing of mainland tax ties — and expect Beijing to tighten the exit tax precisely because that's the pressure valve.

Two things follow for the money centers. First, Singapore, Hong Kong and Tokyo are being framed as the losers, and the New York bankers quoted smelling opportunity think they're the winners. That's backwards. A US banker promising to shield a Chinese client from Beijing's taxman is offering a product that collides directly with CRS reporting and with Washington's own appetite to know who holds what — and the US isn't a CRS signatory, which is exactly why it looks like a haven and exactly why it will attract scrutiny. The liability doesn't disappear; it moves to a jurisdiction with its own hungry revenue agency.

Second, the repricing. The offshore trust was sold to a generation of Chinese entrepreneurs as a permanent, opaque wrapper. It is now neither. Every structure built on the premise that Beijing couldn't see through it has to be re-underwritten at a lower value, because the core service it provided — invisibility — is gone. The firms in Singapore fielding the inquiry surge aren't booking a windfall. They're managing the controlled demolition of their own most profitable product, and the smart ones are already selling the replacement: clean residency, real disclosure, and structures that assume the taxman reads everything.

The other side

China's fiscal position makes this campaign structurally necessary, not merely ideological. Local government revenue from land sales has plateaued or fallen, the central government is covering more provincial spending on education, health, and civil service salaries, and Victor Shih at UC San Diego says the trajectory is becoming untenable. A 20% tax on offshore trust income is a relatively targeted instrument compared to the alternatives — broad consumption taxes or cuts to public services — and bringing China's treatment of offshore wealth in line with US and European norms is a defensible policy goal. If the campaign raises meaningful revenue without triggering mass capital flight, Beijing will have demonstrated that the offshore trust era was an anomaly, not a permanent feature of the system.

The FT's reporting adds two facts that materially change the calculus for every adviser and family office with Chinese client exposure. The first is the retroactive reach: while the announced rules cover 2023 to 2025, officials are in practice reviewing offshore investments going back to 2000. That is not a tax bill. It is an open-ended audit of decisions made before the rules existed, using records that were never required to be kept. Harry Yu at Fung Yu Trust Services — a Hong Kong firm specializing in cross-border wealth structuring for China-connected families — describes the compliance work as archaeology. The metaphor is exact: you are reconstructing a site that was never meant to be excavated, and the cost of the dig is unknowable before you start.

The second new fact is the mechanism of discretionary enforcement. Beijing has not published a uniform collection schedule. Instead, provincial tax authorities are applying pressure at varying levels of severity, and in at least one documented case the National Development and Reform Commission — China's top economic planning body, not a tax agency — directly contacted a company executive about unpaid obligations. When a planning commission is doing tax collection, the tax has become something else: a tool of administrative leverage that can be applied to any entrepreneur for any reason, including reasons that have nothing to do with offshore income. The families reading discretionary enforcement as a sign of leniency have the logic inverted. Selective enforcement means your liability is not determined by the code; it is determined by your relationship with the state at the moment they choose to look.

This dynamic is already driving the decision that Beijing most wants to prevent: deliberate exit from mainland tax residency. The rules impose a one-off 20% departure charge on trust assets when a Chinese individual acquires foreign citizenship or ceases to be a mainland tax resident. That charge looks punitive until you compare it to the alternative — remaining inside a system where your historical offshore exposure is an open variable and your compliance cost has no ceiling. For families with large, complex offshore structures, the departure tax is a known, finite number. The archaeology is not. Expect naturalizations and residency changes to accelerate before Beijing closes that exit, which it will, because the departure option is the pressure valve the current system cannot afford to leave open.

The money-center implications cut against the obvious read. New York bankers quoted in the FT's reporting see an opportunity to capture Chinese clients fleeing Beijing's reach. The US is not a signatory to the Common Reporting Standard — the global framework under which most financial centers automatically share account data with clients' home countries — which makes it look like a structural haven. But that non-signatory status is precisely what draws regulatory attention. A US institution marketing itself as a shield against a foreign tax authority is operating in territory that Washington's own revenue agencies and financial regulators have historically found interesting. The liability does not disappear by crossing the Pacific; it relocates to a jurisdiction with its own enforcement appetite and its own disclosure expectations.

For the Singapore and Hong Kong advisory firms fielding the current inquiry surge, the honest framing is that they are managing the obsolescence of their core product. The offshore trust was sold to a generation of Chinese entrepreneurs on the premise that Beijing could not see through it. That premise is gone. The firms that survive this transition are the ones already selling the replacement architecture: structures built on the assumption of full visibility, clean residency documentation, and disclosure frameworks that treat the taxman as a permanent counterparty rather than an obstacle to route around.

Private Markets Secondaries Wealth Channel Europe

ICG launches the exact product its CEO warned institutional LPs to avoid.

secondaryscoop.com, icgam.com
  • Amundi completed its 9.9% economic stake in ICG for about €620 million in September, with the equity stake and 10-year exclusive distribution deal now both active.
  • The evergreen vehicle is run by Oliver Gardey and Ryan Levitt — the same co-portfolio managers simultaneously raising ICG's next closed-end institutional LP secondaries fund, which Durteste said would be in market through March 2027.
  • Evercore data shows LP-led dry powder fell 15% in H1 2026 to $90 billion while roughly $40 billion of new evergreen and retail money entered the LP-led buyer pool over the same period, pushing quality buyout interests to around 90% of NAV.

The take

ICG and Amundi launched Amundi ICG Global Private Equity Secondaries this week, an evergreen vehicle that sells wealth clients access to ICG's LP-led secondaries strategy — buying existing fund stakes from limited partners across North America and Europe. This is the eighth European manager to bolt secondaries onto a wealth-distribution wrapper, and the first product to come out of a partnership where Amundi, a €2.6 trillion traditional house, took a 9.9% economic stake in ICG for about €620 million and locked in a ten-year exclusive right to sell its evergreen products.

The part worth watching is the conflict the firm hasn't addressed. ICG's CEO Benoît Durteste stood up at FY26 results in May and explicitly positioned the firm against asset gatherers — he said institutional LPs are wary of managers whose deployment depends on "the ebbs and flows of wealth capital in evergreen vehicles," and that they worry about how deals get allocated. Five months later ICG launches exactly that kind of vehicle, run by Oliver Gardey and Ryan Levitt — the same two names raising ICG's next institutional LP secondaries fund, which Durteste said would be in market through March 2027. Same team, same deal pipeline, two pools of capital with different fee structures and different liquidity promises, and no published allocation policy. That is the single disclosure that matters here, and it's missing.

The economics underneath are quietly deteriorating for everyone in this trade. The day-one markup — buy an LP stake below NAV, mark it to NAV at the next valuation, book an instant gain — is what sells these funds to private banks. But Evercore counts roughly $40 billion of fresh evergreen and retail money added to the LP-led buyer pool over the last twelve months, while LP-led dry powder actually fell 15% to $90 billion. More bidders, less selectively deployed capital, and pricing on quality buyout interests already sitting near 90% of NAV. Each new launch — Coller via Deutsche Bank, Ardian via iCapital, now ICG via Amundi — bids up the very discount that justifies the product. The marketing premise erodes in direct proportion to the product's success.

So the thing Amundi actually bought for €620 million isn't ICG's underwriting edge. It's an origination funnel pointed at 200 million individual investors and the right to be first through it. ICG gets permanent, non-redeemable-at-will capital and a distribution machine it could never build alone; Amundi gets a manufactured product it chose to source externally rather than run through its own Zurich secondaries platform. Both sides are solving distribution, not returns. The wealth client is buying a diversified mature-asset portfolio at compressing discounts, and paying evergreen fees for a J-curve someone else already climbed. Fine as an allocation — just don't underwrite the day-one gain as if it's durable. It's being competed away in real time.

The other side

The structural critique of evergreen vehicles assumes institutional and retail capital are chasing identical assets at identical prices, but ICG's platform — including its listed PE fund-of-funds — gives the team information and access advantages that a pure-play retail distributor can't replicate. If ICG can source deals that don't clear through the main auction market, the compression dynamic is real but overstated. And Amundi's 200-million-investor network isn't just a distribution pipe — it's a source of permanent, patient capital that actually lengthens ICG's investment horizon rather than shortening it, which is a genuine edge in a market where sellers increasingly care about counterparty certainty.

The timeline itself is a signal. When ICG and Amundi announced their alliance in November 2025, they committed to two European evergreen funds — one in PE secondaries, one in private debt — by mid-2026. The secondaries fund arrived roughly a quarter late. The private debt fund hasn't been announced at all. A partnership that took eleven months to produce one product, and appears to have quietly shelved the second, is not running ahead of schedule on execution.

The equity stake closing in September and the fund launching six days later is not coincidence — it's sequencing. Amundi needed the stake closed before it could formally distribute the product, and ICG needed the distribution machine live before its next institutional fundraise gets deep into the market. The timing serves both firms' near-term calendars, which is fine, but it means the allocation question — which deals go to the evergreen and which go to the closed-end fund — is live right now, not theoretical.

ICG's own website describes the evergreen as offering exposure to "the same deals" as the closed-end programs. That is either a selling point or a conflict disclosure, depending on which pool of capital you're in. Institutional LPs in the next closed-end vintage are paying closed-end fees and accepting illiquidity precisely because they expect priority or parity on deal flow. Wealth clients in the evergreen are paying evergreen fees and getting quarterly liquidity. If the deals are genuinely the same, one of those fee structures is mispriced. If they're not the same, the marketing language is misleading.

The broader European secondaries wealth market context matters here. ICG becomes the eighth European manager to sell secondaries through a wealth wrapper, joining Coller Capital via Deutsche Bank, Ardian via iCapital, and Eurazeo through French insurance structures, among others. Each of those entrants made the same pitch — mature assets, early distributions, entry discount — and each one added to the buyer pressure that compresses the discount the next entrant needs to make the pitch work. The market is not yet saturated, but it is crowding in a specific and measurable way.

Amundi's decision to source this product from ICG rather than run it through its own Amundi Alpha Associates secondaries platform in Zurich is worth noting. Amundi already has secondaries capability in-house. It chose to pay €620 million for an equity stake in an external manager and lock in a decade of exclusivity instead. That implies Amundi concluded its internal platform couldn't generate the deal flow or the brand credibility needed to sell this product at scale to private banks. ICG gets capital and distribution; Amundi gets a product it couldn't build fast enough internally. The wealth client gets a portfolio of mature buyout fund stakes at prices that, per Evercore's data, are already near 90% of NAV on quality assets — with the day-one markup that private banks use to justify the allocation getting thinner with every new vehicle that enters the market.

Tech & AI Infrastructure AI Capex Credit Risk

The AI buildout's $10.3 trillion financing stack is hiding its own leverage

brookings.edu, tokenpost.com, afr.com
  • Van Nieuwerburgh's Brookings paper estimates AI infrastructure spending at 3.63% of US GDP annually through 2032 — 50% larger than the railroad boom at its peak
  • Meta and Microsoft are both spending beyond their operating cash flow, funding the gap through leases, joint ventures, and special-purpose vehicles that keep debt off the balance sheet
  • Roughly 2,061 gigawatts of generation and storage sit in interconnection queues, meaning chip orders financed today may not draw power until 2028 or later

The take

A new Brookings paper from Columbia's Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh puts a number on the AI buildout: $10.3 trillion in data centers, power, and chips from 2025 through 2032, averaging 3.63% of US GDP per year. That's roughly 50% larger than the railroad boom at its peak and more than three times the internet/fiber boom. The headline is the size. The part worth your attention is where he says the money is coming from.

Van Nieuwerburgh's actual point isn't that the spending is big — everyone knows that. It's that Meta and Microsoft are now spending more on AI than their operating cash flow brings in, so the balance has to be financed, and it's being financed through leases, joint ventures, and special-purpose vehicles specifically structured to keep the leverage off the balance sheet where you can see it. His worked example is Meta's Hyperion project. The mechanism is debt that doesn't look like debt until something stops servicing it.

We've been tracking the individual instances of this for two weeks without naming the pattern. Broadcom lending Anthropic $42 billion to lease Broadcom's own chips, with an equity conversion right if Anthropic can't pay. Samsung's $1 billion "investment" in Helix that's really a vendor contract. AMD locking five-year supply against customers whose revenue is short-dated. Each one moves the obligation into a structure where the counterparty risk is circular — the lender, the chip supplier, and the equity holder are frequently the same three parties wearing different hats. Van Nieuwerburgh has just given the academic frame for what the deal flow has been screaming.

Now layer the physical constraint on top. The power-bottleneck reporting matters because it changes the timing of when these obligations convert from paper to cash. Roughly 2,061 gigawatts of generation and storage sit in interconnection queues. Nvidia's Ohio project doesn't phase online until 2028. Dell is carrying a $95 billion AI-server backlog. The chips get ordered and financed years before the power exists to run them — which means the lease payments and the SPV coupons come due on a schedule set by grid connections, substations, and transformers, not by revenue. GPU suppliers can steer boxes to sites that already have power. Memory, optical, and power-management vendors are tied to facility completion, which is the slow variable.

So the question isn't whether demand holds — Nvidia at $89 billion in quarterly data-center revenue, Broadcom's AI semis up 221%, settles that for now. It's whether a financing stack built on hidden leverage can survive an 18-to-24-month gap between when the debt starts accruing and when the concrete actually draws power. China's answer in Inner Mongolia — 89 data centers at Ulanqab, abundant electricity, no permitting fight — is to remove the slow variable entirely. The West is financing around it instead, and financing around a physical constraint is how good vintages become bad ones.

The other side

The off-balance-sheet structures Van Nieuwerburgh flags are not inherently fraudulent — they are standard project-finance tools used in every major infrastructure buildout, from LNG terminals to toll roads, precisely because they match long-duration assets to long-duration capital. The companies using them — Meta, Microsoft — have balance sheets and cash generation that dwarf most sovereign borrowers, and the lenders extending private credit into these structures are doing so with eyes open and collateral in hand. If the underlying demand signal holds — and Nvidia's $89 billion quarterly data-center revenue suggests it does — the leverage resolves itself through utilization, not distress.

Say more →

Van Nieuwerburgh's paper is useful not because it discovers something hidden but because it forces a consolidated view of what has been visible only in fragments. The Brookings framing puts the aggregate at $10.3 trillion across data centers, power, and chips from 2025 to 2032. To calibrate that: the railroad boom peaked at roughly 2.2% of GDP annually over its best two decades; the internet and fiber buildout ran about 1.1%. The AI number, at 3.63%, sits in a category of its own in American economic history.

The financing shift is the mechanism that makes this structurally interesting rather than just large. When a company spends within its operating cash flow, the risk is visible on its income statement and balance sheet. When it leases a data center through a special-purpose vehicle — a separately capitalized legal entity set up to hold a specific asset, keeping the associated debt off the parent's books — the obligation still exists, but it requires active digging to find and aggregate. Van Nieuwerburgh uses Meta's Hyperion project as his worked example of how this functions in practice. The paper doesn't call it fraud; it calls it opacity, which is a different and in some ways more durable problem, because opacity survives conditions that would expose outright fraud.

The deals this brief has tracked over the past two weeks now read as a pattern rather than a series of one-offs. Broadcom extended a $42 billion lending facility to Anthropic to finance Anthropic's lease of Broadcom's own chips — with an equity conversion right if Anthropic can't service the debt. The lender, the chip supplier, and the prospective equity holder are the same entity. Samsung's $1 billion commitment to Helix Digital Infrastructure, a data-center platform launched in June 2026, was structured as an investment but functions as a vendor relationship — Samsung gets a customer, Helix gets capital, and the risk sits in a vehicle that is neither clearly on Samsung's books nor clearly off them. AMD is locking customers into five-year supply commitments at a moment when those customers' revenue visibility extends perhaps 12 to 18 months. In each case, the obligation is real; the question is where it appears and when.

The power constraint sharpens the timing problem considerably. The 2,061 gigawatts sitting in interconnection queues represent projects that have applied to connect to the grid but have not yet received approval, built the necessary substations, or secured the transformers and transmission lines required to actually deliver electricity. Nvidia has publicly flagged that shortages of land, power, shell capacity, and capital could affect future revenue — and its Ohio project, one of the largest single AI infrastructure commitments announced, phases online beginning in 2028. Dell is carrying a $95 billion AI-server backlog, meaning orders are booked and presumably financed well ahead of the facilities that will house the equipment.

This creates a gap between the financing schedule and the physical schedule. Lease payments and SPV coupons accrue on a calendar set by contract. Power arrives on a calendar set by grid operators, permitting agencies, and transformer manufacturers. When those two calendars diverge by 18 to 24 months — which the current queue data suggests is plausible — the structures that were designed to match long-duration assets to long-duration capital instead become short-duration liabilities against long-duration delivery timelines.

China's approach in Inner Mongolia removes this variable by fiat. Ulanqab alone has 89 data centers built or planned, built at speed with abundant electricity and no meaningful permitting opposition. The West's answer is financial engineering around the constraint rather than removal of it. Van Nieuwerburgh stops short of calling this a bubble, which is the intellectually honest position given that demand remains genuinely strong. But he is explicit that measurement and disclosure need to improve while the capital structure is still forming — because once these structures are seasoned and widely held, unwinding the opacity becomes much harder than preventing it.

Wildcard AI Biotech Anthropic

Anthropic's autonomy claim and its data-hygiene claim cannot both be true at once.

scrippsnews.com, theconversation.com
  • Mestre's patent filing on jumbo-phage reverse transcriptases dates to July 2023, predating Anthropic's announcement, and he says his dissertation drafts fed into his private Claude account describe 'essentially the same finding'
  • In ten additional test runs after the initial discovery, Claude's agents missed the repeat array entirely — the core of the claimed breakthrough — undermining the 'autonomous discovery' framing
  • Anthropic's biology lab tool, Claude Science, competes directly with OpenAI's GPT-Rosalind, Microsoft's Quine, and Google DeepMind's Co-Scientist — all of which depend on scientists uploading sensitive pre-publication data to function

The take

Second time on the Anthropic ARTs claim in ten days. Since the September 30 take, the dispute has sharpened from "they don't know what it does" to "whose work was it in the first place." Mestre now says explicitly that his unpublished dissertation and manuscript drafts, which he fed into his private Claude account, describe "essentially the same finding" — and the patent record shows his team filed on jumbo-phage reverse transcriptases back in July 2023. Anthropic denies training on user transcripts. The honest answer, per every outside scientist quoted, is that the available evidence cannot resolve it either way.

That irresolvability is the whole story, and it is a liability question dressed as a science question. Anthropic made two incompatible marketing moves at once. It wants credit for an autonomous "Claude-led discovery" — Amodei's phrase, which moved gene-editing stocks down on announcement — while simultaneously claiming the model never saw the one researcher's confidential files that would explain the result mundanely. You cannot sell maximal autonomy and maximal data hygiene in the same press release. If Claude is brilliant enough to find this unprompted, it is also brilliant enough to have absorbed and regurgitated someone's dissertation, and the firm has no clean way to prove which happened.

The commercial damage is already visible and it is the thing to actually track. Mestre, a paying user, has stopped using the product. On September 30 the Eli Lilly CEO — a customer, not a jealous academic — endorsed the critique. Finkelstein at UT Austin says researchers will now think twice before inputting unpublished results into these tools. That is the knock-on effect: the entire value proposition of Claude Science, GPT-Rosalind, DeepMind's Co-Scientist and Microsoft's Quine rests on scientists uploading their most sensitive, pre-publication, pre-patent material. The moment a lab believes its competitive edge might surface in a rival's "discovery," the funnel dries up. You are asking a researcher to hand their unfiled IP to a counterparty that also runs a biology lab with prize ambitions. No serious PI does that twice.

Read the actual document and the autonomy claim gets thinner still. In ten additional runs the agents missed the repeat array entirely. The enzyme itself was already identified by a Texas A&M group. Key evidence came from reanalyzing a 2022 dataset produced by someone else. Four of six authors are senior molecular biologists, and the lead trained in Jennifer Doudna's lab. This is expert-directed grunt search — genuinely useful, genuinely faster — relabeled as machine genius for the funding narrative. The gap between "interesting pattern" and "working gene editor," as King's College London's Sudre put it, is where all the work still lives, and none of it is done.

The mispricing is not in gene-editing equities, which overreacted to a preliminary, unpeer-reviewed result. It is in the lab-platform businesses themselves. Their moat was supposed to be proprietary scientific data flowing in. One unresolved provenance fight just taught every researcher in the field to keep the good stuff off the server.

The other side

Simultaneous discovery is genuinely common in science — Watson and Crick, Darwin and Wallace, calculus twice — and the fact that two groups converged on the same unusual enzyme family in bacteriophages may say more about the maturity of the underlying database than about any data leak. Anthropic's commercial and personal service tiers have different training rules, and the company explicitly denies training on user transcripts. If the biology holds up under peer review, the provenance dispute becomes a footnote rather than the headline, and the demonstration that 950 agents can compress months of bioinformatic search into 21 hours remains a real, if incremental, capability advance.

Say more →

The provenance question matters more than the biology question right now, because it is the one with commercial consequences that compound.

Start with what the source material actually establishes. Mestre's US patent application, published July 2023, lists reverse transcriptase sequences from jumbo phages — the same class of virus at the center of Anthropic's announcement. That filing documents earlier work on the enzyme family, though the outside expert quoted in The Conversation is careful to note it does not by itself prove Mestre's team had already characterized the full system Anthropic describes. The distinction matters: Anthropic's claim is not just that it found the enzyme, which the Texas A&M group had already done, but that it identified a larger biological system around it — repeated DNA sequences, a possible partner protein, and small RNA molecules produced by those sequences. Whether Mestre's unpublished work described that full system is precisely what cannot be resolved from the available evidence.

Anthropid's own technical document quietly undermines the autonomy framing from a different angle. The repeat array — the structural feature that makes the finding interesting — was missed in ten out of ten additional runs after the initial discovery. Some models could recognize the pattern when handed the relevant sequences directly, but the researchers themselves flag that recognizing a supplied pattern is different from finding it independently. That is a significant caveat to bury in a supplementary document after Amodei has already posted on X about a 'Claude-led discovery' that moved gene-editing stocks.

The lead author's background adds another layer. Dr. Peter Yoon trained in Jennifer Doudna's lab — Doudna shares the 2020 Nobel for CRISPR. Four of the six authors are described by Finkelstein at UT Austin as senior molecular biologists. The search was not a machine running loose on a database; it was a machine running a specific query — find 'interesting new examples' of reverse transcriptase — designed by people who already knew what interesting looked like. That is expert-directed computation, which is genuinely valuable, but it is a different product than the one being marketed.

The commercial exposure sits in the data flywheel, not in any single discovery claim. Claude Science, like its direct competitors, is structurally dependent on scientists uploading their most sensitive material: unpublished sequences, draft manuscripts, pre-patent analyses. The value of the tool scales with the quality of what goes in. But the moment a researcher believes that material could surface — even inadvertently, even through a training process the company itself may not fully audit — the rational move is to keep the good stuff local. Finkelstein said researchers will now think twice. Mestre has already stopped using the product entirely. The Eli Lilly CEO's public endorsement of the critique, noted in the September 30 coverage, came from a paying enterprise customer, not an academic competitor with a grievance.

Anthropics's policy documentation, as described in The Conversation, does not cleanly resolve the concern. Commercial tiers exclude inputs from training by default, but exceptions exist when users provide feedback or grant permission. Claude Science specifies that datasets can stay on the researcher's computer while the instructions and replies are processed by Anthropic. Researchers who did not read that distinction carefully — and most won't — may have shared more than they intended. The company's denial is plausible; it is not dispositive.

The broader race context makes this worse, not better. Every major AI lab has now launched a science-facing research tool. The competitive logic pushes all of them toward announcing discoveries that validate the platform. That incentive structure is exactly backwards from what careful science requires, and this episode is the first clear demonstration of the tension between the two.
