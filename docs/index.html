Private Markets Secondaries M&A LP Strategy

EQT bought the liquidity layer. The rest of private markets is still figuring that out.

  • Secondaries transaction volume hit $120 billion in H1 2026 — the strongest first half on record, up nearly 20% year-on-year — while the primary buyout exit market remains largely stalled
  • EQT paid $3.2 billion in stock for Coller Capital (one of the world's largest dedicated secondaries firms, founded 36 years ago), with key Coller managers contractually reinvesting most of their up-to-$500 million earnout back into EQT shares
  • The deal adds 9 new strategies and a new Secondaries business segment, lifting EQT's total AUM to €341 billion ($389 billion), with EQT committed to doubling Coller's fee-generating assets within four years

The take

EQT just closed its all-share purchase of Coller Capital, folding one of the world's oldest dedicated secondaries platforms into a new fourth business segment and lifting group AUM to €341 billion ($389 billion). The base price is $3.2 billion, paid in 80.4 million new EQT shares — about 7% of the company — plus up to $500 million contingent on Coller's performance through March 2029. The tell is buried in the deal mechanics: EQT paid almost entirely in its own paper, and key Coller managers are contractually reinvesting the bulk of their earnout back into EQT stock. Nobody sells a franchise they think is peaking for equity in the acquirer and then agrees to hold the acquirer's equity.

Read the Jeremy Coller quote literally, because he means it: "in the long-term secondaries will become private equity." That is not a founder puffing his exit. It is a structural claim, and it's the one thing in this release worth pricing. For thirty years secondaries were the plumbing — the discount bin where LPs dumped unwanted stakes at 15-cents-on-the-dollar in a bad year. What Coller is saying is that the plumbing is becoming the building. When primary exits stay jammed and IPO windows stay narrow, the only reliable way to convert paper marks into distributions is to trade the stakes themselves. $120 billion in H1 volume, up nearly 20% year-on-year, is the strongest first half on record — and that's happening while the underlying buyout machine is stalled, not because it's humming.

Here's the second-order move nobody's pricing. EQT didn't buy Coller to add a product line. It bought a permanent liquidity desk sitting on top of its own portfolio. A firm with €341 billion in assets and a jammed exit environment has a structural conflict and a structural opportunity in the same room: it can now originate GP-led secondaries and continuation vehicles — structures that let a manager hold a winning asset past a fund's normal life — on its own book, price them, and place them with its own insurance and private-wealth clients. The independence language around Coller's origination is there precisely because regulators and LPs will ask who's on both sides of the trade. Watch that governance question; it's where the fee economics actually live.

For allocators the read-across is blunt. The big platforms are no longer competing on buyout returns — Apollo, KKR, Ardian and now EQT are competing to own the liquidity layer of an asset class that can't exit itself. EQT committed to doubling Coller's fee-generating assets in four years, which tells you where management thinks the growth is, and it isn't in new LBOs. If you're an LP still treating secondaries as an opportunistic sleeve rather than the market's clearing mechanism, you're underwriting the wrong side of the trade.

The other side

The governance risk here is real and underappreciated. A firm that both manages primary PE assets and runs the secondaries desk pricing and placing those same assets is sitting on both sides of the trade — and the 'independence' language around Coller's origination process is a disclosure, not a solution. LPs and regulators have seen this movie before with captive placement arms, and the scrutiny will be proportional to how much GP-led deal flow EQT routes through its own secondaries platform. If that conflict becomes a headline, it doesn't just hurt Coller EQT — it puts EQT's broader LP relationships at risk across all four segments.

The deal structure itself is the most instructive data point. EQT issued roughly 80.4 million new shares — about 7% of the company — as the primary consideration, keeping cash outlay to a sliver of the $3.2 billion base price. Then it layered on up to $500 million in contingent consideration tied to Coller's performance through March 2029, with approximately 64% of that earnout going to key Coller managers who are contractually committed to reinvesting their net proceeds back into EQT stock. That is three layers of alignment: the seller took paper, the seller's key people are locked into the paper, and the earnout clock runs nearly three years. You don't architect a deal that way if either side thinks the secondaries market is near a top.

The market context makes the timing legible. Secondaries volume surpassing $120 billion in a single half-year, up nearly 20% from the same period a year earlier, is not a cyclical blip — it is a structural response to a primary market that cannot clear itself. When buyout funds can't IPO their winners and strategic M&A is slow, the only mechanism that actually returns capital to LPs is trading the stakes. Secondaries went from being the distressed-exit valve — the place you sold when you had no other choice — to being the market's primary liquidity mechanism. Jeremy Coller's claim that secondaries will eventually become private equity is a compressed way of saying that the secondary market is absorbing functions the primary market used to perform.

EQT's strategic logic goes beyond product diversification. A firm sitting on €341 billion in assets across buyout, infrastructure, and real estate has a permanent inventory problem in a jammed exit environment: marks on paper that LPs want converted to cash. Owning a dedicated secondaries platform means EQT can now structure GP-led transactions — continuation vehicles that let a manager hold a high-performing asset past a fund's normal life — originate them internally, and distribute them to the insurance and private-wealth clients that Coller EQT already serves. The combined evergreen platform, which the source material puts at over €10 billion in net asset value, is the distribution channel for exactly that product. EQT committed to doubling Coller's fee-generating AUM in four years; the mechanism for doing that is almost certainly GP-led deal flow, not just buying LP stakes on the open market.

The competitive read-across is worth sitting with. The large alternative asset managers — Apollo, KKR, Ardian, and now EQT — have all moved toward owning the full liquidity stack rather than competing purely on buyout returns. The race is no longer about who generates the best IRR on a 2019 vintage fund. It is about who controls the infrastructure through which private capital gets priced, traded, and ultimately returned. Secondaries is that infrastructure. EQT just paid $3.2 billion, plus a potential $500 million more, to own a piece of it. That price implies a view about where the growth is — and it is not in new leveraged buyouts.

Private Markets Real Estate Japan Pensions

Ares hit the hard cap on Japan warehouses. CPP's anchor check explains why.

  • JDP V closed at ¥612 billion (~$4 billion), hitting its hard cap and coming in nearly 50% larger than the 2021 predecessor fund JDP IV
  • CPP Investments — manager of a C$864 billion pension fund — committed ¥150 billion (~$968 million), roughly a quarter of the entire fund, and has backed every JDP vintage since 2011
  • The fund has already committed to projects representing approximately ¥450 billion in total investment out of ¥1.7 trillion in total investment capacity

The take

Ares just closed its fifth Japan logistics development fund at ¥612 billion — call it $4 billion at the ¥155 rate they used — hitting its hard cap and coming in nearly 50% larger than the 2021 vintage. In a fundraising market where the median PE vehicle now grinds for the better part of two years to reach a close, a real estate development fund oversubscribing to hard cap in Japan warehouses is the tell worth sitting with. This isn't a story about logistics rents. It's a story about where the world's most disciplined pension capital is choosing to compound.

Look at who anchored it. CPP Investments wrote ¥150 billion — roughly $968 million, a quarter of the entire fund — and it has been in every JDP vintage since 2011. That is not opportunism; that is a sovereign-scale allocator treating Japanese logistics development as a fifteen-year core holding. When the manager of a C$864 billion pension book concentrates a single cornerstone check that heavily into one GP's development pipeline, it's making a currency and rates bet as much as a real estate one: Japanese assets, yen-denominated, developed and leased locally, financed against the last positively-yielding cost of capital in the developed world. The carry math on building in Japan still works in a way it does not in a 5%-plus dollar or euro world.

The piece nobody is pricing is the GCP International acquisition Ares closed in March 2025. That's what turned a credit-and-buyout shop into a vertically integrated logistics developer running 655 million square feet globally under the Marq platform. This fund is the first big proof that the deal wasn't empire-building — it was a capability purchase that immediately lets Ares raise a bigger fund, faster, because LPs are underwriting an operating platform, not a promise to go find warehouses. The fund has already committed to roughly ¥450 billion of projects out of ¥1.7 trillion of total investment capacity. Capital that pre-deploys into a proprietary pipeline is capital that isn't sitting in a fee-drag dry-powder pile. That's the actual product LPs are buying: shovels in the ground on day one.

The read-across for allocators is that the bifurcation everyone talks about in buyout is now visible in real assets, and it favors the scaled, integrated operator. A pure capital-raiser pitching Japanese logistics against Ares' vertically integrated Marq platform is bringing a checkbook to a construction fight. And the second-order signal for anyone allocating out of Singapore: the smart pension money isn't chasing AI multiples or distressed US office. It's quietly building physical supply-chain infrastructure in the one major market where the cost of capital still rewards development. When CPP writes the anchor check, that's not a rent thesis — it's a statement about where real yield lives.

The other side

The yen carry trade has a long history of blowing up on the unwind, and a fund that is structurally long yen-denominated development assets is exposed if the Bank of Japan accelerates rate normalization faster than the market expects — compressing the cost-of-capital advantage that makes the development math work. Beyond currency risk, Japan's logistics market is not immune to oversupply: CBRE projects rent growth in major metros by late 2027, but that forecast assumes demand absorption keeps pace with the wave of new institutional supply that funds like this one are actively adding. If absorption slows, a fund that has already pre-committed ¥450 billion into a proprietary pipeline has limited ability to pump the brakes.

The fundraising context matters here. Closing a real estate development fund at hard cap — oversubscribed, not just fully subscribed — in the current environment is genuinely unusual. Development funds carry construction risk on top of the leasing and financing risk that core real estate already carries, which means they typically face more LP scrutiny, not less, in a cycle where rates have punished leveraged real assets globally. The fact that JDP V closed nearly 50% larger than JDP IV, which itself raised ¥412 billion in 2021, suggests LPs are not just rolling over prior commitments — they are actively sizing up.

The GCP International acquisition, which Ares closed in March 2025, is the structural story underneath the fundraise. Before that deal, Ares was primarily a credit and private equity platform with real estate exposure. The acquisition brought in what is now branded as Marq Logistics, a vertically integrated development and operating platform that manages approximately 120 million square feet in Japan alone and over 655 million square feet globally as of June 30, 2026. That is not a financial engineering story — that is a physical infrastructure story. LPs underwriting JDP V are not betting on Ares' ability to source and acquire stabilized assets; they are underwriting an in-house development machine with a proprietary pipeline already loaded.

The pre-deployment point deserves more attention than it typically gets in fund announcements. JDP V has already committed to projects representing approximately ¥450 billion in total investment — that is roughly 73% of the fund's equity raise already spoken for before the ink is dry on the final close. For LPs, that changes the risk profile meaningfully. Dry powder sitting uninvested is a fee drag and a J-curve problem; capital committed to a proprietary development pipeline on day one is a different product. The fund's total investment capacity of ¥1.7 trillion — which includes leverage on top of the equity raise — gives a sense of the platform's actual deployment footprint, not just the fund size.

The geographic concentration is deliberate. JDP V targets Greater Tokyo, Greater Osaka, and Nagoya — Japan's three core logistics corridors — rather than spreading across emerging Asian markets where execution risk is higher and local relationships thinner. Ares has been operating in Japan since the JDP series launched in 2011, which means the platform has lived through multiple yen cycles, multiple supply waves, and multiple tenant negotiations. That institutional memory is part of what CPP is paying for when it writes a cornerstone check fifteen years into a relationship.

For allocators watching from the outside, the read-across is about platform scale as a fundraising moat. Ares Real Estate managed approximately $121 billion in assets as of June 30, 2026, across equity and debt strategies globally. A mid-sized manager pitching a Japan logistics strategy today is competing against a platform that controls the development pipeline, the operating infrastructure, and a decade and a half of LP relationships in the same market. That is a structural disadvantage that capital alone cannot close.

Family Office & Wealth Planning China Offshore Trusts Singapore Tax Policy

China's offshore trust deadline isn't a tax event. It's a forced disclosure.

  • China tightened its offshore trust tax rules one month ago, with an October deadline now forcing wealthy clients to reconstruct asset schedules, distribution histories, and beneficiary records going back years.
  • DLA Piper's Asia tax co-head Windson Li says most clients are underestimating the scope and have been slow to act — the compliance burden alone is described as 'a mix of everything.'
  • Advisers across Singapore and the region are fielding a surge of inquiries not just on settling tax bills but on rethinking cross-border structures entirely, signaling a longer-term repricing of offshore vehicles for Chinese UHNW families.

The take

One month ago, China tightened how it taxes offshore trusts held by its wealthy citizens, and there's an October deadline attached. Advisers in Singapore and Hong Kong describe clients scrambling to value assets, reconstruct distribution histories, and raise cash to settle bills — with DLA Piper's Asia tax co-head Windson Li flagging that most clients are underestimating the scope and have been slow to move. The consensus read is procedural: China is simply catching up to Western norms, taxing settlors and beneficiaries the way mature regimes already do. That framing is comfortable, and it misses what's actually being repriced.

The tell is in the phrase "distribution histories." This isn't a forward-looking rule that says structure cleanly from here. It's a lookback that forces Chinese principals to document, in granular detail, who put what into which offshore vehicle and who pulled money out — years of flows that many of these structures were explicitly designed to keep opaque. The tax bill is the visible cost. The real event is the disclosure: Beijing now has a mechanism to compel its wealthiest citizens to hand over a complete map of their offshore wealth. For a UHNW family that chose a trust precisely for separation between them and the state, the settlement isn't a tax payment. It's a confession.

Here's what nobody in the "convergence with the West" narrative is pricing. The offshore trust industry across Singapore, Hong Kong, and the BVI was built on a single implicit promise — that a properly settled trust puts assets beyond the settlor's home jurisdiction. China just demonstrated that promise is conditional on Beijing's tolerance, revocable on a deadline. Every Chinese family that pays this bill is confirming the structure was penetrable all along. That's a permanent haircut to the perceived value of the entire offshore stack for this client base, and it won't reverse when the October deadline passes.

The second-order move is the one to watch. Clients aren't just settling — they're rethinking structures "across Asia," which is adviser-speak for redomiciling out of vehicles China can now see into cleanly. Expect a rotation toward tools that sit further from trust reporting: direct holdings, insurance wrappers, operating-company structures, and outright emigration of the tax residence itself. Singapore is the obvious beneficiary of the relocation, but it's also the jurisdiction under the most pressure to prove it won't become the next lookback target, given its own tightening of family-office incentives and beneficial-ownership rules.

For allocators, the read-across is simple. The China wealth pool moving into the region carries a new and correct assumption — that no offshore structure is politically bulletproof. That prices sovereignty risk into everything Chinese capital touches, and it rewards whoever offers genuine distance from Beijing over whoever offers the cleverest paper.

The other side

The straightforward read deserves a fair hearing: China is doing what the US, UK, and most of Europe already do — taxing residents on offshore trust income and distributions regardless of where the vehicle sits. If the rules are applied consistently and the lookback is bounded, this is a one-time compliance event, not a structural rupture. Wealthy families have absorbed similar normalization shocks in other jurisdictions and adapted without abandoning offshore planning altogether. The offshore trust industry is resilient precisely because it has always evolved around new reporting regimes, from FATCA to the Common Reporting Standard, and there's no obvious reason China's version breaks that pattern.

Say more →

The mechanics of what China is demanding matter more than the headline tax rate. When advisers talk about clients needing to produce 'asset schedules, distribution histories, and beneficiary details,' they are describing a forensic reconstruction of structures that, in many cases, span a decade or more of layered contributions and withdrawals. These aren't records that sit in a tidy folder. Offshore trusts used by Chinese UHNW families were frequently structured across multiple jurisdictions — BVI holding companies feeding into Singapore or Hong Kong trusts, with distributions routed through intermediary entities — precisely because opacity was part of the value proposition. Reassembling that paper trail under a government deadline is not a clerical task. It is, in effect, a compelled audit.

The October deadline creates a specific kind of pressure that generic tax compliance doesn't. Clients who are slow to act aren't just risking a penalty — they're risking being caught without documentation that may no longer exist in retrievable form, or that, if retrieved, surfaces flows they would prefer remained unexamined. That asymmetry between the cost of compliance and the cost of non-compliance is what's driving the scramble advisers are describing.

The longer-term structural question is where Chinese capital goes from here. The source material notes clients are exploring options 'across Asia,' which is the polite way of saying they are looking for vehicles and jurisdictions that sit outside the perimeter of what Beijing can now see into. The candidates — insurance wrappers, direct operating-company holdings, and personal tax residency changes — share a common feature: they are harder to characterize as 'trusts' under the new rules, and they move the principal further from any single disclosure trigger. Singapore is the most obvious landing zone for both capital and relocated tax residents, but it is also the jurisdiction most exposed to the next round of pressure, given its own recent tightening of family-office tax incentives and beneficial-ownership transparency requirements.

For context outside this story: the Common Reporting Standard, the OECD's global framework for automatic exchange of financial account information, already covers over 100 jurisdictions and has been steadily closing the gaps that offshore structures once exploited. China's move fits that broader arc, but with a political dimension that the OECD framework lacks — Beijing is not just collecting revenue, it is building a map of where its wealthiest citizens' assets actually sit. That map has uses well beyond taxation.

The practical implication for wealth managers and allocators working with Chinese family offices is that the risk premium on any structure touching Chinese-sourced capital has permanently shifted. Clients who once priced offshore vehicles on their legal robustness now have to price them on their political durability — a much harder thing to model, and one that favors simplicity and genuine jurisdictional distance over sophistication.

Tech & AI Open Source China Inference Licensing

Chinese labs aren't closing the capability gap. They're winning the distribution war.

  • Four frontier-scale open-weight models shipped from five Chinese labs in a single 30-day window between July 27 and August 25, 2026
  • DeepSeek V4-Flash accumulated 4.65 million Hugging Face downloads under MIT license; Qwen's gated 2.4T flagship — the highest-parameter model in the group — has just 38,800
  • Qwen3.8's commercial license triggers at $50 million in licensee revenue, converting open-weight adoption into a royalty-bearing asset once developers scale

The take

Between July 27 and August 25, five Chinese labs shipped four frontier-scale open-weight models — Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4-Flash, Qwen3.8, and GLM-5.3-Flash — on a monthly cadence that used to be quarterly. What makes this window worth writing about isn't the speed. It's that the licensing split has hardened into a deliberate two-tier funnel: DeepSeek and GLM shipping under permissive MIT terms to flood the developer base, while Kimi and Qwen gate their flagships behind revenue thresholds — Qwen's kicks in at $50 million of licensee revenue — to tax scale once it arrives.

The consensus read is "China is closing the gap on capability." Look at the numbers and that's the wrong frame. On DeepSWE 1.1, the contamination-resistant coding benchmark that actually matters for agents, the best of these models sits at 67.5 — still short of the Western frontier on the one axis enterprises pay a premium for. The real story is in the download counts. DeepSeek V4-Flash has 4.65 million downloads under MIT; Qwen's gated 2.4T flagship has 38,800. The market has already voted: developers will take "good enough and unencumbered" over "best and metered" by a factor of a hundred. That is not a capability race. It's a distribution war, and permissive licensing is winning it.

Here's the second-order effect nobody's pricing. The MIT models aren't the giveaway — they're the acquisition channel for something more valuable than tokens: architectural mindshare. Both Qwen3.8 and GLM-5.3 are betting on hybrid linear-attention designs, the first time these have shipped at multi-trillion parameter scale. If that pattern converges into the default way frontier models get built, the reference implementations sitting in every startup's stack are Chinese. You don't need to gate the weights when you've set the architecture the ecosystem trains its people on. That's the Android playbook applied to model internals — give away the layer that commoditizes your rival, monetize the layer you control.

For allocators, the read-across cuts against the compute-landlord thesis everyone's long. The bull case for Nvidia and the hyperscaler capex cycle assumes frontier inference stays scarce and expensive. These releases are engineered to do the opposite — GLM-5.3 activates just 18B of 321B parameters, the most aggressive sparsity in the group, precisely because the game is driving inference cost toward zero. Every point of activated-parameter efficiency is a point of margin extracted from the token-pricing model the Western labs and their chip suppliers are counting on.

The tell is the revenue gate itself. You only build a $50 million tollbooth if you're confident the traffic is coming. The Chinese labs aren't hoping developers adopt at scale — they're pre-writing the invoice. Watch the conversion rate from Flash to Max. If it's low, the gates are friction and the whole frontier just got repriced downward for everyone.

The other side

The capability ceiling still matters, and Chinese models haven't cleared it. On DeepSWE 1.1 — the benchmark most resistant to data contamination and most predictive of real agentic performance — the best of these models scores 67.5, and the source material itself acknowledges US models hold a structural advantage on agentic coding tasks. Enterprise buyers in regulated industries also face real procurement constraints around Chinese-origin models that won't dissolve just because the license is permissive. If the Flash-tier models prove insufficient for production-grade agentic workflows, the entire funnel logic breaks: developers don't migrate to the revenue-gated Max tier, the tollbooth collects nothing, and the distribution lead never converts into revenue.

Say more →

The architectural diversity across these four releases is worth slowing down on, because it signals something about the maturity of the Chinese open-weight ecosystem that raw download counts don't capture. These are not four versions of the same model. Kimi K3 runs a custom attention mechanism called Kimi Delta Attention with Attention Residuals, activating 16 of 896 experts per token — the widest expert pool of any open model in this cohort. Qwen3.8 introduces Gated DeltaNet, a hybrid that alternates between linear-attention layers and full-attention blocks across 92 layers, and represents the first deployment of linear-attention variants at multi-trillion parameter scale. GLM-5.3-Flash — from Zhipu AI, a Beijing-based lab — combines sparse and linear attention with something called Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections, activating just 18 billion of 321 billion total parameters. That sparsity ratio is the most aggressive in the group and is not an accident of design: it is a direct attack on inference cost.

The inference economics point matters for anyone long the Western compute stack. The bull case for Nvidia and the hyperscalers rests on frontier inference remaining expensive enough to justify the capex cycle. These architectures are engineered to undercut that assumption. Every design choice that reduces activated parameters at inference time is a choice that drives token costs lower, which compresses the margin the Western labs and their chip suppliers are pricing into their forward revenue. GLM-5.3-Flash's 18B active parameter count on a 321B total model is the clearest expression of this logic, but the pattern runs across all four releases.

The Android parallel in the take is worth extending. When Google open-sourced Android, the strategic goal was not to make money on the operating system — it was to prevent Microsoft and others from owning the mobile platform layer, and to ensure Google's services ran on every device. The MIT-licensed Flash models are playing the same game one level deeper: not the platform, but the architecture. If hybrid linear-attention designs become the default way frontier models get built — and both Qwen3.8 and GLM-5.3-Flash are betting they will — then the reference implementations embedded in every startup's stack are Chinese-origin. The weights are open, but the architectural intuitions, the training recipes, and the engineering talent that understands these systems at depth all accrue to the labs that shipped first at scale.

The benchmark picture adds one more layer. On Terminal Bench 2.1 and GPQA Diamond — the headline reasoning benchmarks — the gap between the top Chinese models and the Western frontier has narrowed to the point where it is no longer a clean selling point for closed-model vendors. Kimi K3 posts 88.3 on Terminal Bench and 93.5 on GPQA Diamond. Those are not numbers that let a Western lab charge a 10x inference premium on reasoning tasks alone. The premium, if it survives, survives on agentic reliability over long horizons — exactly the dimension where the source material concedes US models still lead. That is a narrowing moat, not a permanent one.

Financial Markets & Economic News Fed Policy Rates Inflation

Warsh boxed himself in at Jackson Hole. The market is only pricing one way out.

  • September rate hike odds jumped from near-zero to 66.1% on CME FedWatch after Warsh's Jackson Hole speech Friday
  • Three straight weak nonfarm payrolls prints and a Dallas Fed trimmed-mean inflation reading of 2.3% — near the Fed's 2% target — argue against tightening
  • At the July FOMC meeting, three of twelve voters supported a hike; Citigroup's Andrew Hollenhorst says there was no consensus then and data since has only gotten softer

The take

Kevin Warsh gave the Jackson Hole keynote Friday as Fed chair, said inflation progress "does not tell me underlying trends have meaningfully improved," and the market did something it almost never does at this point in a cycle: it flipped to pricing a hike. September odds went from near-zero to 66.1% on CME FedWatch over a weekend. The tell isn't the words — Citi's Andrew Hollenhorst is right that Warsh has said versions of this every time he's opened his mouth. The tell is that the market finally believed him.

Here's what's actually going on. Warsh spent his first months in the chair with a credibility gap — back in July he pledged the Fed "will not waver" on 2%, and the market bid up yields and priced out hikes anyway, essentially calling him a talker. Friday reversed that. Bank of America's read is the honest one: Warsh has now boxed himself in. "The onus is now on Warsh to deliver a hike, otherwise he risks undermining the credibility he gained on Friday." That's the trap. A chair who craves credibility will hike into weak data to prove he's not bluffing, which means the reaction function has quietly shifted from data-dependent to credibility-dependent. That's a regime change nobody is pricing.

And the data does not support a hike. Three straight soft nonfarm payrolls prints. The Dallas Fed trimmed-mean measure — which strips the extreme movers — sits at 2.3%, a hair off target. Even the Treasury Secretary is on TV telling him not to do it: Bessent's "you don't raise into a supply shock unless you see second- or third-order effects" is not a random comment, it's the administration drawing a line in advance so it can assign blame if growth rolls over. When the Treasury and the Fed chair are publicly disagreeing on direction two weeks before a meeting, you are watching an institutional fight, not a data debate.

The allocator read: this is a coin-flip meeting where the two outcomes are genuinely far apart, and the market is only priced for one of them at 66%. If Friday's jobs number is soft — and three priors say it will be — a chair who hikes anyway is telling you he'll prioritize his own credibility over the labor market, which repriced the front end violently. A chair who holds after all that hawkish theater hands the market a "cried wolf" narrative and yields fall. Either way, realized vol in rates over the next three weeks is underpriced.

The cleaner trade isn't guessing the decision — it's owning optionality around it. Consensus has quietly conceded the Fed reaction function to one man's need to be believed. That's a fragile equilibrium, and fragile equilibria are where the money is.

The other side

Bank of America's case deserves a fair hearing: Warsh's Jackson Hole framing — that the Fed should focus on trends rather than isolated data points, and that underlying inflation hasn't meaningfully improved — is a deliberate raising of the bar, and a chair who sets a bar and then clears it is exactly how central bank credibility gets rebuilt after years of being doubted. Headline PCE is still running at 3.7%, which is nearly double the target, and if the trimmed-mean measure is the only number that looks clean, that's a selection argument, not a victory lap. A hike into soft-but-not-collapsing data might be precisely the kind of uncomfortable move that makes future forward guidance actually land.

Say more →

The institutional dynamic here is what makes this meeting unusual, and it is worth slowing down on. Warsh came into the chair with a credibility deficit that was visible in real time: he pledged in July that the Fed would not waver on 2% inflation, and the market's response was to bid up Treasury yields while pricing out hikes — the bond market equivalent of a slow clap. Friday's speech did not contain materially new language by Citigroup economist Andrew Hollenhorst's own accounting, which makes the repricing stranger and more significant. Markets did not respond to new information; they responded to a shift in their read of Warsh's resolve. That is a different thing entirely, and it means the credibility trade is now the dominant variable in the room.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's Monday comments from the G20 summit in Asheville are not background noise. Bessent told CNBC directly that you do not raise rates into a supply shock unless you see second- or third-order inflationary effects, and that core inflation has remained, in his words, very, very restrained. A Treasury secretary does not go on television two weeks before an FOMC meeting to editorialize about monetary policy without knowing exactly what he is doing. That is the administration pre-positioning the blame narrative: if Warsh hikes and growth softens, the White House already has its quote in the archive. The Fed's formal independence does not prevent this kind of public pressure, and the fact that it is happening openly tells you the internal disagreement is real.

The data calendar between now and the September 15-16 meeting is dense in exactly the ways that matter. Jobs numbers arrive this week, against a backdrop of three consecutive weak nonfarm payrolls prints. Consumer and producer price indexes land the following week, feeding into the PCE gauge that the Fed formally targets. Retail sales print the day of the decision itself. That sequencing means Warsh will have a full picture before he votes — and if the jobs number disappoints again, he will be choosing between his credibility and the labor market in a way that is unusually legible to everyone watching.

JPMorgan Asset Management's chief global strategist David Kelly flagged this directly, writing that the economy does not have quite as much momentum as Warsh's Jackson Hole speech implied, and that markets may have been premature in assigning roughly 60% odds to a September hike. That is a careful way of saying the repricing overshot. But overshoot or not, the options market is the place to watch. A meeting where the two realistic outcomes — hike into soft data, or hold after hawkish theater — produce violently different front-end moves is a meeting where implied volatility in rates should be higher than it is. The consensus has effectively handed the Fed reaction function to one man's institutional need to be taken seriously, and pricing a coin-flip binary at 66-34 as if it were a calm, data-driven call is exactly the kind of complacency that gets punished.
