Secondary volume forecasts hit $1 trillion. Growing the market and solving the liquidity crisis are not the same thing.

businesstimes.com.sg
  • Secondary transaction volume hit a record $226 billion in 2025, with forecasts ranging from $350 billion to $1 trillion annually by 2030 — but volume measures assets changing hands, not capital returned to original investors.
  • A $4 trillion backlog of unrealized asset value is the fuel for secondaries growth; it is also the measure of how badly the primary exit machine — IPOs, sponsor-to-sponsor sales, continuation vehicles — has seized.
  • Private bank allocators are now 42% overweight alternatives, up from 24% last year per PWM's 2026 Global Asset Tracker, arriving as the institutional investors with the longest track records in these assets are the structural sellers.

The take

Tenth time this section has circled secondaries and continuation vehicles since mid-October, and the latest round of industry forecasting is where the frame finally cracks. The sell-side keeps repeating that $226 billion of 2025 secondary volume is on its way to $500 billion or even $1 trillion by 2030, off the back of a $4 trillion backlog of unrealized asset value. Coller "absolutely believes" $500 billion. Robard at Dawson has said $1 trillion for years. Even Ardian's more sober Wilfred Small is at $350 billion.

Notice what all three numbers describe. They are forecasts of transaction volume — how many assets change hands — not forecasts of distributions back to the people who actually committed the capital. Those are different things, and the gap between them is the whole story. A secondary sale doesn't create liquidity for the asset class; it relocates it. When a pension sells its stake at a discount to Ardian, the pension gets cash and Ardian gets the exposure. Net liquidity delivered to LPs as a group is zero. You've just moved the holding problem to a buyer who believes they bought it cheap.

So when Jefferies' Beckelman says "all the ingredients are in place for continued long-term expansion," he is describing the growth of a liquidity-reselling business, and the fuel for that business is other people's inability to get their money back. A $4 trillion unrealized backlog isn't a bullish tailwind — it's the measure of how badly the primary exit machine has seized. IPOs are shut to sub-$1 billion sponsor companies, sponsor-to-sponsor deals face mark scrutiny, and continuation volume itself fell 50% year on year. Secondaries scale precisely to the extent those channels stay broken.

Here's the part the forecasts quietly concede: the market is "undercapitalised relative to the potential demand for liquidity." Translate that. Demand for liquidity means sellers. An undercapitalised market of sellers means the clearing price falls — bigger discounts to NAV, which is the same stale-mark problem this section has tracked for three weeks. The bull case for secondaries volume and the bear case for private equity marks are the identical fact stated with opposite emotions.

For anyone deciding where to put money inside this: the attractive seat is Ardian's and CVC's, buying forced liquidity at a discount, not the LP sleeve that keeps re-upping into primaries and then has to sell its 2021 vintage to those same buyers at eighty cents. The PWM survey showing private banks jumping to 42% overweight alternatives, up from 24%, is wealthy retail arriving exactly as the institutions who know these assets best are the structural sellers. The $500 billion forecast will probably be right. It just won't mean what the people cheering it think it means — it'll mean the distribution drought lasted long enough to build a trillion-dollar industry out of monetizing it.

The other side

The secondaries market does more than relocate distress — it sets a price, and that price disciplines the whole system. When Ardian or CVC buys at eighty cents, the mark compression that follows forces GPs to either improve operations or accept reality on exit timing, which is exactly the correction primary markets need. Rising secondary volume also draws in new pools of capital, including sovereign wealth and private bank money, that genuinely expand the buyer base rather than just recycling existing LP commitments. If that new capital is net additive, aggregate liquidity delivered to the asset class does increase, even if any single transaction is a zero-sum transfer.

The forecasting exercise itself is worth examining. Coller Capital's Jeremy Coller first made the $500 billion call in 2018, and he is standing by it now that 2025 volume has reached $226 billion. Dawson Partners' Yann Robard has been at $1 trillion for years. Ardian's Wilfred Small, described as the conservative voice in the room, lands at $350 billion. The range is wide, but the direction is unanimous, and that unanimity should prompt a question: every firm making these predictions is also raising capital to deploy into secondaries. Coller, Ardian, CVC — these are not disinterested forecasters. The $500 billion prediction is also a fundraising document.

The structural argument underneath the forecasts is real, even if the framing is self-serving. Holding periods have extended materially. Distributions as a share of NAV are running below 20% against a historical average of around 27%, per data cited in recent European coverage. The IPO window remains effectively closed to the sub-$1 billion enterprise value companies that make up the vast majority of sponsor-owned assets — Hamilton Lane's Erik Hirsch made that point explicitly. Continuation vehicle volume fell roughly 50% year on year. Every channel that used to return capital to LPs is either shut or under pressure, and secondaries expand to fill that vacuum.

The mechanism matters here. A secondary transaction transfers the illiquidity problem from a seller who needs cash to a buyer who believes they bought the discount wide enough to profit. The $4 trillion unrealized backlog does not shrink because secondaries volume grows — it shrinks only when underlying companies are sold, taken public, or recapitalized at real prices. Until that happens, secondaries are a pressure valve, not a solution. The market's own language gives this away: the Business Times source describes the secondary market as 'undercapitalised relative to the potential demand for liquidity.' Potential demand for liquidity means there are more motivated sellers than there is buying capacity, which means clearing prices fall. Bigger discounts to NAV are the direct consequence, and those discounts are the same stale-mark problem that has run through this section since mid-October.

The private bank data from PWM's 2026 Global Asset Tracker adds a timing dimension that the volume forecasts ignore. Wealthy retail allocators moving from 24% to 42% overweight alternatives are, in many cases, buying into primary fund structures — the same vintage exposure that institutional LPs with longer track records are now selling into the secondary market at a discount. The information asymmetry runs in one direction. Institutions who have held these assets through the full cycle, watched the marks, and sat through GP extension requests are the sellers. The buyers arriving late, attracted by the diversification and longevity arguments Jean Chia at Bank of Singapore articulates, are absorbing that supply.

None of this makes secondaries a bad business. The attractive position, as the take notes, is on the buy side — Ardian, CVC, and their peers acquiring forced liquidity at a discount and earning the spread between entry price and eventual exit. The unattractive position is the LP who keeps committing to primaries, watches the holding period extend, and then sells the position to those same buyers at eighty cents to fund the next commitment. The $500 billion forecast will very likely prove correct. The question is who captures the economics when it does.

Private Markets TPG GP-Led Continuation Vehicles Credit

TPG booked $6.6 billion in exits and kept $7 million in carry. Its secondaries business is the reason that's sustainable.

secondaryscoop.com, secondarylink.com
  • TGS II, TPG's GP-led secondaries fund, grew from $1.7 billion to $2.2 billion in commitments in a single quarter and is still raising — 19% larger than its 2022 predecessor already.
  • TPG's asset-based finance team completed what the firm called a first-of-its-kind credit secondaries deal, buying liquidity from another manager's end-of-life residential mortgage fund — extending the GP Solutions playbook from equity into credit.
  • Net accrued performance allocations hit a record $1.5 billion while realized performance allocations on $6.6 billion of exits came to just $7 million, because proceeds went to returning cost basis and covering software markdowns in TPG Capital.

The take

This section has tracked the secondaries boom hard for three weeks — the record flagship closes, the LP roll-rate collapse, the ILPA rulebook that blessed the practice it meant to police. TPG's Q3 print adds the piece those stories kept circling: what it looks like when one house sits on both sides of the trade at once, and reports it as a feature.

Start with the number that explains the whole posture. TPG booked $6.6 billion of realizations in the quarter and generated $7 million of realized performance allocations — about eleven basis points of carry on proceeds, because the cash went to returning cost basis and plugging marked-down software losses in TPG Capital. That is a sponsor whose own funds are not producing the DPI it keeps telling LPs it prioritizes. Net accrued performance hit a record $1.5 billion, which is to say the carry exists on paper and refuses to convert. This is the exact pattern the earlier NAV-versus-exit-price study described, now showing up inside a single public filing.

So watch where the growth actually is. TGS II has climbed to $2.2 billion of commitments from $1.7 billion a quarter ago, 19% bigger than its 2022 predecessor and still raising. Winkelried says the GP Solutions pipeline is up roughly 35% over two years. The firm that cannot crystallize carry from its own aging assets is building a fast-growing business buying the same liquidity problem from everyone else. That is a rational hedge — if your exit engine stalls, own the toll booth on the detour.

The governance question the Secondary Scoop piece raises is the one worth pressing, and it is the CV-squared problem this section flagged on October 14 wearing a corporate uniform. MMDL Continuation I is a $1.2 billion vehicle where TPG is the sponsoring GP. GP Solutions is a buyer of GP-led deals. Nothing structurally stops a TPG-sponsored continuation vehicle from being bought, in whole or part, by a TPG secondaries fund — the firm pricing the asset, selling the asset, and buying the asset, collecting fees at each station. TPG has not said it discloses when this happens. Until it does, LPs in TGS II are underwriting a conflict policy they cannot see.

Two further details sharpen the read. TGS I shows a 36% net IRR on a 1.3x multiple with only $222 million of $2.2 billion realized — a mark-driven number on a book that buys late-hold assets, exactly the kind of figure the October 26 study warned converts poorly. And the "first-of-its-kind" credit secondary — liquidity for another manager's end-of-life residential mortgage fund — tells you the model is spreading from equity into credit, where late-life collateral is even harder to mark honestly.

The clean story is that TPG is a diversified $346 billion manager monetizing the liquidity squeeze from both ends. The truer one is that the same squeeze starving its carry is the raw material feeding its fastest-growing fund, and the firm has every incentive to keep the squeeze going.

The other side

The bull case on TPG's dual positioning is that it is exactly what a mature multi-strategy manager should look like in a dislocated market: using sector expertise built in primary investing to underwrite secondary assets that generalist buyers cannot price. GP Solutions buying late-hold PE assets and ABF buying end-of-life mortgage collateral are both extensions of capabilities TPG already owns, not conflicts of interest dressed up as diversification. And the 35% pipeline growth Winkelried cited reflects real market demand — GP-led secondaries are on track for another record year industry-wide, so TPG is growing with a rising tide, not manufacturing volume.

The structural oddity in TPG's Q3 print is that the firm's two biggest growth stories are causally linked in a way the earnings call never quite says out loud. GP Solutions exists because sponsors cannot exit assets cleanly. TPG Capital cannot exit assets cleanly. The pipeline feeding TGS II is, at least in part, the same liquidity problem TPG's own funds are generating for their LPs.

The October 26 study this section covered documented systematic gaps between private equity NAV marks and actual exit prices. TGS I's 36% net IRR on a 1.3x net multiple, with only $222 million of $2.2 billion in total value actually realized, is a live illustration of that dynamic. The IRR is largely mark-to-model at this stage — the fund buys assets late in their hold period, so the clock runs fast and the multiple stays compressed. Whether that 36% survives to cash is the question TGS II investors are implicitly betting on, and the answer depends heavily on exit conditions that TPG's own Capital platform is currently struggling to navigate.

The credit secondaries move deserves more attention than it got on the call. TPG's asset-based finance team deployed over $2 billion in Q3, and one piece of that was buying a liquidity solution for another manager's residential mortgage fund nearing the end of its life. TPG described it as first-of-its-kind, though that framing could not be independently verified. What is verifiable is the logic: ABF already underwrites residential mortgage collateral in its primary business, so pricing a secondary on the same asset type is a natural extension. The same pattern GP Solutions uses in equity — sector expertise applied to assets that need a new owner before the fund clock runs out — now has a credit analog. If that template scales, TPG's secondaries footprint expands well beyond what the Market Solutions segment label suggests.

The conflict disclosure question the source material raises is the sharpest governance issue in the print. MMDL Continuation I is a $1.2 billion continuation vehicle where TPG is the sponsoring GP — meaning TPG selected the assets, structured the vehicle, and is collecting management fees. GP Solutions is a buyer of GP-led continuation vehicles. The firm has not disclosed whether it has policies governing situations where a TPG-sponsored continuation vehicle could be acquired, in whole or in part, by a TPG secondaries fund. That would put TPG on the sell side setting the price, the buy side accepting it, and the fee meter running at both ends. TPG has not said this has happened. It also has not said it hasn't, or that it would disclose it if it did. LPs in TGS II are effectively underwriting a conflict management framework they have not been shown.

The CFO's comment in Q&A is worth noting for what it reveals about internal prioritization. Asked about fourth-quarter outlook, Axel André listed flagship fund closes and then added TGS II in the same breath as the Sports strategy — framing it as one of the newer businesses becoming a meaningful contributor. That is a significant shift from Q2, when GP Solutions appeared in the fund tables without any explicit connection to the GP-led secondaries market in TPG's own prepared remarks. The Q3 call made that connection explicit for the first time. The firm is now publicly tying its fastest-growing fund to the same liquidity crisis that is suppressing carry in its legacy strategies.

Anthropic's Q3 profit flip exposes the cost structure its IPO valuation depends on ignoring.

at-pulse.com, 4artificialintelligence.com, techfundingnews.com
  • Q3 operating income swung from a $974M profit to a $536M loss in a single quarter, driven by a $5B emergency compute charge for hardware bought at a premium when planned capacity ran short.
  • Quarter-on-quarter revenue growth decelerated from 144% in Q2 to 47% in Q3, even as the absolute number hit $17 billion — more than eleven times the year-ago figure.
  • Institutional investors are now pushing Anthropic's IPO target down to $1.5–1.6 trillion from the $2 trillion figure circulating weeks ago, citing market volatility, data-center political risk, and uncertainty about when hyperscalers show AI-related profits.

The take

Third time on the Anthropic IPO in two weeks. Since the October 30 S-1 take, the amended filing now carries Q3 numbers, and they reframe the whole offering. Revenue hit $17 billion for the quarter — more than eleven times a year ago — but quarter-on-quarter growth collapsed from 144% in Q2 to 47% in Q3. And the small operating profit we flagged in June flipped: a $974 million operating profit in Q2 became a $536 million operating loss in Q3.

The thing to watch is why it flipped. This isn't demand softening — $17 billion on 47% sequential growth is still a monster. It's that Anthropic got caught short on compute and had to buy hardware at a premium, taking a $5 billion charge because it couldn't book capacity 12–18 months out the way it normally does. In other words, the company's own cost base is now hostage to the same GPU scarcity that inflates its revenue. That is the single most important disclosure in the filing, and it is being buried under the headline revenue number.

Here's the mechanism that matters. The $895.4 billion in infrastructure commitments — up from the $417 billion we wrote about on October 30 — assumes Anthropic can lock capacity ahead of need and spread the cost. The $5 billion Q3 charge is proof that when demand runs hotter than forecast, it can't, and it eats a premium that lands straight on the operating line. So the faster Anthropic grows, the worse its unit economics can get in any quarter where supply is tight. That is the inverse of the operating leverage public investors are being asked to pay for.

The market is already repricing this. Institutions are now pushing Anthropic toward a $1.5–1.6 trillion IPO, down from the $2 trillion floated weeks ago — citing data-center political backlash and doubt about when Amazon and Meta show AI profit. Pair that with OpenAI cutting its private round from $30 billion to $20 billion at $1.25 trillion, with a first close landing below even that. Both of the frontier labs are getting marked down in the same window, by overlapping investors — Coatue, MGX, Founders Fund sit on both cap tables. When the same handful of funds price both sides of a duopoly, "competitive tension" is a story they tell each other, not a market.

For the portfolio specifically: every ICONIQ name riding Anthropic's rails — Legora at $8.8 billion, the coding and legal-AI tier — is underwritten on Claude's cost curve staying flat as it scales. The S-1 just showed the cost curve is reflexive, not flat. A lab that takes a $5 billion surprise charge in a single good quarter will pass that volatility through in token pricing the moment its own margins demand it. Replit already walked. The downstream repricing risk sits with the apps that can't.

The other side

The $5 billion compute charge is a one-time catch-up cost, not a structural feature of the business. Anthropic's own filing distinguishes between spot hardware purchases — which carry the premium — and its standard 12-to-18-month forward commitments, which do not. A company growing revenue eleven times year-over-year will inevitably hit capacity ceilings during acceleration phases; the right read is that demand outran supply, not that the cost model is broken. Adjusted operating income, which strips out the charge along with stock compensation and charitable contributions, actually rose $300 million sequentially to $2.5 billion — which is the number that reflects the underlying unit economics once the emergency spending normalizes.

The amended S-1 landed a week after the original filing, which only covered results through June 30. The update was necessary to include Q3 numbers before the offering could proceed, and those numbers immediately complicated the roadshow narrative. The original filing showed a business accelerating into profitability — $16.3 billion in H1 revenue, a Q2 operating profit of $974 million. The amended version shows a business that can swing $1.5 billion on the operating line in a single quarter depending on whether its compute procurement goes according to plan.

The mechanism is worth spelling out precisely. Anthropic's normal practice is to book computing capacity from cloud providers 12 to 18 months in advance, locking in rates before the hardware is needed. When Q3 demand came in hotter than those forward bookings anticipated, the company had to go to the spot market — buying GPU time at whatever price was available, which is structurally more expensive. The $5 billion charge is the cost of that gap. It is not a write-down or an accounting adjustment; it is cash out the door for compute that was consumed in the quarter.

What makes this disclosure structurally important rather than just a bad quarter is the relationship between growth and procurement risk. The faster Anthropic grows, the harder it is to forecast capacity needs 12 to 18 months out with precision. A company growing 47% in a single quarter — even after decelerating from 144% — is operating in a demand environment that is genuinely difficult to model that far forward. Every quarter where actual demand exceeds the forward book is a quarter where the spot premium lands on the operating line. Public investors being asked to pay a multiple on future operating leverage are implicitly betting that Anthropic's forecasting gets better as the business gets bigger. The Q3 filing is evidence it hasn't yet.

The infrastructure commitment figure sharpens this. The $895.4 billion in total compute and infrastructure commitments — $69.8 billion due within the next twelve months alone — is the planned-capacity side of the equation. The $5 billion Q3 charge is what happens when actual demand outruns even that level of forward commitment. Both numbers are in the same filing, and they point in opposite directions: the commitments suggest aggressive capacity planning, while the charge suggests the planning still fell short.

The investor pressure on valuation is a direct response to this dynamic. Institutions pushing for a $1.5 to $1.6 trillion IPO price, against the $2 trillion figure that was circulating in recent months, are effectively asking for a discount that reflects the earnings volatility the amended filing just demonstrated. The same investors — Coatue, MGX, Founders Fund — appear on both Anthropic's and OpenAI's cap tables, which means the markdown pressure on one filing informs the pricing conversation on the other. OpenAI's private round has already been cut from $30 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation to $20 billion at $1.25 trillion, with the first close expected to land below even the reduced target.

For companies built on top of Claude — the legal-AI and coding-AI tier that includes names like Legora, which closed a $550 million round at an $8.8 billion valuation in October — the S-1 disclosure changes the risk calculus in a specific way. Their unit economics assume that Claude's token pricing stays roughly stable as Anthropic scales. The Q3 charge shows that in quarters where Anthropic's own margins compress, the pressure to pass costs downstream through token pricing is real. Replit, which was a top-five Anthropic customer by spend before switching its default model to OpenAI, made that repricing calculation explicitly. The apps that cannot switch as cleanly are the ones absorbing the residual risk.

Canva's $40 billion valuation is now $30.4 billion and the IPO math has to start over.

forbes.com.au, startupdaily.net
  • T. Rowe Price marked its Canva stake down 22% to an implied $30.4 billion, from $40 billion at the start of 2026 — the latest in a cascade that also includes cuts by Blackbird, Airtree, and Canva's own internal mark
  • 1,271 roles — 22% of the workforce — are being cut or relocated in what CEO Melanie Perkins called the company's first major restructure in thirteen years
  • Canva's in-house model Brush costs 46% less per edit and runs up to 69% faster than the tool it replaces, launched four days before the company simultaneously expanded its OpenAI partnership to include GPT-6 Astra

The take

Canva just cut 1,271 roles — 22% of its workforce — in its first real restructure, and T. Rowe Price marked its stake down to an implied $30.4 billion, from the $40 billion the company carried entering 2026. Melanie Perkins is calling it the "hardest year yet" and pitching a "refounding" back to small teams. That's the standard narrative wrapper. The number that actually explains this is from four days earlier: Brush, Canva's second in-house model, costs 46% less per edit than the tool it replaces.

Here's the mechanism everyone is glossing over. Canva's problem was never demand — it's that it bolted frontier AI onto a product whose users mostly don't pay. When Perkins cut the revenue growth forecast from 30% to 20% in August, the stated reason was that serving OpenAI and Anthropic models to a free base was too expensive to scale features against. So the entire 2026 story is a gross-margin compression story disguised as a growth story. You can't serve GPT-6 Astra to a free tier and keep software multiples. The layoffs and the in-house models — Craft for generation, Brush for editing, both adapted from open weights — are the same project: pull inference cost out of the P&L before the IPO window reopens.

Which is why the OpenAI deal, announced the same week as the cuts, is the genuinely interesting tension. Canva ships two of its own models to cut costs, then immediately expands its dependence on OpenAI's most expensive frontier models. Those two moves only reconcile one way: Canva keeps OpenAI for the hard reasoning tasks where quality sells seats, and shifts the high-volume, low-value edits — the stuff the free tier burns — onto Brush at 46% less. This is the same tiering Replit and half the developer market have already adopted. Canva is late to it, and the markdowns are the price of being late.

For the IPO math, the markdown cascade matters more than the layoffs. Blackbird cut to $34.9 billion in August, Airtree matched it, Canva's own internal mark fell to roughly $31 billion, and now T. Rowe is at $30.4 billion. That's a coordinated repricing from the people closest to the books, converging on a number a full quarter below the 2025 employee-sale mark of $42 billion. A 25% haircut across every holder in three months is not a soft patch — it's the market telling Canva its pre-AI multiple was underwriting margins it can no longer defend.

The structural read: Canva is proving, in public, that a mostly-free consumer software model and frontier AI are chemically incompatible at scale. The fix — build cheap in-house models, ration the expensive ones, cut 22% of staff to fund the transition — is rational and probably works. But it resets the terminal margin. Whoever buys the Nasdaq listing is buying a 20%-growth, cost-disciplined software company, not the 30%-growth design monopoly the 2025 round implied. The $30 billion mark isn't the bottom of a dip. It's the new baseline.

The other side

The restructure is painful but it is also exactly what a disciplined pre-IPO company should do when its cost structure gets ahead of its revenue model. Cutting 22% of headcount, consolidating product teams, and shipping in-house models that run at 46% lower cost per edit are the moves that rebuild gross margin — and gross margin is what public market investors will actually underwrite. If Canva exits this year with a leaner cost base, a credible inference strategy, and a stabilized growth rate around 20%, the $30 billion mark may look like a trough rather than a ceiling. Plenty of software companies have repriced through a transition and re-rated on the other side.

The valuation cascade is worth mapping precisely because the sources are not random. Blackbird, Canva's largest shareholder, cut its internal mark by 17% to $34.9 billion in August. Airtree made the same 17% cut from the $42 billion set in a 2025 employee share sale. Canva's own internal valuation fell to roughly $31 billion. Now T. When insiders and crossover institutions move in the same direction at the same magnitude over a single quarter, that is a coordinated signal about the underlying business, not a market mood.

The underlying business problem is structural. Canva reorganized its platform around AI — internally called Canva 2.0 — and discovered that serving frontier models from OpenAI and Anthropic to a user base that is mostly non-paying is economically incoherent. The cost of inference scales with usage; the revenue does not, because the free tier does not convert fast enough to cover it. That is what forced Perkins to cut the revenue growth forecast from 30% to 20% in August, and it is what is driving both the layoffs and the in-house model push simultaneously.

Craft, Canva's first proprietary model, handles image generation. Brush, the second, handles design editing — changing headlines in a presentation, for instance, without regenerating the entire slide. Both were adapted from existing open-weights models and trained further on design-specific tasks, which is a materially cheaper path than training from scratch. Brush's 46% cost reduction per edit and 69% speed improvement are the numbers that matter for the P&L, because editing is the high-frequency, low-value action that the free tier burns through constantly. Shifting that volume off OpenAI and Anthropic infrastructure and onto a cheaper proprietary model is how Canva starts to rebuild the margin it gave away during the Canva 2.0 rollout.

The simultaneous OpenAI expansion — broader access to GPT-6 Astra and GPT-5.6, plus expanded internal use of ChatGPT and Codex — is not a contradiction of that strategy. It is the other half of it. Canva is tiering its inference stack: proprietary models for the repetitive, high-volume tasks where cost is the constraint, and frontier models for the complex reasoning and generation tasks where quality is the differentiator and where paying customers are more likely to be sitting. Canva Code, which lets users build interactive apps and websites by describing what they want, is the kind of feature that justifies a Pro subscription and can absorb frontier model pricing. A free-tier user resizing a logo cannot.

This tiering approach is now standard across developer-facing AI products — Replit made a version of the same move recently — but Canva is arriving at it later and under more financial pressure, which is why the restructure is as large as it is. The $10,000 departure grants for staff who want to start their own ventures, and the six months of salary plus twelve months of healthcare for departing employees, suggest Canva is trying to manage the cultural cost of the cuts carefully. That is worth noting, but it does not change the math. The company that lists on the Nasdaq — whenever that window reopens — will be priced as a 20%-growth, cost-disciplined software business. The $30 billion mark is where that business trades today.

Founders are holding assets in their own name on purpose. The structure is the succession plan.

privatebankerinternational.com, wealthbriefing.com, fintechnews.sg
  • 54% of family offices hold key assets in the founder's personal name, 52% use personal investment companies, and only 20% use a professionally administered trust — per DBS's survey of 200-plus offices across Asia, Europe and the Middle East
  • 33% of founders cite their own reluctance to cede control as the primary reason for delaying wealth transfer, and 15% expect a transfer only when forced by incapacity or crisis
  • More than 85% have no formal business continuity plan, 38% have not addressed cybersecurity risk, and only 3% have or plan to obtain cyber insurance

The take

The eighth family-office succession survey we've covered since late September, and the numbers have stopped telling us anything new about the gap and started telling us something about the industry selling the research. DBS's Almanac of 200-plus offices lands on the same beat as Bank of America, Citi, UBS and RBC: most families want wealth to last generations, few have the structures to make that happen, and the heirs aren't in the room. The one figure worth isolating is the ownership stack. Fifty-four percent still hold key assets in their own name, 52% use personal investment companies, and only 20% use a professionally administered trust.

That mix is the actual story, and it's not a planning oversight. It's a revealed preference. A founder who holds assets in his own name and refuses to cede control — the 33% who cite exactly that as the reason for delay — is behaving rationally within his own time horizon. He originated the wealth, he trusts his own judgment, and a personal holding keeps the decision rights undivided. The trust, the family council, the continuity plan: every one of these transfers authority away from him while he's still alive. The survey frames this as a capability gap. It's closer to a control feature the founder installed on purpose.

Here's where it compounds, and where the bank-sponsored framing goes quiet. Lay the DBS structure data over what we've seen in the Bank of America and Citi work this month. Offices are pushing further into private equity, direct deals and private-company stakes — 59% planning to add — while 47% of those who tried to exit a private position this year couldn't. So you have illiquid assets, rising fast, sitting inside personal-name holdings and PICs designed for a single home jurisdiction, owned by a founder who won't restructure, held for heirs who are 55% unsure which structures even apply across the borders they now live in. Every axis is moving toward harder-to-transfer, and the legal wrapper is moving nowhere.

The cost shows up as tax, and the source material already prices part of it: 23% cite cross-border taxation as a barrier to transfer, 21% cite multi-jurisdiction tax exposure for family members. For context, UBS puts the global handover at roughly $83 trillion over two to three decades — a number every bank in this space now quotes because the advisory fees on restructuring that wealth are the actual product. These reports are lead generation. The gap they document is the sales funnel.

What a Singapore allocator should take from this is narrower than the headline. The binding constraint on this wealth transfer isn't heir readiness or AI adoption — it's that the asset side is getting more illiquid and more cross-border while the ownership side stays frozen in a founder's personal name. When a founder in his 70s finally dies with a PIC full of un-exitable private stakes spanning four tax regimes, the estate doesn't get a succession plan. It gets a liquidation at the worst possible price, into the same clogged secondary market everyone already knows about.

The other side

The founder-control critique assumes the alternative structures actually work better, and that's not obvious. Professionally administered trusts introduce trustee discretion, legal costs, and jurisdictional complexity that can destroy value in a crisis faster than a personal holding ever would. A founder who has compounded wealth across decades has earned some skepticism toward advisors telling him to hand authority to a committee — and the 20% who do use trusts aren't demonstrably better off yet. The survey documents a gap in formal planning, but formal planning and good outcomes are not the same thing.

The DBS Almanac is the eighth succession-focused report this brief has covered since late September, and the pattern across all of them is consistent enough to be structural rather than coincidental. Bank of America, Citi, UBS, RBC, and now DBS are all publishing research that documents the same gap — heirs unprepared, structures outdated, founders unwilling to move — and all of them are doing so because the advisory and restructuring fees on a multi-decade, multi-trillion-dollar wealth transfer are the actual product. UBS puts the global handover at roughly $83 trillion over two to three decades; that figure appears in the DBS source material as outside context, and every bank in this space now quotes it for the same reason. The gap is the sales funnel.

What makes the DBS data worth reading past the headline is the ownership stack, which is more specific than most of what the competing reports publish. Fifty-four percent of respondents hold key assets in their own name. Fifty-two percent use personal investment companies — essentially a single-shareholder corporate wrapper designed for the founder's home jurisdiction. Only 20% use a professionally administered trust. These are not the numbers of an industry that is planning to restructure. They are the numbers of an industry that has already made its structural choice and is not revisiting it.

Lay that ownership picture against the investment data from the Bank of America and Citi work published earlier this month. Fifty-nine percent of family offices plan to increase private equity and private-company exposure. Forty-seven percent of those who tried to exit a private-markets position this year could not complete the exit. So the asset side is becoming more illiquid and more complex at exactly the moment the ownership side is staying frozen in personal-name and personal-investment-company structures that were designed for a single jurisdiction and a single decision-maker.

The cross-border dimension sharpens this further. The DBS report notes that 55% of respondents are unsure which ownership structures to adopt as heirs move across borders, and 23% already cite cross-border taxation as a concrete barrier to transfer — not a theoretical one. Twenty-one percent specifically flag multi-jurisdiction tax exposure for family members. These are costs that are already accruing, not risks that might materialize. A personal-name holding or a personal investment company that made sense when the founder was building a business in one country becomes a structurally expensive instrument the moment heirs are resident in three different tax regimes.

The business continuity data reinforces the same point from a different angle. More than 85% of offices either have no formal continuity plan, rely on informal arrangements, or are unsure whether a plan exists. Only 7% regularly test or review a formal plan. Thirty-eight percent have not addressed cybersecurity risk. Three percent have cyber insurance. These are not capability gaps that will be closed by a bank report. They are the predictable result of organizations built around a single founder's judgment, where the founder's continued presence is the plan.

The AI adoption numbers are almost beside the point given everything else, but they follow the same shape: 60% say learning to use AI effectively is their main objective, 47% have not used digital tools or AI in daily operations. Internal expertise gaps and data security concerns are the stated barriers. In an environment where the ownership structure, the continuity planning, and the succession arrangements are all underdeveloped, AI adoption is not the binding constraint — it is a symptom of the same underlying condition, which is that these organizations were built to be run by one person and have not been rebuilt for anything else.
