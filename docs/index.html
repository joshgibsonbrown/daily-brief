Private Markets Secondaries GP-Led Fundraising

CVC's secondaries fund nearly quadrupled in seven years. This isn't a cycle trade anymore.

  • CVC's Secondary Opportunities Fund VI closed at $10bn — up from $5.8bn in 2023 and $2.7bn in 2019, nearly a 4x increase in one fund generation
  • More than 200 new and existing investors backed the raise, and CVC now manages approximately €20bn across its private equity and credit secondaries strategies
  • Secondaries transaction volume hit a record $121bn in H1 2026 alone, per Evercore, with GP-led deals — where the original fund manager controls the process — making up a growing share

The take

CVC just closed its sixth secondaries fund at $10bn — nearly quadruple the $2.7bn it raised for the same strategy in 2019, and up from $5.8bn as recently as 2023. Over 200 investors, backing a strategy CVC didn't even own until it bought Glendower Capital in 2021. That's the number that matters: a firm that entered secondaries five years ago now runs roughly €20bn across secondaries and credit, and its flagship secondaries pool has grown faster than almost anything else on its books.

The consensus read is that this is a liquidity story — sponsors need exits, LPs need distributions, and secondaries are the release valve while the IPO window stays shut. True, but that framing undersells what's actually happening. Secondaries stopped being a countercyclical trade and became a permanent fee engine. The tell is in Pirzio-Biroli's own pitch: he's not selling discounted assets, he's selling the "substantial pipeline" of unrealized PE sitting in aging funds. That pipeline doesn't shrink when markets recover. Holding periods are structurally longer, DPI is structurally worse, and every extra year an asset sits unrealized is another year of secondaries deal flow. CVC isn't timing a dislocation. It's underwriting a permanent feature of the asset class.

Here's the second-order effect nobody's pricing. When a buyout house like CVC builds a $10bn secondaries book, it's no longer a neutral buyer of other people's fund stakes — it's a buyer of its own. The continuation vehicle and GP-led secondary market means CVC can now sit on both sides: originate an asset in a buyout fund, then warehouse it in its own secondaries vehicle when the clock runs out, collecting fees at both ends. Evercore clocked $121bn of secondaries transactions in H1 alone, a record, and a rising share of that is GP-led. The conflict-of-interest question this raises for LPs is real, and the industry is resolving it by simply scaling until the practice looks normal.

For allocators, the read-across is that "secondaries capacity" is now a core competency the big buyout franchises are buying rather than building — CVC via Glendower, and the pattern repeats across the top tier. If you're an LP still treating secondaries as an opportunistic sleeve you rent from a specialist, you're missing that your primary GPs are becoming your secondary counterparties. The question to ask CVC isn't whether $10bn deploys. It's what discount they'll pay when the asset on the block came out of their own Fund IX.

And watch the smaller signal in the same news cycle: Ocean Avenue chasing $750m to co-invest alongside independent sponsors. The barbell is forming — mega-platforms aggregating secondaries fees at the top, nimble co-invest vehicles feeding the sponsor ecosystem at the bottom. The undifferentiated middle is where the fundraising pain lives.

The other side

The bull case on secondaries assumes the overhang of unrealized PE is permanent, but if rate cuts accelerate and the IPO window reopens meaningfully, sponsor-to-sponsor exits and public listings could absorb a chunk of that pipeline before secondaries buyers get to it. A $10bn fund that raised into peak dislocation anxiety could find itself competing for deals in a less distressed market, compressing the discounts that historically justified the strategy. And LP appetite for a $10bn vehicle isn't infinite — at that scale, CVC needs to deploy into larger and larger transactions, which narrows the universe and hands pricing power back to sellers.

The structural argument here is worth unpacking carefully, because it's easy to mistake CVC's growth for a macro call that could reverse. It isn't. The mechanics that are driving secondaries volume — longer holding periods, sluggish distributions, LP portfolios overweight illiquid assets — don't unwind cleanly even in a better exit environment. They accumulated over a decade of cheap capital and aggressive deployment, and the backlog is measured in trillions, not billions. Carlo Pirzio-Biroli, who heads CVC's secondaries strategy, framed the pitch around the large volume of capital tied up in aging PE funds still waiting to be realized. That's not a temporary condition; it's the accumulated consequence of a fundraising supercycle that outran the exit market by years.

CVC's entry point into this business is also worth noting. The firm didn't build a secondaries capability organically — it bought Glendower Capital in 2021, a specialist with its own investor relationships and deal sourcing infrastructure. Five years later, that acquisition has compounded into a €20bn secondaries and credit platform and a flagship fund that is now one of the largest dedicated secondaries vehicles in the market. The acquisition-as-capability-shortcut playbook is increasingly how the top buyout franchises are moving: identify a structurally growing adjacent market, buy the specialist, and scale it under the parent brand's LP relationships. The organic path would have taken a decade; the acquisition path took five years and one fundraising cycle.

The conflict-of-interest dynamic embedded in this model deserves more attention than it typically gets. As GP-led secondaries — transactions where the original fund manager orchestrates the sale of assets into a new vehicle, often one they also manage — become a larger share of total secondaries volume, the line between buyer and seller blurs. A firm running both a buyout fund and a large secondaries fund has the theoretical ability to move an asset from one pocket to the other, collecting management fees at both ends. LPs in the buyout fund and LPs in the secondaries fund may have directly opposing interests in how that asset is priced. The industry's answer to this tension has largely been disclosure and independent valuation, but at $121bn of H1 volume and rising, the scale of the practice is outrunning the governance frameworks built to contain it. Allocators who haven't stress-tested their GP relationships for this dynamic are behind.

The smaller data point in the same news cycle — Ocean Avenue Capital Partners, a special situations investor, targeting up to $750m for a vehicle focused on co-investments alongside independent sponsors — points to the other end of the market forming up. Independent sponsors are deal-by-deal operators who source and execute buyouts without a committed fund behind them, relying on co-investors to fund each transaction. It's a fragmented, relationship-intensive corner of the market that the mega-platforms largely ignore. The barbell structure this creates — aggregated fee engines at the top, nimble co-invest vehicles at the bottom — is real, and the fundraising difficulty is concentrated in the middle tier of secondaries and buyout managers who are neither large enough to offer the CVC platform nor specialized enough to own a niche.

Private Markets Real Estate Japan Infrastructure Fundraising

Ares bought a fund business. The LPs just proved it worked.

  • JDP V closed at ¥612 billion ($4 billion), hitting its hard cap and coming in nearly 50% larger than the 2021-vintage predecessor that raised ¥412 billion
  • CPP Investments — a founding LP in the series since 2011 — wrote a ¥150 billion ($968 million) cornerstone check, equal to 24.5% of the fund
  • With leverage, JDP V carries roughly ¥1.7 trillion ($11 billion) of total investment capacity, and has already committed to projects representing about ¥450 billion

The take

Ares just closed its fifth Japan logistics development fund at ¥612 billion ($4 billion), hitting the hard cap and coming in nearly 50% larger than the 2021 vintage. What makes this one worth flagging isn't the headline number — it's the plumbing underneath. This is the first JDP vintage raised entirely under Ares ownership, inherited via the $3.7 billion purchase of GLP's ex-China fund business in March 2025. Ares didn't just buy assets; it bought a 15-year LP relationship with CPP Investments, and it just proved that relationship survives the change of control.

That's the real story allocators should price. CPPIB wrote a ¥150 billion cornerstone check — 24.5% of the fund, roughly $968 million — into a vehicle managed by a GP that wasn't the GP when the previous vintage closed. LP capital is supposed to be sticky to teams, not logos. Ares just demonstrated you can acquire the team, the track record, and the anchor LP as a package and roll them into a bigger fund without the base flinching. That's the acquisition thesis validated in a single close, and it reframes what the GLP deal actually was: not a real estate buy, a distribution buy.

Here's the contrarian read. Everyone's watching whether the big alternatives managers can keep gathering assets organically. The more interesting move is M&A as a fundraising strategy. Ares paid $3.7 billion and got a platform now running ¥1.7 trillion ($11 billion) of investment capacity in this single fund series, plus Marq Logistics at 655 million square feet globally, plus a data center developer in Ada Infrastructure. The multiple you pay for a fund business is a multiple on the LP relationships it comes with — and those relationships just re-underwrote themselves at a 50% larger fund size. The payback math on the GLP acquisition looks a lot better today than it did on announcement.

Second-order effect nobody's pricing: this is how the digital infrastructure land grab gets financed without anyone calling it that. Logistics real estate and data centers share tenants, power, land banking, and increasingly the same capital pools. Ares inherited Ada Infrastructure inside a logistics wrapper, and the same CPPIB check that funds warehouses in Greater Tokyo is funding the platform that builds compute. Sovereign and pension money that would never green-light a standalone speculative data center allocation is happily cornering a logistics development fund with a proven 2011-vintage track record. The AI buildout is being underwritten through the boring back door.

The read-across: in a market where organic fundraising takes 22 months and DPI stories are scarce, buying an intact LP franchise is now a legitimate go-to-market. Watch which managers are shopping for fund businesses, not just assets. That's the tell.

The other side

Acquiring LP relationships is not the same as owning them. CPP Investments cornered this vintage, but a 24.5% concentration in a single anchor is a fragility, not just a feature — if that relationship sours on vintage six, the fund series reprices overnight. More broadly, the GLP acquisition gave Ares a Japan-specific platform in a market with real structural tailwinds, but the $3.7 billion entry price assumed those tailwinds persist; any sustained yen depreciation, logistics oversupply in Greater Tokyo, or shift in e-commerce demand patterns hits both the asset values and the fundraising narrative simultaneously.

The mechanics of what Ares pulled off here deserve more attention than the headline number. JDP V is, per the source material, the largest closed-end institutional fund ever raised by the Ares real estate business — not just the largest Japan fund, the largest in the entire real estate vertical. That milestone lands roughly 18 months after Ares closed the GLP acquisition in March 2025, which means the integration timeline from deal close to fund close was tight by any standard in institutional real estate.

The GLP deal — Ares paid $3.7 billion for GCP International, GLP's ex-China fund management business — is worth unpacking for readers who weren't tracking it. GLP is one of the largest logistics real estate operators in the world; its ex-China business included the Japan development fund series, the Marq Logistics operating platform, and Ada Infrastructure, a data center developer. Ares didn't acquire a portfolio of buildings. It acquired a fund management franchise with a 15-year vintage history, an operating company capable of developing and running assets, and a set of LP relationships that had been re-underwriting the same manager through multiple cycles.

The Marq Logistics platform, now the operating arm for JDP V, managed approximately 120 million square feet in Japan and more than 655 million square feet globally as of June 30, according to the source material. That scale matters for two reasons. First, it gives Ares a vertically integrated development-to-operations capability that most pure-play fund managers don't have — they can originate, build, and manage without outsourcing the value-creation step. Second, it creates a durable fee stream that sits underneath the fund economics, which changes the return profile of the acquisition itself.

The Ada Infrastructure angle is the one that hasn't been fully priced into the narrative. Ares inherited a data center developer inside a logistics wrapper, and the capital funding JDP V — pension money, sovereign wealth, insurers — is the same capital that is structurally cautious about standalone AI infrastructure exposure. A speculative data center fund pitched cold to a Canadian pension investment board runs into committee-level resistance around track record, concentration, and technology risk. A logistics development fund with a 2011 inception date and five consecutive vintages of institutional backing does not. The AI buildout is finding its way into conservative institutional portfolios through exactly this kind of structural camouflage, and Ares is not the only manager who has noticed.

The fund's geographic focus — Greater Tokyo, Greater Osaka, Nagoya, and other major Japanese markets per the source material — also reflects a deliberate structural bet. Japan's logistics real estate market has been undersupplied in modern, high-clearance facilities relative to the e-commerce penetration curve, and the yen environment over the past several years has made dollar-denominated entry attractive for foreign capital. Whether that entry-point advantage persists into the deployment window for JDP V's ¥450 billion already-committed pipeline is a real question, but the LP base clearly underwrote it.

The broader read-across for the alternatives industry is about M&A as distribution strategy. Organic fundraising in this environment is slow and expensive. Buying a fund business with an intact LP roster, a proven track record, and an operating platform collapses that timeline — and if the first post-acquisition vintage closes 50% larger than the predecessor, the payback math on the acquisition price compresses fast.

Databricks raised $5 billion when it wanted $1 billion. That gap is the whole story.

  • Databricks crossed a $7 billion revenue run rate growing 80% year over year in Q2, up from a $4.8 billion run rate in Q3 of last year
  • The $5 billion round closed at a $190 billion valuation — led by Coatue alongside Blackstone, MGX, T. Rowe Price, and Sixth Street Growth — after investor demand reportedly hit $15 billion against an initial $1 billion target
  • Lakebase, Databricks' serverless Postgres database built for AI agents, already crossed a $100 million revenue run rate, while its Lakehouse data warehousing business surpassed $1.5 billion and is growing more than 100% year over year

The take

Databricks just closed $5 billion at a $190 billion valuation — up from $134 billion in January — while crossing a $7 billion revenue run rate on 80% growth. The number that actually matters isn't the headline valuation. It's Ali Ghodsi's admission that he went in wanting $1 billion and walked out with $5 billion because a news leak turned his phone into a queue of investors terrified of being left off the cap table. That's not a fundraise. That's a demand-side auction where the product being sold is access, not equity.

Here's the contrarian read: the consensus treats this as more AI-bubble froth, and the froth is real, but the underlying business is doing something the froth crowd is missing. Databricks is now generating over $10 million a year each from more than 100 customers, over $1 million from more than 1,000, and it's been adjusted free-cash-flow positive for twelve straight months. That last fact is the tell. A company burning to grow doesn't raise $5 billion when it wanted $1 billion — it raises because capital is being handed to it below its cost of refusing it. The round is a balance-sheet weapon, not a lifeline.

The second-order effect nobody is pricing: this is a Snowflake kill shot dressed as a data-warehouse story. Snowflake popped 20% the same week on a raised outlook and CoCo, its coding assistant, hitting 9,100 accounts — genuinely good numbers. But look at where Databricks is spending. Lakebase, a serverless Postgres built for AI agents, already at a $100 million run rate. Genie for querying. Unity AI Gateway for model governance and cost control. Databricks is not competing for the analytics workload anymore. It's positioning to own the operational data layer that enterprise AI agents actually run on — the place where the query becomes an action. Lakehouse warehousing crossed $1.5 billion and is growing 100%, which means it's taking share directly out of Snowflake's core while Snowflake celebrates a coding tool.

For allocators, the read-across is about who captures the agent economy's rent. Everyone's paying up for the model layer — the OpenAIs and Anthropics. Databricks is quietly building a toll booth one level down, at the data-and-governance layer, which is stickier, harder to rip out, and doesn't get commoditized every time a cheaper open-weight model ships. That's the more defensible position, and the market is still pricing it as a warehouse company.

The caution: $190 billion on a $7 billion run rate is a 27x multiple, and the historical parallel worth flagging is that these private marks are set by the marginal desperate buyer, not by an exit. When the IPO window opens, the public market will re-underwrite the durability of that 80% growth, not the cult. Ghodsi has the cash and the cash flow to wait it out. His investors, chasing relevance, may not be as patient.

The other side

The bear case isn't that the business is fake — it's that 27x revenue is a private-market mark set by the most desperate buyer in a crowded room, not by a public underwriter stress-testing growth durability. Snowflake, the closest public comparable, just raised its own annual revenue outlook and saw its stock jump more than 20% in a single session on genuine AI product traction, which means the competitive pressure on Databricks' core warehousing business is real and accelerating. If Databricks' growth rate compresses even modestly by the time an IPO forces price discovery, the distance between $190 billion and where the public market clears could be painful for late-stage investors who paid up for access rather than value.

The funding mechanics here are worth slowing down on. Databricks CEO Ali Ghodsi reportedly went into this raise targeting $1 billion — a number consistent with a company that has been adjusted free-cash-flow positive for twelve straight months and doesn't need outside capital to operate. What he got was $5 billion, drawn from a reported $15 billion in investor demand. That gap between what was sought and what flooded in tells you something important about the current state of late-stage AI investing: the fear of missing out has become so acute that institutional allocators — Blackstone and T. Rowe Price are not small, unsophisticated players — are effectively bidding against each other for the right to be on the cap table. The product being sold in that dynamic is access and relevance, not a particularly attractive entry price.

The business underneath that frenzy is, however, genuinely strong in ways that get obscured by valuation debates. More than 1,000 customers now generate over $1 million in annualized revenue for Databricks, and more than 100 exceed $10 million annually. That second number is the one to watch — $10 million-plus accounts are deeply embedded, multi-year relationships that don't churn when a competitor cuts price. Combined with twelve months of adjusted free-cash-flow positivity, this is a company that raised $5 billion from a position of strength, not necessity. The capital is a strategic reserve, not oxygen.

How Databricks deploys that reserve matters more than the round size. The three named investment targets — Lakebase, Genie, and Unity AI Gateway — are not incremental feature additions to a data warehouse. They represent a coherent architectural bet on where enterprise AI actually gets operationalized. Lakebase is a serverless Postgres database purpose-built for AI agents, the software programs that increasingly take autonomous actions inside enterprises rather than just answering questions. Genie lets users query business data and trigger actions from natural language. Unity AI Gateway provides governance and cost controls across multiple AI models simultaneously — a critical capability as enterprises run dozens of models from different vendors and need to manage spend and compliance centrally.

Taken together, these products are an attempt to own the operational data layer that sits beneath the model layer. Every enterprise AI agent needs to read from and write to real business data, needs guardrails on what models it can call and at what cost, and needs audit trails for compliance. Databricks is building the infrastructure that makes all of that possible. That layer is structurally stickier than the model layer above it — swapping out GPT-4 for a cheaper open-weight alternative is increasingly routine, but ripping out the data governance and agent infrastructure woven into your production systems is a multi-year migration project.

The Snowflake comparison is instructive as outside context. Snowflake, which competes directly with Databricks' Lakehouse warehousing product, raised its annual product revenue outlook to approximately $6.07 billion and saw its stock jump more than 20% after reporting that its AI coding assistant CoCo reached 9,100 customer accounts. Those are real numbers worth respecting. But Databricks' Lakehouse business alone is now at a $1.5 billion run rate growing more than 100% year over year — meaning it is taking share in Snowflake's core market at the same time Databricks is building an entirely new product surface that Snowflake does not yet have a direct answer to. Snowflake is winning the analytics layer. Databricks is competing there and simultaneously trying to own the agent infrastructure layer that comes next.

Tech & AI Supply Chain Energy National Security

The AI buildout's real chokepoint isn't chips. It's the gear nobody talks about.

  • Trump signed an executive order last week declaring a national emergency over foreign-made bulk-power equipment, directly targeting data center supply chains
  • Power transformers are already in a 15% market shortage in 2026, with the high-capacity segment hyperscalers depend on most acutely affected
  • Chinese firms supply roughly two-thirds of global optical transceiver units, and Western rivals Coherent and Lumentum cannot absorb that volume within 24 months per Counterpoint research

The take

Everyone spent 2025 obsessing over the compute stack — who gets Nvidia allocation, who's export-controlled, whether Chinese labs can close the frontier gap. Trump's executive order last week, declaring a national emergency over foreign-made bulk-power equipment, quietly reveals the real chokepoint: the boring gear that turns grid voltage into server power. China supplies roughly 30% of certain transformer and switchgear categories, over 40% of U.S. battery imports, and Chinese firms account for about two-thirds of global optical transceiver units. The war for AI supremacy was never just about silicon. It's about steel, copper, and cathode material.

Here's what nobody is pricing correctly. The compute constraint is solvable with money and time — you can build fabs, you can design around export controls. The power stack is a physical bottleneck with a lead-time problem that money makes worse, not better. Power transformers are already in a 15% market shortage in 2026, and the 100 MVA+ segment — exactly what hyperscalers need — is precisely where they've been leaning on Chinese suppliers to compress lead times. Ban the Chinese units and you don't reshore the supply, you just extend the queue. Counterpoint's own analyst says Coherent and Lumentum can't absorb Innolight and Eoptolink's volume inside 24 months. That's not a policy gap, that's a physics gap.

So the trade is straightforward and it's already visible in Nvidia's behavior: they put $2 billion each into Lumentum and Coherent in March, well ahead of any formal ban. That's not portfolio diversification, that's Jensen buying insurance on his own supply chain before Washington forces the issue. The allocator read: the scarce asset in the AI buildout is shifting from the thing that computes to the thing that energizes. Grid equipment makers — Hitachi Energy putting $1B into U.S. transformer capacity, Siemens Energy matching it — are the picks-and-shovels play that isn't priced like one yet, because the market still files them under "industrials" instead of "AI infrastructure."

The second-order effect is margin, and it runs the wrong way for the hyperscalers. Every restriction that removes a Chinese transformer or transceiver from the bill of materials raises the delivered cost of a gigawatt and lengthens the timeline to fill it. The capex numbers Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon are guiding to assume the cheapest, fastest components — the ones about to be banned. When U.S. capacity has to triple from 62 GW to 152 GW by 2030 on a domestically constrained power stack, either the buildout slips or the cost curve bends up. Both outcomes compress the returns on the very AI capex the whole market has already underwritten. The compute bulls are modeling a supply chain that Washington is actively dismantling.

The other side

The reshoring argument has real momentum behind it: Hitachi Energy committed $1 billion to U.S. transformer production in September 2025, Siemens Energy matched it in February, and Nvidia put $2 billion each into Lumentum and Coherent in March — private capital is already moving before any formal ban lands. If the administration sequences restrictions carefully, giving domestic suppliers time to scale before Chinese units are cut off, the transition could be managed without a catastrophic supply gap. The national security case is also genuine: a grid that depends on foreign-made switchgear and batteries is a vulnerability that compounds as AI infrastructure becomes more central to the economy.

The deeper problem is that the power stack and the compute stack have completely different industrial logics, and Washington is applying compute-stack thinking to a physical-infrastructure problem. Chips are hard to design but relatively fast to manufacture once you have a fab running. A large power transformer — specifically the 100 MVA-plus units that hyperscalers need to step transmission voltage down to server-usable levels — takes years to engineer, tool, and deliver. The shortage in that segment isn't a policy artifact; it exists right now, in 2026, before any ban. Chinese suppliers became the pressure valve precisely because they could compress lead times when domestic and European manufacturers were already backlogged. Remove the valve and the backlog doesn't shrink, it grows.

The optical transceiver situation has a similar structure but moves faster, which makes it both more tractable and more immediately disruptive. Zhongji Innolight and Eoptolink — two Chinese firms that most people outside the data center industry have never heard of — collectively anchor a supply chain that moves data between servers at the speeds AI workloads require. The Trump administration is reportedly drafting an import ban on new Chinese optical transceivers, and the FCC already added foreign-made power inverters to its national security risk list in July. Counterpoint's Neil Shah put the capacity gap plainly in an August report: Coherent and Lumentum have the photonic designs, but they lack the cleanroom capacity, automated packaging infrastructure, and yield scale to absorb Chinese volume within 12 to 24 months. That is a manufacturing gap, not a funding gap — and it doesn't close just because Nvidia wrote two large checks.

What makes this structurally different from the chip export-control story is the upstream exposure. Johns Hopkins associate professor Yury Dvorkin, whose research is cited in the source material, flags that the dependency runs deeper than finished equipment: copper, electrical steel, and battery cathode materials are all areas where China holds significant upstream leverage. China accounts for over 40% of U.S. battery imports. Restricting the finished transformer or transceiver doesn't automatically reshore the raw material inputs those domestic alternatives depend on. You can build a new Hitachi transformer plant in the United States and still find yourself waiting on Chinese-sourced electrical steel.

The S&P Global forecast that U.S. data center capacity needs to grow from 62 GW in March 2026 to 152 GW by 2030 — roughly a 2.5x increase in under four years — is the number that puts all of this in context. That growth rate was modeled against a supply chain that includes Chinese components. Strip those components out mid-buildout, without domestic capacity ready to absorb the volume, and the math on that forecast stops working. The hyperscalers' capex guidance assumes the cheapest, fastest bill of materials. The executive order signed last week is the beginning of a process that makes that bill of materials more expensive and slower to fulfill. The market hasn't repriced that yet, because it's still filing Hitachi Energy and Siemens Energy under industrials rather than treating them as critical AI infrastructure plays.

Financial Markets & Economic News Fed Policy Rates Inflation

Warsh flipped the Fed's burden of proof. The economy didn't give him a reason to.

  • ING now calls a September 25bp hike more likely than not — not because their macro model changed, but because Warsh's Jackson Hole speech shifted the Fed's reaction function from 'hold unless forced' to 'hike unless justified'
  • Inflation has been above the Fed's 2% target for 65 consecutive months, but has been undershooting expectations recently — shelter, which carries a 35% weight in the CPI basket, is actively cooling
  • Middle- and lower-income households are under severe strain: real disposable incomes have flatlined for 18 months, the savings rate sits near 3%, and delinquency rates on credit cards and auto loans are at record highs

The take

Here's what happened at Jackson Hole: Kevin Warsh, now chairing a Fed most of the Street assumed was parked in neutral until 2027, flipped the burden of proof. The framing went from "hold unless the data forces a hike" to "hike unless the data justifies a pause." ING, whose own macro model still says the Fed can afford to wait, is now calling a 25bp September hike more likely than not — because the reaction function changed even though the economy didn't. That's the whole story, and it's more important than any single data print.

The consensus read is that Warsh is fighting inflation that's been above target for 65 straight months. Look closer and that doesn't hold. Inflation has been *undershooting* recently. Shelter — 35% of the basket — is cooling as the property market stagnates. Wage growth is subdued. The disinflation stall is an oil-and-Hormuz story, which is supply-side and which a rate hike does exactly nothing to fix. You don't hike into flatlining real incomes, an 18-month income stall, a 3% savings rate, and record credit-card and auto delinquencies to fight a problem that's already resolving. This isn't a data-driven hike. It's a credibility hike — Warsh sounded hawkish in June, backtracked in July, went hawkish again in August, and now can't blink without torching his own authority in his first year running the building. The move is about him, not the CPI.

The tell is who's cheering. Bessent is "on board" while watching long-dated yields climb, and Trump has given Warsh a pass with "he'll do what he has to do." A White House that spent two years demanding cuts is suddenly comfortable with a hike. Why? Because a bond market that's punishing the long end for fiscal profligacy needs an inflation-fighting Fed to anchor the curve. The hike isn't monetary policy — it's a term-premium management operation dressed as insurance. They're paying 25bp at the front end to try to buy back credibility at the 10- and 30-year, where the actual funding problem lives.

For allocators, two things. First, don't position for a hiking cycle — ING's late-1990s parallel (one "risk management" hike in March 1997, then a long pause) is the right frame, and one-and-done is bullish for duration once the September theatre clears. Second, and this is what nobody's pricing: a Fed hiking into a two-speed economy where the top end and tech capex carry everything while the bottom two-thirds drown in delinquencies is deliberately squeezing the weak cohort to protect a credibility number. That's not a soft landing. That's a policy choice to widen the gap, and the credit spread that eventually reprices it is the trade.

The other side

Warsh's hawkish pivot isn't irrational on its own terms. Inflation has been above target for over five years, financial conditions don't look tight in an economy running at full employment, and a Fed that keeps finding reasons to delay loses the forward-guidance credibility that makes monetary policy work in the first place. If Warsh lets one more soft print talk him out of a hike he telegraphed at Jackson Hole, the market learns that Fed signals are negotiable — and that's a much harder problem to fix than one unnecessary 25bp move.

The mechanics of what Warsh did at Jackson Hole are worth spelling out, because the shift is subtle in language and large in consequence. Before the speech, ING's framework required a specific combination of weak data — non-farm payrolls below 75,000, unemployment holding at 4.1%, and core CPI at 0.3% month-on-month or above — to trigger a hike. After the speech, the bar inverted: it now takes payrolls below 25,000, possibly outright job losses, and a core CPI print below 0.2% month-on-month to prevent one. That's not a tweak. That's a different central bank.

The macro backdrop ING describes makes the hike harder to justify on fundamentals alone. The economy is running on two engines — high-income consumer spending and tech capital expenditure — while the rest stalls. Business investment outside tech is subdued. Imports are dragging on growth as foreign-sourced components feed the tech boom. Higher government borrowing costs are feeding through to mortgage rates and corporate credit, adding a headwind that a rate hike would only extend. The $166 billion in IEEPA tariff refunds — money returned to corporate America from tariffs collected under emergency trade powers — is providing a cash flow cushion that masks some underlying weakness, but it isn't structural relief.

The inflation picture is similarly complicated. The headline disinflation stall traces directly to the Strait of Hormuz situation disrupting oil and gas flows — a supply-side constraint that rate policy cannot address. ING's own geopolitical read is that the situation improves and oil resumes its decline later in the year. Chipflation tied to semiconductor shortages and re-escalating trade tensions with Canada add noise, but computers and smartphones together carry less than 0.7 percentage points of weight in the CPI basket, limiting their aggregate impact. ING's base case has inflation returning to 2% by summer 2027 without a hike.

The late-1990s parallel ING invokes is worth taking seriously. The Fed cut rates in early 1996, paused, then delivered a single 25bp 'risk management' hike in March 1997 before holding for well over a year. The economy didn't crater; the hike served its signaling purpose and the cycle ended there. If that's the template — and ING explicitly says they see no need for a series of hikes — then the September move is less about tightening financial conditions and more about resetting the Fed's credibility baseline after a summer of mixed signals.

For the bond market, the implication is directional once the September decision clears. A credible one-and-done removes the tail risk of a sustained hiking cycle, which is the scenario duration investors are currently pricing against. The more uncomfortable second-order question is what it means to deliberately tighten into a two-speed economy. The cohort with floating-rate debt, depleted savings, and maxed credit cards absorbs the hike immediately. The tech-and-high-income cohort, which is driving the growth numbers Warsh is pointing to as evidence that conditions aren't tight, barely feels it. The hike lands hardest on the people least responsible for the inflation it's nominally fighting.
