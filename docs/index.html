Private Markets Secondaries Spinouts LP Dynamics

The secondaries boom is minting spinouts. The incumbents built the training ground themselves.

  • Clipway Secondary Fund I closed at $6.4 billion — the largest debut raise in secondaries history — from 186 LPs including sovereign wealth funds and pension funds across the globe.
  • Secondaries market volume crossed $50 billion in just the first half of 2025, with Ardian closing a $30 billion fund in January and Blackstone targeting $22 billion for its next vehicle.
  • Clipway, founded in 2021 by alumni of Ardian (a leading private markets firm), built a proprietary database covering 38,500 private companies and 3,400 PE funds in roughly three years — the kind of data infrastructure incumbents claimed took decades to assemble.

The take

A four-year-old firm with no track record just raised $6.4 billion for its first-ever fund — the largest debut in secondaries history — from 186 LPs including Mubadala, sovereign wealth funds, and pensions. Clipway was founded in 2021 by Ardian alumni, and it began raising in 2023 into what the industry politely calls a "challenging" fundraising environment. In any other strategy, a debut manager with zero realized returns raising this much would be a red flag. Here it's a signal of where the money is actually moving.

The consensus reads this as "secondaries are hot" and moves on. That misses the real story, which is a talent-and-capital arbitrage running inside the secondaries boom itself. The LPs didn't back Clipway despite the lack of track record — they backed the Ardian pedigree and treated the new firm as a cleaner, hungrier, better-aligned version of the platform these same executives helped build. When Ardian closes a $30 billion fund and Blackstone targets $22 billion for Strategic Partners X, the marginal secondaries dollar starts worrying about deployment pressure and fee drag on a fund that size. A $6.4 billion middle-market vehicle is the antidote: big enough to matter, small enough to actually pick.

Here's what nobody's pricing. The barrier to entry in secondaries was supposed to be data — you can't underwrite a portfolio of 3,400 funds without decades of proprietary deal flow and NAV history. Clipway just showed you can rebuild that in 38,500 companies of tech-enabled tooling and walk out the door with the relationships. That collapses the moat that Lexington, Coller, and Ardian spent thirty years building. The incumbents' pitch has always been "you need our history." The new pitch is "we have the same brains, better software, and none of the legacy fund tail." If LPs believe that — and $6.4 billion says a meaningful cohort does — expect a wave of Ardian, Lexington, and Coller partners to spin out over the next 24 months. GP-stakes buyers and seeders should already be circling.

The second-order effect is on pricing discipline. Secondaries crossed $50 billion in first-half volume, and every mega-fund closing tightens the bid on LP-led portfolios. When Ardian, Blackstone, Coller, Ares, and now Clipway are all deploying into the same middle-market book, the discounts that made this strategy attractive compress toward par. The DPI story that pulled LPs into secondaries in the first place — cash back faster than in primary buyout — erodes exactly when the capital gets crowded. Clipway raised into the sweet spot. The firm that raises Fund II at $12 billion in 2027 will be buying a worse book at a worse price and calling it scale.

The other side

Three years of database-building is not thirty years of cycle-tested judgment. Secondaries underwriting lives and dies on knowing which GPs will defend NAV honestly versus which ones are marking portfolios to wishful thinking, and that instinct comes from having sat across the table from the same managers through 2009 and 2016, not from a well-structured spreadsheet. Clipway's $6.4 billion raise also happened at the exact moment LP enthusiasm for secondaries is peaking — meaning Fund I gets the tailwind, but the real test is whether the team can generate strong DPI (cash returned to investors relative to capital invested) when the cycle turns and portfolio marks compress across the board.

The framing that matters here is not that secondaries are popular — everyone knows that. The framing is that a specific structural arbitrage just got proven out, and it has implications well beyond Clipway.

For the past two decades, the secondaries business operated like a guild. The pitch to LPs was simple: we have proprietary data on thousands of funds, relationships with every major GP, and a track record of buying LP interests at discounts that produce superior cash returns. Lexington Partners, one of the earliest dedicated secondaries firms, took the better part of a decade to build credibility before institutions wrote it real checks. Coller Capital, another early pioneer, similarly relied on institutional memory accumulated over multiple market cycles. The moat was real — or at least it appeared to be.

What Clipway demonstrated is that the moat was mostly about people, not institutions. When experienced Ardian executives walked out the door with deep GP relationships and the conviction to build a tech-enabled data layer from scratch, they were able to credibly replicate years of institutional knowledge in a compressed timeframe. Strategic partnerships with Mubadala (the Abu Dhabi sovereign wealth fund), Carmignac (a French asset manager), and General Atlantic (a U.S. growth equity firm) gave Clipway early validation that accelerated LP trust. That combination — pedigree, software, and a few anchor validators — turned out to be sufficient.

The historical parallel worth drawing is what happened to bulge-bracket fixed income desks in the 1990s. When Salomon Brothers and Goldman Sachs trained an entire generation of mortgage traders and quants, they didn't anticipate that the skills were portable. Those teams spun out and built hedge funds that competed directly with their former employers, often with better alignment and less bureaucratic drag. Private equity went through a similar dynamic with the first wave of KKR and Blackstone alumni. Secondaries is now entering its own version of that cycle, and Clipway is the canary.

The capital dynamics accelerate the pressure. When Ardian is managing $30 billion in a single fund, the deployment math gets uncomfortable fast. A fund that size needs to put to work roughly $6 to $8 billion a year to stay on a reasonable investment pace, which means you are buying larger positions, accepting tighter discounts, and crowding into the same top-tier GP portfolios as every other mega-fund. The middle market — smaller LP-led transactions, less-covered fund vintages, regional buyout managers — becomes systematically underserved. A focused $6.4 billion vehicle is structurally better positioned to work that part of the market than a $30 billion platform that needs to write $500 million checks to move the needle.

The risk worth watching is price. When secondaries volume hits $50 billion in a single half-year, the discount-to-NAV that historically powered the strategy's return profile starts to compress. LP-led transactions that traded at 15 to 20 percent discounts a few years ago are now clearing closer to par or even above in competitive processes. Clipway may have raised at the right moment, but the next cohort of spinouts raising in 2026 and 2027 will be deploying into a market where the structural advantage has already been partially arbitraged away. The window for this trade is open right now — it will not stay open indefinitely.

Private Markets Tech PE LP Dynamics Fundraising

Francisco Partners took 81% of tech PE's oxygen. The remaining 37 funds are on life support.

  • Francisco Partners closed $21.2B across two funds — a $16.4B flagship and a $4.6B mid-market vehicle — both beating targets, in just eight months from launch to final close
  • The firm claims top-3 finishes in six consecutive HEC-Dow Jones Large Buyout Performance Rankings, an independently verified consistency run that almost no competitor can match
  • The broader tech PE market has shed roughly 82% of its 2022 peak volume on an annualized basis, collapsing from $146B that year to a $26.2B pace in H1 2026

The take

Francisco Partners just closed $21.2 billion across two funds — a $16.4B flagship and a $4.6B mid-market vehicle — both blowing past target by 17% to 31%, in what is the firm's largest raise in 27 years. The number worth sitting with isn't the $21B. It's that Francisco Partners, a single firm run by DJ Deb and Andrew Brown, captured roughly 81% of all tech-focused PE capital raised in the first half of 2026. Thirty-eight funds split $26.2B; one firm took $21.2B of the ecosystem's oxygen. That's not a fundraise. That's a market clearing event.

The consensus read is that this proves LPs still love tech PE. Wrong. It proves LPs have stopped believing in tech PE as a category and are now buying exactly three names and starving everyone else. The 2022 peak was $146B across the sector. Annualized H1 2026 is running at a fraction of that. The money didn't leave — it concentrated. When allocators cut their manager lists in a cold market, they don't trim proportionally; they keep the top-decile track record and zero out the middle. Francisco Partners ranking top-3 in six consecutive HEC-Dow Jones large-buyout rankings is the entire pitch. The other 37 funds are competing for scraps, and most of them are quietly managing out old vintages while pretending they're marketing new ones.

Here's the second-order effect nobody is pricing: this concentration is happening at the precise moment the underlying asset class is structurally impaired. Tech buyouts work when you buy software at 8x and exit at 8x-plus after operational improvement. AI breaks the exit multiple, not the operations. An AI-native competitor can now replicate core vertical-SaaS functionality within 18 months of your acquisition, and no operational lever covers a compression from 8x to 5x. FP's answer — hide in healthcare IT, fintech, industrial software where regulatory moats slow adoption — is a real thesis, but it's a thesis about how long the moat holds, not whether it holds. AI moves faster than compliance frameworks. Even inside regulated verticals, contract renewals are already repricing down as buyers gain credible AI alternatives.

Now stack that against portfolio construction. FP VIII is 20-22 companies. High conviction cuts both ways: one or two AI-disrupted exits drag fund-level returns in a way a 40-name book absorbs. So the smartest LPs in the world — CalPERS, PennSERS, Boston Retirement — just concentrated their tech exposure into a concentrated fund at the top of a compressing cycle, and they did it because the alternative was worse.

The tell for allocators: this is a flight to the manager, not to the strategy. When the safest name in a sector is raising records while the sector collapses 82%, you're not looking at conviction. You're looking at the last chair before the music stops, and everyone's paying a premium to sit in it.

The other side

The LP flight to Francisco Partners may reflect rational portfolio construction rather than panic. CalPERS and PennSERS — two of the largest public pension funds in the United States — didn't arrive at this allocation by accident; they've watched FP compound returns through multiple cycles and are paying for proven operational infrastructure, not just a brand name. On the AI disruption risk specifically, FP's historical tilt toward healthcare IT and industrial software — sectors where a hospital system can't swap its billing platform because a new AI model showed up — means the multiple compression thesis has to work through genuine switching costs and multi-year regulatory cycles before it touches exit prices, which may be slow enough that FP's 5-to-7 year hold periods clear the worst of it.

Start with the mechanics of how capital concentration actually works in private equity, because it matters for understanding what just happened. When a pension fund or endowment runs a manager review in a down market, the analyst presenting to the investment committee is not recommending a basket of promising mid-tier funds. The political economy inside institutional allocators punishes career risk. You don't lose your job for backing the firm that ranked top-3 in six consecutive HEC-Dow Jones large-buyout rankings. You do lose your job for backing the 22nd-best tech PE fund in a vintage that blew up. That dynamic, not some abstract confidence in tech buyouts, is the engine behind Francisco Partners pulling $21.2B while 38 competitors split the remaining $5B.

The historical parallel worth reaching for here is what happened to hedge fund allocations after 2008. Capital didn't stay evenly distributed across the hedge fund universe as the market recovered — it collapsed into a small number of multi-strategy platforms like Citadel and Millennium while hundreds of single-strategy managers slowly wound down. The mechanism was identical: institutional allocators, burned and risk-averse, stopped diversifying across managers and started concentrating in the names they could defend to their boards. Tech PE appears to be in the early innings of the same consolidation.

What makes the Francisco Partners raise structurally interesting beyond the headline number is the two-fund architecture. Running a flagship buyout vehicle at $16.4B alongside a mid-market fund, Francisco Partners Agility IV, at $4.6B simultaneously is not standard practice. Most firms separate those processes to avoid LP confusion about where their capital lands. FP ran them in parallel, closed both oversubscribed, and used the mid-market fund to capture deal flow that would be too small for a $400M minimum check. That's a full-spectrum sourcing machine, not just a big flagship.

The carveout pipeline deserves more attention than it's getting in the coverage of this raise. When large corporations face activist pressure to improve margins, the first thing that goes is the non-core technology division that made sense to acquire in 2019 but no longer fits the narrative. That pipeline is expanding in real time. Francisco Partners has built its sourcing relationships — with corporate development teams, investment banks, and operating executives — specifically around identifying those assets before they hit a formal auction process. That proprietary sourcing advantage is hard to replicate and is probably worth more in the current environment than any amount of dry powder.

The AI multiple compression risk is real but the timing is uneven across verticals, and that unevenness is where FP is making its actual bet. A generic horizontal SaaS company selling project management software to mid-market businesses is already facing pricing pressure from AI-native alternatives. A company managing pharmacy benefit administration for a large insurer is not, because the regulatory liability for errors is enormous and the integration surface area with legacy systems is vast. FP is essentially arbitraging the difference between those two disruption timelines. The question allocators should be asking is not whether the moat exists but whether it lasts long enough for a fund closed in mid-2026 to deploy, improve, and exit before AI pricing compression reaches those regulated verticals. On a seven-year fund life, that's a 2033 horizon. Betting that healthcare IT switching costs hold through 2033 is a more defensible position than it sounds today.

Tech & AI

Big Tech's open-weight letter is a margin defense dressed as principle. The absentees are the tell.

  • Over 20 companies — including Nvidia, Microsoft, Meta, Palantir, Dell, and Andreessen Horowitz — sent the Trump administration a letter opposing any ban on Chinese open-weight AI models, which are downloadable models users run on their own hardware rather than paying per-query through a cloud service.
  • The White House is actively weighing whether to ban Chinese open-weight models like DeepSeek and Qwen on national security grounds, and the coalition warns that small businesses already dependent on those models would be stranded by such a move.
  • OpenAI and Anthropic — the two frontier labs whose per-token revenue is most directly threatened by free Chinese alternatives — did not sign the letter.

The take

More than 20 tech companies — including Nvidia, Microsoft, Meta, Palantir, Dell and Andreessen Horowitz — sent the Trump administration a letter this week arguing against a ban on Chinese open-weight AI models, the kind you can download and run on your own hardware rather than renting through an API. Their stated worry: block DeepSeek and Qwen, and American small businesses that already depend on them get stranded, while nothing stops the models from spreading anyway. It reads as a principled free-market defense. It isn't.

Look at who signed and who didn't. Nvidia wants every model everywhere running on its chips — Chinese weights fine-tuned in Ohio still sell H-series GPUs and, more importantly, keep the CUDA software ecosystem as the default substrate. Meta needs the "open-weight is safe and good" narrative alive because Llama is its entire strategic wedge, and the cleanest way to defend Llama is to defend the category. Microsoft hosts everything on Azure and is agnostic about whose model wins as long as the compute meter runs. This isn't a coalition of principle. It's a coalition of people whose margins don't care what nationality the weights are.

Now notice the absentees. OpenAI and Anthropic — the two labs whose per-token pricing gets vaporized when a free Chinese model does 90% of the job — are not on this letter. That's the tell. The signatories are the infrastructure and distribution layer, which monetizes AI regardless of which model wins. The abstainers are the frontier model layer, which only monetizes if Western models stay scarce and expensive. When a policy fight splits an industry precisely along the line of "who sells picks and shovels versus who sells the gold," the picks-and-shovels crowd will always argue for more mining, more entrants, more models. That is not the same as arguing for American strategic advantage.

The second-order effect nobody is pricing: banning Chinese open weights wouldn't protect anything, because the capability is already downloaded onto ten thousand laptops and mirrored across every model-hosting site on earth. What a ban actually does is hand pricing power back to OpenAI and Anthropic by making the cheap alternative legally radioactive for regulated enterprises — banks, hospitals, defense contractors. That's why the frontier labs are staying quiet and letting the infrastructure players look like the greedy ones. They'd take a ban in a heartbeat.

For allocators, the read-across is simple. The open-weight commoditization thesis is now so obvious that the entire hyperscaler and chip complex is willing to lobby the White House to protect it, because it's the demand engine for their capex. The scarce, defensible asset isn't the model — it's the compute, the distribution, and the regulated-enterprise moat. Bet on the toll roads, not the frontier.

The other side

The coalition's core empirical claim isn't wrong: Chinese open-weight models are already downloaded and widely mirrored, so a ban would inflict real costs on American small businesses while doing almost nothing to suppress the capability. There is also a legitimate civil-liberties and innovation argument that letting the government decide which model weights are legal to run sets a chilling precedent that could eventually be turned against domestic open-source development as well. If the ban is mostly security theater, endorsing it hands regulators a tool with long-term downsides that outweigh any near-term strategic gain.

The structural logic of this coalition becomes clearer when you map each signatory's actual revenue model. Nvidia's business is selling graphics processing units and locking developers into its CUDA software stack — the layer that sits beneath every model regardless of origin. Whether a company fine-tunes a Chinese open-weight model or trains its own from scratch, those workloads almost certainly run on Nvidia silicon. More open-weight adoption, even from Hangzhou, is simply more addressable market. Dell sells the servers those models run on at the enterprise edge. Microsoft's Azure cloud platform charges for compute and storage whether the model it's serving was built in San Francisco or Shenzhen. None of these companies have a business reason to care about the nationality of the weights — they monetize the plumbing, not the water.

Meta's position is slightly different and worth unpacking separately. Meta releases its Llama series of open-weight models as a deliberate strategic move — the company doesn't sell AI directly, so commoditizing the model layer hurts competitors like OpenAI while costing Meta very little. Defending Chinese open-weight models is therefore defending the legal and regulatory legitimacy of the entire open-weight category, which is the same category Llama lives in. An administration willing to ban DeepSeek on national security grounds is an administration that has just established the precedent to restrict any open-weight model it deems problematic. Meta cannot afford that precedent even if the first target is Chinese.

Andreessen Horowitz, the venture firm behind many AI startups, has been the most vocal public advocate for open-weight models for years, so its signature is consistent with its published position. But it's worth noting that a16z's portfolio companies overwhelmingly build on top of commodity model layers rather than competing at the frontier. Cheap, freely available models are literally the input cost their portfolio companies depend on.

The historical parallel that comes to mind is the semiconductor export control debates of the 1990s, when American chip and software companies lobbied hard against encryption export restrictions. The argument was framed around freedom and competitiveness. The underlying driver was that every regulation-compliant foreign sale they lost was revenue they'd never recover. The government eventually loosened the rules, and the companies were right that the restrictions were porous — but the lobbying effort was nakedly commercial even when the rhetoric was principled.

The second-order dynamic that most commentary is missing is what a ban would actually accomplish for the companies not on this letter. OpenAI and Anthropic don't need to lobby for a ban; they just need to stay quiet while the infrastructure players argue against one, and then collect the regulatory windfall if the White House acts anyway. Regulated enterprises — banks, hospital systems, defense contractors — can't afford the legal ambiguity of running a banned model even if it technically still works. That constraint doesn't apply to a startup in a coworking space, but it absolutely applies to the Fortune 500 procurement cycles where OpenAI and Anthropic are trying to expand. A ban hands them that market without requiring them to compete on price or capability.

For anyone allocating capital, the takeaway is that this letter inadvertently maps the commodity layer of the AI stack in real time. When the entire hyperscaler and chip complex is willing to spend political capital defending open-weight Chinese models, they are telling you exactly how dependent their demand forecast is on continued model proliferation. The compute and distribution infrastructure is the durable toll road. The frontier model business remains a pricing war with no obvious floor.

Tech & AI Semiconductors South Korea Memory

The AI bottleneck moved from chips to memory. The market hasn't caught up.

  • SK Hynix and Nvidia announced a US$500 billion partnership covering next-generation HBM4 high-bandwidth memory supply and jointly developed AI data centers, with a 2-gigawatt facility due online in 2027.
  • Samsung signed an MOU with Broadcom covering up to US$200 billion across memory chips, foundry services, and advanced packaging using sub-2-nanometer process technology.
  • Anthropic was valued at US$965 billion in a May funding round that named Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron as critical supply partners — signaling frontier AI labs are treating memory access as an existential input, not a procurement line item.

The take

South Korea just announced US$950 billion in AI supply deals in a single afternoon in San Francisco — SK Group signing US$750 billion (including a US$500 billion SK Hynix-Nvidia memory pact), and Samsung inking up to US$200 billion with Broadcom. The reflexive read is that this is another headline capex number in an industry drowning in them, the kind of round trillion-dollar figure that means nothing because nobody will ever reconcile it against actual bookings. That read is lazy. The interesting thing here is not the size. It's what the buyers are willing to pre-commit to lock down, and what that tells you about where the real constraint in the AI stack now sits.

For two years the bottleneck story was GPUs and the choke point was Nvidia's logic silicon. That's over. The choke point is now high-bandwidth memory — the stacked DRAM that feeds the accelerator — and there are exactly three companies on earth who can make HBM4 at volume: SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron. Two of the three are Korean, and Jensen Huang just wrote what amounts to a half-trillion-dollar option to guarantee his slice of their output through the Rubin generation. When Chey Tae-won quotes Huang as effectively saying "I want more" and warns Nvidia's own five-year memory forecast is already too low, that's not salesmanship. That's a duopoly supplier telling you he has more pricing power than at any point in the history of the memory business, which has historically been the most brutally cyclical, commoditized corner of semiconductors.

Here's the second-order move nobody is pricing. The tell is buried at the bottom: Chey says Anthropic is "making its own computing power," and OpenAI has asked SK to supply chips directly rather than through Nvidia. The frontier labs are trying to disintermediate Nvidia by going straight to the memory and foundry layer — Anthropic exploring custom silicon, Broadcom's Samsung deal covering sub-2nm foundry and advanced packaging, not just DRAM. The labs figured out that owning the model isn't a moat if you're renting the compute at Nvidia's margin. So they're racing upstream to the one layer that is genuinely capacity-constrained and can't be spun up in eighteen months. Memory fabs take three-plus years and US$20 billion each to build.

For an allocator, the read-across is that the value in this cycle is migrating from the logic monopolist to the memory oligopoly, and from merchant chips to captive vertical supply. If you're long the AI trade purely through Nvidia, you own the layer everyone is now actively engineering around. The Korean memory names, priced for cyclicality, are being handed multi-year take-or-pay demand by counterparties with US$965 billion valuations. That is not a commodity anymore. That's toll infrastructure that hasn't re-rated yet.

The other side

These headline numbers are aspirational frameworks, not signed purchase orders, and the history of announced tech megadeals is littered with MOUs that quietly expired. South Korea's semiconductor champions have seen this movie before: a supercycle demand wave triggers record capacity commitments, supply catches up faster than anyone models, and the memory price collapses — as it did violently in 2022 and again in 2019. If even one of the hyperscalers pulling demand forward hits a capex pause or a model architecture shift reduces HBM requirements, the Korean fabs are left holding stranded capacity at exactly the moment they've priced themselves as toll infrastructure.

Say more →

The history of the memory business is worth dwelling on, because it makes what is happening right now genuinely unusual. DRAM and NAND have for decades been the canonical examples of commodity semiconductor markets: capital-intensive, cyclical, brutally price-competitive, and structurally incapable of sustaining margins because no single producer could maintain meaningful differentiation. SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron survived largely by outlasting weaker competitors through downturns, not by wielding pricing power. The idea that memory suppliers would one day have AI labs — companies with nearly trillion-dollar valuations — anxiously checking in on chip availability and urging Korean conglomerates to supply them directly is a genuine structural inversion of the power dynamics that defined this industry for thirty years.

The reason it is happening now comes down to physics and time. High-bandwidth memory, the specific type of DRAM at the center of these deals, is not conventional memory. HBM is built by stacking multiple DRAM dies vertically and connecting them through microscopic channels called through-silicon vias, then bonding the finished stack directly onto an AI accelerator using advanced packaging techniques. The process is extraordinarily difficult to execute at volume, which is why only three companies can do it and why SK Hynix, which pioneered the technology, still leads in yield and generation. You cannot solve an HBM shortage the way you solve a vanilla DRAM shortage, by running existing fabs harder or converting capacity from another product line. A new HBM-capable fab requires north of US$20 billion in capital expenditure and takes three or more years from groundbreaking to first usable output. That time constraint is the entire basis of the current supplier leverage.

The Anthropic detail is where this gets strategically interesting beyond the headline numbers. Anthropic, the AI safety company backed by Google and Amazon among others, is reportedly exploring custom chip design — meaning it wants to build its own AI accelerators rather than buy them from Nvidia. OpenAI has separately asked SK Group to supply chips directly, bypassing Nvidia as the intermediary. Both moves are pointing at the same recognition: if you are a frontier lab running inference and training at scale, your cost structure is largely determined by what you pay for compute, and what you pay for compute is largely determined by Nvidia's gross margin, which has been running above 70 percent. The labs are not going to accept that indefinitely if there is an alternative path.

The alternative path goes through the foundry and memory layer. Broadcom, which designs custom AI chips for Google and others, is now deepening its direct relationship with Samsung's foundry and packaging operations. The economic logic is straightforward: if Anthropic or OpenAI can design their own accelerator and have it manufactured at Samsung or TSMC, they capture the margin Nvidia currently extracts while also locking in the memory supply that is now the actual constraint. Nvidia's moat is its CUDA software ecosystem, which is real and durable at the model-training layer. But inference — running finished models at scale to serve users — is less CUDA-dependent, and that is where the cost pressure is most acute as these labs move from research to product.

For anyone trying to position in this cycle, the implication is that the Korean memory names are being structurally reclassified by their largest customers even if public equity markets are still pricing them on cyclical earnings multiples. Multi-year, high-volume demand commitments from counterparties with nearly unlimited capital access are not the same animal as spot DRAM contracts. The re-rating, if it comes, will not be gradual.

Financial Markets & Economic News AI Semiconductors Capex

Cheaper AI models don't kill the capex cycle. They just kill the wrong companies.

  • UBS argues that enterprise adoption of lower-cost open-source AI models will increase total GPU and inference demand, not shrink it — the classic Jevons paradox logic applied to compute
  • Enterprises are already routing routine tasks to cheaper alternatives and reserving frontier models like OpenAI's GPT-4 or Anthropic's Claude only for high-complexity work, compressing per-unit revenue at the model layer
  • The shift structurally benefits Nvidia and the major cloud providers — Microsoft, Amazon, Google — while eroding the pricing power of the pure-play AI labs that most late-stage private funds are marked against

The take

UBS put out a note this week arguing that the cheaper-model wave — enterprises routing routine work to open-source and Chinese models while reserving the frontier engines for hard problems — won't derail the AI capex cycle, and might actually help Nvidia, Broadcom, and the hyperscalers by growing total inference demand. This is the Jevons paradox trade dressed in a sell-side suit: make compute cheaper per token, and you use so much more of it that the aggregate bill goes up. It's a comforting story. It's also the exact story you tell clients when you're long the entire complex and need a reason not to trim.

Here's what the note buries. Jevons works beautifully for the people selling raw compute and works terribly for the people who priced their equity on selling reasoning. If the marginal enterprise task moves to a model costing a fifth of Sonnet, the total token count can triple while the revenue captured by OpenAI and Anthropic per unit of useful work collapses. UBS is right that GPU demand holds and wrong to treat that as a bullish read for the AI trade as most people are positioned in it. The trade isn't "AI." The trade is a stack of very different businesses with wildly different pricing power stapled together in the same ETF, and the cheaper-model shift transfers margin down the stack — from the model layer toward the silicon and, eventually, toward whoever owns the power and the cooling.

The second-order effect nobody is pricing: this makes the frontier labs more dependent on the hyperscalers, not less, at exactly the moment their pricing power erodes. Microsoft, Amazon, and Google can run the cheap models themselves, capture the inference margin, and let OpenAI and Anthropic eat the R&D cost of training the next frontier that gets commoditized in nine months. The lab is now a loss-leading feature that drives someone else's cloud consumption. That's not a business, that's a subsidy — and the entity being subsidized is Jensen Huang.

For allocators the read-across is to stop treating AI as a single factor exposure. Own the layer with pricing power that survives commoditization: power generation, grid interconnect, high-bandwidth memory, the handful of network and cooling names with no substitute. Be actively skeptical of any private mark on a pure model-layer company still justified by a 2023 assumption that frontier capability equals durable moat. The demand-stays-strong headline is true. The mistake is inferring that strong aggregate demand protects the specific names most retail and most late-stage private books are overweight. It doesn't. It protects the toll roads, and it strands the racecars.

The other side

The Jevons paradox has a real track record in tech: cloud made per-unit compute cheaper and total spend exploded, mobile made per-message costs negligible and data revenue quintupled. If frontier models keep pulling ahead on capability — and the gap between GPT-4 and a cheap open-source model is still meaningful for complex reasoning, legal analysis, and agentic tasks where errors are costly — enterprises may find themselves pulled back up the stack faster than the commoditization thesis predicts. The model layer may compress, but it may not compress to zero, and a company like Anthropic with strong enterprise contracts and safety credibility in regulated industries still has defensible ground.

The UBS framing is seductive because it resolves a tension that has been hanging over AI positioning all year. If cheaper models threatened the infrastructure buildout, the entire trade unravels. If cheaper models actually grow total demand, you can stay long everything and call the rotation a feature. The note does the work of letting a long-the-complex book avoid a hard decision.

But resolving the tension at the aggregate level hides a disaggregation problem that matters enormously for anyone with concentrated exposure. Consider what actually happens when an enterprise deploys a multi-model strategy — routing 80 percent of queries to a cheap open-source or Chinese model and reserving the frontier engine for the remaining 20 percent. Total token volume may well triple. Nvidia sells more H100s and H200s to run the inference. The hyperscaler captures the margin on hosting. The frontier lab, however, is now monetizing a smaller share of a larger pie, and the smaller share is the hard, expensive-to-serve work while the easy, high-volume, high-margin routine queries go elsewhere. Revenue per unit of useful work delivered by OpenAI or Anthropic drops sharply even if the absolute dollar figure holds temporarily on contract minimums.

This dynamic has a historical parallel in enterprise software. In the early database wars, the emergence of cheaper relational databases did not destroy Oracle immediately — large enterprises kept Oracle for mission-critical workloads while routing lower-stakes applications to cheaper alternatives. But over a decade, the definition of what counted as mission-critical kept shrinking, Oracle's addressable market compressed, and the companies that thrived were the ones owning the underlying infrastructure those databases ran on, not the database vendors themselves. The model layer is on a faster version of that same curve, measured in months rather than years because open-source iteration cycles are now ferociously quick.

The second implication worth sitting with is what cheaper models do to the negotiating leverage of the frontier labs with their hyperscaler distribution partners. Microsoft distributes OpenAI models. Amazon distributes Anthropic. Google has its own models but also partners broadly. When the frontier lab's product was irreplaceable, that distribution relationship was roughly balanced. When a hyperscaler can offer customers four viable model tiers including cheap open-source options, the frontier lab needs the cloud more than the cloud needs the lab. At that point, the lab's R&D spend on the next training run is partly subsidizing the hyperscaler's ability to offer a complete menu to enterprise buyers. The lab bears the cost, the cloud captures the retention.

For anyone allocating capital today, the practical implication is granularity. The aggregate demand signal — compute up, inference up, power up — is real and durable. But that signal does not flow evenly. It concentrates in businesses with no substitutes: the power generators and grid operators feeding data centers, the high-bandwidth memory suppliers like SK Hynix and Micron where there are only a handful of credible producers globally, and the specialized networking and cooling names where demand is structurally constrained by physical build times. Those are the toll roads. The businesses priced on the assumption that building the best model today creates a defensible moat in two years are the ones worth revisiting, particularly in private portfolios where marks have not caught up to the commoditization reality that public markets have at least begun to price.
