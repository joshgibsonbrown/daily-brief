Private Markets Secondaries Endowments Buyout

UC sold the crown jewels at a 10% haircut. That's the honest mark everyone else is hiding.

  • HarbourVest Partners, a $161B Boston-based private markets firm, paid roughly 90 cents on the dollar for $1B of UC's software and tech-focused buyout fund stakes.
  • UC Investments manages $190B in total assets and had $39B in private assets as of June 2025 — this is not a distressed seller dumping junk.
  • The broader secondaries market (where investors buy and sell existing fund stakes) hit $15B in credit alone in 2024, a 5x jump from 2019, with projections of $50B+ by 2030.

The take

The University of California just sold $1 billion of private equity stakes to HarbourVest at a discount of more than 10%, mostly software and tech-focused buyout funds, with Campbell Lutyens running the process. UC isn't a distressed seller. It manages $190 billion, its private book is $39 billion at June 2025 marks, and it doubled its PE allocation from 5% to 10% since 2020. When a seller like that takes a double-digit haircut on exactly the funds everyone claims are the crown jewels of the asset class, that's not a liquidity event. That's a price discovery event, and the price is bad.

Here's what nobody wants to say out loud: the discount is the honest mark. UC's software buyout stakes are carried on manager NAVs that reflect 2021 valuation multiples in a world that has repriced software down 30-40%. HarbourVest just told you what the real clearing price is — carrying value minus 10-plus percent — and it's a floor, not a bargain, because HarbourVest underwrites to make money from there. The interesting number isn't the $1 billion. It's the gap between what UC's June report said these stakes were worth and what a sophisticated buyer would actually pay. That gap is the DPI illusion made visible on a single ticket.

Watch which funds got sold. Tech buyout is supposed to be the highest-conviction sleeve, the stuff you hold through the cycle. UC chose to dump it first because it's the most liquid part of a stale portfolio — there are buyers precisely because the underlying names are marked in a way HarbourVest can arbitrage. You sell your good positions when your bad ones can't be sold at any dignified price. The tech tranche going out the door is a tell about what the rest of the $39 billion looks like.

The second-order effect: every large endowment and pension CIO now has a comp. When UC prints a 10% discount on quality tech funds with Campbell Lutyens on the mandate, that becomes the reference transaction their own boards cite. The polite fiction that secondaries clear "near NAV for the good stuff" just took a public dent from a name too big to dismiss. Expect a wave of copycat processes into year-end as CIOs race to sell before the next comp resets lower.

For allocators, the read is simple. The secondaries buyers — HarbourVest, Ardian, Lexington — are not liquidity providers doing sellers a favor. They are the price-setters, and they are setting prices below the marks that GPs still report to LPs. If you own primary fund interests carried at NAV, the UC trade tells you what your book is actually worth when you need cash. It's not the number in your quarterly letter.

The other side

A 10% discount on a $1B secondary trade doesn't necessarily indict the whole asset class — it may reflect the specific vintage mix, concentration risk, or UC's desire for speed over price optimization in a thin summer market. HarbourVest is also buying a concentrated sleeve of tech buyout, which carries its own idiosyncratic premium for complexity, and sophisticated buyers routinely demand a spread to compensate for that work. If the underlying companies are genuinely compounding and the hold period is long enough, HarbourVest could be the one taking the worse end of a long-dated bet, not UC.

Start with the mechanics of why this matters beyond UC's balance sheet. In private equity, fund stakes are carried at NAV — net asset value — which is a number the general partner, meaning the fund manager, sets based on its own appraisal of the underlying companies. LPs, the outside investors like UC, typically receive that number quarterly and report it to their own boards as the value of their holdings. The problem is that appraisals are backward-looking, methodologically conservative, and updated slowly. When software multiples compressed 30 to 40 percent from their 2021 peaks, most GPs did not mark their portfolios down by anywhere near that amount. The result is a gap — sometimes called stale marks — between what the quarterly letter says and what the market will actually pay.

HarbourVest just made that gap legible on a single, named, billion-dollar transaction. That matters because secondaries pricing has historically been opaque. You could argue that your fund's holdings were special, that the discount was idiosyncratic, that your vintage was different. UC is a credible, well-resourced, sophisticated allocator with a professional investment office, advised by Campbell Lutyens, one of the dedicated placement and advisory firms that runs competitive secondaries processes. If anyone could have extracted a better price, they would have. The 10-plus percent discount is not a fire sale artifact — it is the output of a properly run process.

The historical parallel here is the 2008 to 2009 cycle, when large endowments including Harvard and Princeton were forced to sell private fund stakes at 40 to 60 cent prices to cover liquidity needs. Those trades briefly became the reference comp that repriced the entire secondaries market, dragged LP portfolio valuations lower, and ultimately forced a more honest reckoning with how illiquid assets should be carried. The UC trade is not that severe — ten cents on the dollar is not fifty — but the mechanism is identical. A credible, public transaction by a name too large to dismiss becomes the benchmark everyone else is measured against.

The second-order pressure lands on other institutional holders of similar vintage tech buyout stakes. Every pension CIO who owns 2019 to 2021 software buyout fund interests now has a board-level conversation waiting for them. The comp is on the record. If they want to sell, they know the clearing price. If they want to hold, they have to explain why their book is worth more than what UC just received from one of the most active buyers in the market.

For the GPs running these funds, the discomfort is different but equally real. A visible secondary transaction at a discount to NAV is an implicit challenge to every mark they are still defending. It doesn't force a writedown, but it arms LPs who want one with a data point that is hard to dismiss in a quarterly review meeting.

The secondaries buyers — HarbourVest, Ardian, Lexington Partners — are not neutral middlemen. They are price-setters operating with better information than the sellers, deploying capital they raised specifically to exploit the gap between reported NAV and economic reality. When that gap closes, their returns compress. Right now, the gap is wide enough that they can pay 90 cents and still underwrite attractive returns from there. That tells you everything about where the real marks are.

Private Markets Secondaries PE Vintage Liquidity

Eight hundred billion in dead-end PE deals. Someone is getting rich on the exit.

  • Roughly 40% of US PE-backed companies — over $860 billion in NAV — had been held seven years or longer as of end-2025, the highest share since 2016
  • The 2021 fund vintage is generating cash back to investors (DPI — distributions to paid-in capital) at just 0.14x four years in, about 30% below what comparable funds had returned by the same age
  • Fund stakes eight years or older are currently trading in secondaries at 35% to 45% discounts to their reported asset values, creating forced-sale conditions for liquidity-starved LPs

The take

PitchBook just put a number on something LPs have felt in their bones for two years: at the end of 2025, roughly 40% of US PE-backed companies — north of $860 billion in NAV — had been held seven years or longer, the highest share since 2016 and the third straight annual increase. The industry calls these "zombie funds": solvent, operational, and quietly stuck, holding assets with no timely exit and no clear path to the return they were underwritten for. What makes this iteration different from prior liquidity gripes is the vintage concentration. This isn't a diffuse aging problem. It's a specific, dateable cohort — 2018 to 2022 — that overpaid at the top of the market on cheap floating-rate debt and got repriced by the Fed.

Here's the tell nobody wants to say out loud: the 2021 vintage sits at 0.14x DPI four years in, roughly 30% below what prior vintages had returned in cash by the same age. That's not a marking problem you can smooth over in an LP letter. Kline Hill's Michael Bego calculated as much as 0.6x of return erased from the cost of debt alone on middle-market companies that walked into 2022 with full floating-rate loads. The equity underneath those deals is impaired in a way that time doesn't heal, because the entry multiple assumed a rate regime that no longer exists. These aren't diamonds waiting for a window. Many are structurally dead money.

The consensus read is that this is a tragedy for LPs. The correct read is that it's a wealth transfer, and the allocators who understand the mechanics are on the receiving end. Stakes in funds eight years or older are trading at 35% to 45% discounts to reported NAV — and that reported NAV is itself generous. The seller isn't discounting because they're irrational. They're discounting because they need distributions to fund next year's capital calls, and a zombie doesn't distribute. That's forced selling dressed up as portfolio management. Forced selling is the single best structural setup a buyer can ask for.

The move most allocators are missing is that the real edge isn't in diversified LP-stake pools, where everyone now competes and pricing has tightened. It's in the GP-led single-asset trades that Paul Cohn's Agility and Kline Hill are quietly picking off — good companies with capable sponsors that simply can't reach liquidity, sold at a discount manufactured by the prior investors' eagerness to exit. You're not buying a broken business. You're buying a fine business from a desperate seller. That's the whole game.

The read-across: the zombie problem is being framed as a liability on the LP side of the ledger, and it is. But every impaired 2021 vintage is somebody's 2026 entry point. Concentrated secondary capital with the underwriting to separate the merely stuck from the genuinely dead is looking at the best vintage of its own that the cycle has produced.

The other side

The bull case for patience is real: if rate cuts arrive in force over the next 18 months, floating-rate debt burdens ease, deal multiples recover, and the 2021 cohort's path to exit reopens without the punishing discounts. Secondary buyers underwriting at today's distressed prices could find themselves overpaying relative to what a recovering M&A market will eventually clear. The reported NAVs that look generous today might, in a normalized rate environment, turn out to be roughly correct — in which case buyers piling into secondaries at 40-cent discounts are buying a crowded trade with less margin of safety than it appears.

The zombie fund problem is not new, but its current scale is the product of a very specific, avoidable sequence of decisions. Between 2020 and 2021, private equity dealmaking hit nearly $1.3 trillion across roughly 10,000 transactions, fueled by zero-rate borrowing and a post-pandemic optimism that compressed underwriting discipline. The majority of middle-market buyouts during that window were financed with floating-rate private credit — debt whose interest payments move up and down with benchmark rates rather than being locked in at a fixed cost. When the Federal Reserve raised rates aggressively starting in 2022, those payments didn't stay theoretical. They hit income statements immediately, reducing the cash flow that makes businesses attractive to acquirers and makes sponsors look competent to their own investors.

Michael Bego, managing partner at Kline Hill Partners — a firm that buys secondary stakes in smaller buyout funds, known as the lower middle market — ran the numbers on what rising rates actually did to realized returns. For companies that entered 2022 carrying maximum floating-rate debt loads, the cost of that debt alone wiped out as much as 0.6x of the fund's return multiple. That is not a rounding error. On a fund targeting 2.0x returns, losing 0.6x to debt service before you've sold anything means you are already in recovery mode, not value-creation mode.

The historical comparison sharpens the picture. Funds from the 2010 through 2015 vintages — raised in the years following the financial crisis, when entry prices were lower and leverage was cheaper relative to earnings — were distributing 50% to 60% of their total value back to investors in cash by the time they were seven to eight years old. The 2018 through 2022 cohort, at the same ages, is nowhere close. The denominator problem compounds this: LPs who modeled receiving cash from their older PE positions to fund new capital calls from newer funds are instead sitting on paper gains they cannot monetize, while still being asked to write checks.

This is the mechanical driver behind the secondary market's current sell-side surge. LP-led secondary sales — where an investor in a fund sells their stake to a third party — are accelerating not because LPs have lost faith in PE broadly, but because the distribution machine has stalled and institutional portfolios have cash-flow obligations that zombie funds cannot meet. An endowment or pension that needs liquidity does not have the luxury of waiting four more years for a sponsor to find an exit. They sell at 40 cents on the dollar and move on.

What makes the current setup structurally interesting for buyers is the bifurcation in that discount. Younger fund stakes from 2022 through 2024 vintages — where the underlying companies haven't yet been marked aggressively and upside remains — are trading near or above reported NAV. The steep discounts are concentrated in the older, vintage-specific zombie cohort, where motivated sellers are pricing in the urgency of their own liquidity need rather than the intrinsic value of the asset. Firms like Agility Equity Partners, a Pittsburgh-based buyout firm founded by Paul Cohn that specializes in single-asset GP-led deals, are explicitly targeting this gap: good businesses with capable management and reasonable fundamentals, whose prior owners simply cannot hold any longer. The discount is a function of the seller's calendar, not the company's quality. That is a durable edge as long as the zombie backlog persists, and by PitchBook's count, the backlog is still growing.

ICONIQ Portfolio Companies Anthropic IPO AI

Anthropic's $65 billion run-rate is real. The margin story behind it isn't.

  • Anthropic's annualized revenue run-rate crossed $65 billion, up from roughly $9 billion a year ago and approximately $1 billion two years prior — one of the fastest revenue ramps in enterprise software history.
  • A significant portion of that revenue flows through coding tools and AI agent platforms like Cursor and Claude Code, where Anthropic functions as a wholesale supplier with no direct customer loyalty.
  • The US government recently lifted export restrictions on Anthropic's Fable 5 model, giving the company a narrow regulatory tailwind the IPO is designed to exploit before conditions shift.

The take

Anthropic's annualized revenue run-rate just crossed $65 billion ahead of its IPO, up from roughly $9 billion a year ago and about $1 billion the year before that. That's not a growth curve, that's a vertical line, and it's the number that will anchor the roadshow. ICONIQ holds Anthropic across its Strategic Partners vehicles, so for the reader this is the single most important mark in the portfolio — and the one most likely to be misread.

Here's the tell nobody's pricing correctly: $65 billion in run-rate revenue does not mean $65 billion in gross profit, and with a frontier lab it barely means positive gross margin. Anthropic is still buying compute from Amazon and Google at prices set by counterparties who also happen to be its largest investors and its largest customers. When AWS commits billions to Anthropic and Anthropic spends that money back on AWS Trainium capacity, the revenue is real but the cash is round-tripping. The IPO exists to convert that circular financing into permanent public capital before the compute bill compounds past what private markets will fund. The run-rate isn't the story. The burn behind it is.

The consensus read is that a $65 billion run-rate justifies a valuation north of $350 billion and prints a generational return for early ICONIQ paper. Maybe. But look at what the number is actually made of. A large slice is API revenue routed through coding tools and agent platforms — Cursor, the Claude Code surface, enterprise wrappers — where Anthropic is the arms dealer and the customer has zero switching loyalty. That revenue reprices the instant GLM or DeepSeek or an open-weight model closes the capability gap on the specific workflow. High growth, high concentration, low durability. That is the opposite of the recurring-revenue quality public markets pay 15x forward for.

The strategic move to watch is timing. Amodei is taking this public into a window where the US just lifted export restrictions on Fable 5 and the regulatory posture is maximally friendly — but that window is a political artifact, not a moat. IPO now, while the run-rate line is vertical and Washington is waving the models through, because the second-order risk is a 2027 where compute costs are still climbing, Chinese open models have eaten the commodity tier, and the growth rate decelerates from triple digits to merely excellent. Public markets forgive a lot except deceleration.

For the allocator, the read is unsentimental. If you own Anthropic through ICONIQ, the IPO is your liquidity event, not your entry — treat the lockup as a hard sell signal on at least half the position. This is a company monetizing a lead it may not keep, going public precisely because staying private got too expensive. That's a great trade for the founders. It's a distribution, not a compounder, for you.

The other side

The counterargument is that $65 billion in run-rate revenue, growing at triple-digit rates, is simply too large and too fast to dismiss as structurally fragile. Enterprise contracts are getting longer, Claude's performance on reasoning-heavy tasks is genuinely differentiated from cheaper open-weight alternatives, and Amazon's multi-billion dollar commitment creates infrastructure alignment that a commodity competitor can't easily replicate. If Anthropic locks in even 20% of that run-rate as durable, sticky enterprise spend, the public valuation math still works.

Say more →

The number that will define how this IPO trades isn't $65 billion — it's whatever gross margin Anthropic discloses in its S-1. Frontier AI companies have a structural cost problem that no amount of revenue growth can paper over indefinitely. Training and inference compute are priced by the hyperscalers, and in Anthropic's case, those hyperscalers are Amazon and Google, who together have committed over $10 billion to the company. That relationship looks like partnership on the term sheet and looks like dependency in the income statement. Anthropic is effectively a tenant paying rent to its own landlords, and the lease terms are set by parties with competing model ambitions of their own.

The historical parallel worth reaching for here is Palantir in 2020. Palantir, the data analytics company that went public via direct listing, had explosive government revenue growth, a genuinely differentiated product for a narrow set of high-value customers, and a valuation that assumed the wedge customers would become the mass market. For two years, that thesis looked prescient. Then the growth rate normalized, the commercial segment underdelivered, and the stock gave back most of its post-listing gains before eventually recovering on a completely different narrative. The lesson isn't that Palantir was a bad company — it wasn't — it's that public markets price the rate of change, not the level, and deceleration from extraordinary to merely strong is still punished.

Anthropics situation adds a layer Palantir didn't face: the commodity floor is actively being lowered by open-weight models from Meta, Mistral, and Chinese labs including DeepSeek. Open-weight models are AI models where the underlying parameters are publicly released, meaning any developer can run them without paying an API fee. When those models close the capability gap on specific high-volume tasks — code completion, document summarization, basic reasoning chains — the price Anthropic can charge for API access on those tasks collapses toward zero. The revenue that looks like a vertical line today is partly a function of Anthropic having a temporary capability lead on workflows that will be commoditized within 18 to 36 months.

What the IPO actually represents is a financing strategy more than a valuation event. Staying private through 2026 and 2027 would require Anthropic to keep raising at escalating private valuations to fund a compute bill that compounds with every new model generation. Going public converts that treadmill into a permanent capital base — equity that doesn't mature, doesn't require a new lead investor each round, and lets the company absorb operating losses at scale without negotiating terms. That is a rational choice. It is also an admission that the private financing model hit its ceiling.

For anyone holding Anthropic through ICONIQ Strategic Partners VII — the fund's late-stage growth vehicle that took a position in prior rounds — the IPO lockup period, typically 180 days post-listing, is the decision point that matters. The question isn't whether the company is impressive. It is. The question is whether the public market entry price already reflects the best version of a narrative that has real execution risk behind it. History says that when the best story and the IPO window align perfectly, you are usually closer to the top than the beginning.

ICONIQ Portfolio Companies

Stripe bought a market-data monopoly disguised as a payment deal. The 50x multiple is the tell.

  • Stripe is paying roughly $7 billion for OpenRouter — a valuation more than five times the $1.3 billion post-money it carried just three months ago after closing a $113 million Series B in May 2026.
  • OpenRouter's annualized revenue nearly tripled from roughly $50 million in March 2026 to approximately $140 million by mid-2026, but Stripe is still paying around 50 times that figure — a multiple reserved for structural position, not current cash flow.
  • The deal closed roughly 30 percent below the $10 billion figure the Wall Street Journal first reported in July, implying Stripe's own deal team discounted the asset after accounting for the neutrality tensions baked into the structure.

The take

Stripe just paid more than $7 billion for OpenRouter — the startup that routes developer traffic across 400-plus AI models through a single API endpoint — at roughly 50 times its $140 million annualized revenue and five times the $1.3 billion valuation it carried three months ago. That kind of markup on a two-year-old company isn't paid for cash flow. It's paid for a chokepoint. And the number worth staring at isn't the price — it's the 25 trillion tokens per week flowing through OpenRouter's routing log, which is the single most accurate real-time map of which AI models are actually winning production workloads, months before any lab discloses it in a fundraise.

Here's what nobody is pricing correctly. The consensus take is that this is a neutrality problem — Stripe bills OpenAI and Anthropic, so how can it credibly run an agnostic router? True but small. The real asset Stripe just bought is informational, not transactional. Combine OpenRouter's routing ledger with Metronome's billing telemetry, which already sits inside OpenAI and Anthropic's own invoicing, and Stripe now sees both which models are gaining share and what every provider is actually charging in production. That's not a payments company. That's a market-data monopoly on the fastest-growing spend category in software, and Stripe got it for the price of a mid-cap secondary.

Now the contradiction that makes this a capital allocation story rather than a plumbing story. The majority of tokens routed through OpenRouter go to cheap Chinese-origin models — DeepSeek and the GLM family — because that's the economically rational developer choice. Stripe's commercial incentives point the other way: every query routed to DeepSeek instead of GPT-5 is billing it doesn't process for its two most important AI customers. So Stripe has bought a neutrality asset whose value depends entirely on it never using the lever it just acquired. The moment developers suspect cost-optimization toward Chinese models is being quietly throttled, the 8 million users leave, because switching cost is two config lines. That's the fragility the 50x multiple ignores.

Watch the 30% haircut. The Journal reported roughly $10 billion in July; the deal closed near $7 billion. That $3 billion of friction tells you Stripe's own deal team saw the neutrality-versus-billing tension and repriced the durability of the moat accordingly. They paid for the data, discounted the routing franchise, and are betting the log is worth more than the toll.

The read-across for allocators: the agentic economy's most valuable real estate isn't the models or the compute — it's the metering and routing layer that sees the whole board. Stripe just proved that layer is acquirable, and that the incumbents will pay 50x to own it. If you hold anything in the AI-billing or inference-gateway stack, you were just told what it's worth.

The other side

Fifty times revenue for a two-year-old company with two configuration lines standing between it and churn is not a moat — it's a margin call waiting to happen. OpenRouter's entire developer affection is built on the promise that it doesn't favor anyone, and the moment that promise becomes credibly questionable, forks will proliferate: Cloudflare's AI Gateway, AWS Bedrock routing, and a half-dozen open-source alternatives are already positioned to absorb displaced traffic. The data asset Stripe is betting on also degrades the moment routing behavior changes — if developers migrate away, the 25-trillion-token-per-week signal becomes a lagging indicator rather than a leading one, and Stripe will have paid 50x for a dataset that no longer represents the market.

Say more →

Start with the stack Stripe has quietly assembled, because the OpenRouter deal only makes sense inside that context. In late 2025, Stripe acquired Metronome — a real-time usage tracking and billing platform built specifically for AI companies, whose customers already included OpenAI and Anthropic before Stripe bought it. Metronome answers one question precisely: when an AI product consumes tokens, how does that consumption get measured, metered, and turned into an invoice? What Metronome could not tell Stripe was where those tokens were going before they were billed — which model received the query, why, and whether that routing decision was shifting week over week. OpenRouter fills that gap exactly. The combined picture is genuinely novel. Stripe now sits at two points in the same transaction: it sees the routing decision at the moment it happens through OpenRouter's log, and it sees the resulting invoice through Metronome's billing telemetry. No AI lab, no hyperscaler, and no financial investor has that dual vantage point in real time. Benchmark and a16z see cap tables. Stripe sees production traffic and invoices simultaneously, across every provider, before any of it becomes public information.

The historical parallel worth reaching for here is Bloomberg in the early 1980s. Bloomberg's founding insight was not that financial data was valuable — everyone knew that. It was that the terminal sitting between buyers and sellers, aggregating price discovery in real time, was worth more than any individual trade it facilitated. The terminal became the business. OpenRouter's routing log is structurally similar: the value is not the 5 percent markup on inference spend, which is the toll. The value is the routing ledger itself, which is the map. Stripe just bought the map.

The Chinese-model problem deserves more precision than it usually gets in coverage of this deal. DeepSeek and the GLM family — a series of open-weight models developed by Zhipu AI, a Beijing-based lab — have captured a majority of tokens routed through OpenRouter because they are dramatically cheaper than GPT-5 or Claude 4 for workloads where frontier reasoning is not required. That is not a political preference by developers; it is basic cost optimization. The complication is that models from Chinese-origin labs are subject to China's National Intelligence Law, which creates data-handling obligations for the developers and enterprises routing sensitive queries through them. Stripe has not disclosed how it intends to handle compliance obligations that vary by query destination — a gap that matters more for regulated buyers in financial services and healthcare than for indie developers, but that matters nonetheless as OpenRouter moves upmarket.

The 30 percent price haircut between the Journal's July report and the confirmed close is the most underanalyzed number in this story. Deal teams do not give back $3 billion without a reason. The most plausible read is that Stripe's own diligence confirmed the neutrality tension was real and repriced the durability of the routing franchise accordingly. They paid for the data asset at full freight and discounted the toll-gate business for the risk that developer trust erodes. That implicit decomposition — full price for the log, haircut for the moat — is actually a coherent capital allocation thesis. It only fails if the log and the moat turn out to be inseparable, meaning you cannot preserve the data asset without also preserving the trust that keeps 8 million developers routing through the platform in the first place.

Family Office & Wealth Planning Family Offices Wealth Trends Asia

The family office market isn't growing. It's going dark.

  • 70.8% of new family office formations in Q2 chose the single-family structure — the most opaque wrapper in private capital, with no public filings or regulatory disclosure requirements — up from 63% just one quarter earlier.
  • 67.9% of newly discovered multi-family offices (firms that manage wealth for several families under one roof) fell entirely outside the U.S. SEC registration framework, meaning no Form ADV, no mandated disclosure of assets or strategy.
  • Asia and Oceania accounted for nearly 20% of Q2 new formations despite representing only 10.2% of the total FINTRX database, while Latin America posted zero new additions — a sharp geographic concentration signal.

The take

FINTRX, a data platform that tracks family offices, just published its Q2 2026 numbers, and buried in the marketing-friendly summary is a genuine structural signal: 67.9% of newly discovered multi-family offices fall entirely outside the U.S. RIA framework, single family offices jumped to 70.8% of new additions from 63% a quarter earlier, and 92.7% of the new cohort wants direct investments while hedge funds and private credit saw the widest divergence the platform has ever recorded. The industry reads this as "the family office space is growing." That's the wrong read. What's actually happening is the market is going dark.

The consensus story is that new money — entrepreneurial wealth, now 68.6% of additions and led by tech and private investors — is professionalizing into structured vehicles. The data says the opposite. A single family office is the most opaque structure in private capital by design: no Form ADV, no public AUM, no LP letter, no regulatory surface. When 70.8% of new formations choose that wrapper and two-thirds of the multi-family cohort deliberately sits outside the RIA regime, they aren't professionalizing toward transparency — they're building capital vehicles specifically engineered to disappear from the sightlines that fund marketers, regulators, and journalists rely on. The tell is the registration split. You don't structure to avoid the RIA framework by accident. You do it to keep allocation decisions private and reporting obligations near zero.

For an allocator, the second-order effect is the one nobody's pricing. This is a demand-side rejection of the commingled fund model, not a fundraising softness cycle. When 92.7% of new families want to invest directly and they're actively pulling back from hedge funds and private credit — the two most fee-heavy, most intermediated structures — they're telling you the "2 and 20 plus a lockup" bargain no longer clears for the marginal new-money family. They watched 2021 vintage marks, they run their own deal teams staffed by ex-Big Four talent (PwC alone placed 55 of the new contacts), and they'd rather co-invest and control than pay carry to a GP whose DPI story is a paper mark. The GP who thinks the family office channel is a growth market for their flagship fund is selling into a room that has decided to buy the underlying asset instead.

Then the geography. Asia/Oceania at nearly 20% of new formations against a 10.2% database weight, and Latin America at literally zero, is a capital migration map, not a coverage quirk. New Asian wealth is forming offshore, opaque, and direct — much of it routing through Singapore and Hong Kong precisely because those regimes reward the single-family, non-registered structure. If your LP pipeline still assumes the next dollar comes from an SEC-registered multi-family office in the U.S. Northeast, you are prospecting the shrinking half of the market.

The other side

The counterargument is that opacity is the baseline condition of the family office world, not a new development, and what FINTRX is actually capturing is its own database coverage expanding into corners of the market it previously missed. Single-family offices have always preferred non-registration; a spike in their share of new additions may reflect better data collection rather than a behavioral shift. It's also worth noting that 92.7% appetite for direct investments doesn't necessarily mean rejection of funds — many families run direct deal programs alongside traditional LP commitments, and a preference flagged on a data platform profile is not the same as a mandate being withdrawn.

The mechanism worth understanding here is structural, not cyclical. When a newly wealthy founder — the kind of person driving that 68.6% entrepreneurial share of new single-family offices — decides to formalize their capital vehicle, they face a real choice: register as an RIA and accept disclosure obligations, or structure as a family office and retain near-total privacy. The SEC's family office exemption, codified under the Dodd-Frank Act, is explicit: a single-family office managing only family assets is excluded from the definition of investment adviser entirely. No registration. No public filing. No reporting surface of any kind. The fact that new formations are choosing this wrapper at an accelerating rate tells you something about intent, not just preference.

The talent pipeline reinforces the thesis. PwC placed 55 of the new contacts entering family offices in Q2, with the Big Four accounting for 143 combined. These are not generalist wealth management hires. Former Big Four professionals bring transaction structuring, tax optimization, and operational audit skills — exactly the toolkit you need to run an internal deal team capable of evaluating and closing direct investments without relying on a GP's memo. When a family office recruits a former PwC transaction services director, they are building the internal capacity to kill the intermediary, not to work more closely with one.

The hedge fund and private credit divergence is the sharpest confirmation of this. Hedge funds and private credit — meaning direct lending and structured debt strategies — are both high-fee, high-opacity structures from the investor's perspective. The family office can't see the underlying book, can't control the timing of distributions, and pays carried interest on returns they had no hand in generating. The new cohort, apparently, has done the math. Watching 2021-vintage private credit marks hold at par while underlying borrower stress climbed was a tutorial that cost real capital. Direct investment at 92.7% penetration among new formations is the response: own the asset, control the basis, eliminate the fee drag.

The geographic read matters too and it connects to the same logic. Singapore and Hong Kong both offer regulatory environments that are accommodating to single-family structures with minimal filing requirements, favorable treatment of investment holding companies, and strong treaty networks for cross-border capital movement. Asian wealth forming through those jurisdictions isn't doing so out of ignorance of the alternatives — it is selecting the architecture deliberately. The historical parallel is the rise of the Delaware LLC as the default wrapper for U.S. venture and buyout funds in the 1980s: not because founders didn't understand other options, but because the structure was purpose-built to minimize friction and maximize flexibility.

The downstream effect for fund managers is straightforward and uncomfortable. The family office channel has been one of the most reliable sources of LP capital for emerging and mid-market managers for the last decade — lower minimum check sizes, faster decisions, fewer legal committees. If the incoming cohort is structuring specifically to avoid commingled funds and is building internal teams capable of executing direct deals, that historical dynamic does not simply compress. It reverses. The new family office isn't the next LP. It's the next competitor for the same assets.
